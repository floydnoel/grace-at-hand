## Summary

*In Suprema Universalis* (August 22, 1741) is a papal brief in which Pope Benedict XIV addresses all Catholic bishops on the discipline of Lenten fasting. Disturbed by growing laxity, he reaffirms that fasting is a non-negotiable element of Christian discipline from the Church's earliest days, while also clarifying the rules for dispensations: anyone excused from the fast—whether by general dispensation or individual permission—must still observe the single-meal rule and may not add supplementary collations, unless there is a genuine risk of serious illness. The document rebukes those who rationalize away this obligation and exhorts bishops to instruct the faithful on the spiritual purpose of fasting as medicine for soul and body.

## Historical Context

Benedict XIV (Prospero Lorenzo Lambertini, r. 1740–1758) has been widely regarded as one of the most learned and judicious popes of the early modern period. The mid-18th century Church was navigating the pressures of the Enlightenment, the lingering aftershocks of Jansenist rigorism, and widespread attrition in popular religious observance. Relaxation of fasting discipline had become a particular concern: dispensations were multiplying, and many Catholics—including clergy—were treating the obligation loosely. This brief follows an earlier letter of May 30, 1741 (now lost), in which Benedict first urged bishops to address the decline. *In Suprema Universalis* is the definitive canonical clarification, issued early in his pontificate as part of a broader campaign to restore ecclesiastical discipline through clear, moderate, and pastorally sensitive legislation.

## Key Themes

- **The antiquity and centrality of fasting** — Benedict insists that Lenten fasting is an apostolic institution, binding on all Catholics and fundamental to Church discipline.
- **Dispensation does not abolish the fast** — Those excused from fasting (whether en masse or individually) are still bound by the substance of the fast: one meal, no extra collations. Dispensation relaxes the law; it does not license laxity.
- **Rebuke of rationalization** — The pope condemns as "wholly human logic" the argument that the single-meal rule applies only to group dispensations and not to individual ones. He declares this distinction foreign to his thinking and nullifies it.
- **Pastoral balance of firmness and mercy** — Benedict acknowledges the need for dispensations in cases of grave necessity or medical advice, showing a willingness to "become weak with the weak," but he refuses to let pastoral flexibility become a cover for indiscipline.
- **Fasting as spiritual medicine** — The brief frames fasting not as mere dietary restriction but as a remedy for sin, a participation in the cross of Christ, and a source of eternal glory.
- **Episcopal responsibility** — Bishops are charged with teaching, warning, and encouraging the faithful on this point of discipline.

## Significance

*In Suprema Universalis* is a classic example of Benedict XIV's governing style: doctrinally firm, canonically precise, and pastorally moderate. It closed a loophole that had allowed the faithful to receive dispensations while effectively abandoning the fast, thereby protecting the integrity of a penitential practice the pope considered essential. The brief was later incorporated into the broader corpus of modern Catholic teaching on fasting and abstinence, and it remains a reference point for the principle that ecclesiastical dispensations are acts of economy, not abolition—a theme that resonates in later magisterial documents on liturgy and penance.
