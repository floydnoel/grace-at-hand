## Summary

*Pro Eximia Tua* (1741) is a brief papal letter from Benedict XIV to the Archbishop of Turin, condemning the simoniacal practice by certain clergymen and laypeople in the Piedmont region who were treating the celebration of Masses as a mercenary trade driven by greed. The pope orders the archbishop to eradicate this abuse, warns that obstinate offenders should face ecclesiastical penalties, and authorizes calling upon the secular authority of King Charles Emmanuel of Sardinia to assist in enforcement.

## Historical Context

Benedict XIV wrote this document on June 30, 1741, just a year into his pontificate (1740–1758). Simony — the buying and selling of spiritual goods — had long been a recurring problem in the Church, and the abuse of Mass stipends (offerings given to a priest to celebrate Mass for a particular intention) was a particularly persistent form of it. The letter singles out the Piedmont region of northwestern Italy, then part of the Kingdom of Sardinia, and praises King Charles Emmanuel III as a reliably supportive Catholic monarch who would assist in imposing discipline.

## Key Themes

- **Simony in liturgical practice** — The core concern is the reduction of the Eucharist to a commercial transaction, with clergy treating Mass intentions as a source of "sordid gain."
- **Papal authority and local enforcement** — Benedict XIV writes to the Archbishop of Turin personally, making clear that the bishop is the primary agent of reform but also that the pope will back him with the full weight of the Apostolic See.
- **Cooperation with civil power** — The letter explicitly sanctions invoking the secular arm (King Charles Emmanuel) if ecclesiastical penalties prove insufficient, reflecting the early-modern model of church–state collaboration.
- **Pastoral concern for reputation** — The pope is careful not to impugn the general state of discipline in Piedmont, framing his intervention as a targeted remedy, not a blanket condemnation.

## Significance

*Pro Eximia Tua* is a minor but revealing document in Benedict XIV's wider reform program. Known as a scholarly, moderate, and administrative pope, Benedict XIV consistently worked to curb clerical abuse without provoking unnecessary conflict. This letter illustrates that approach: firm in condemning simony, respectful toward the local hierarchy, and pragmatic in enlisting civil authority. It also foreshadows later, more comprehensive legislation against simoniacal practices in the celebration of Mass, a concern that would continue to occupy the Church's attention through the Council of Trent's aftermath and into the modern period.
