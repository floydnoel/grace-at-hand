## Summary

This papal bull, issued by Pope John XXII on December 2, 1333 in Avignon, grants the *scholasticus* (maestrescuela, or cathedral schoolmaster) of the Church of Salamanca the permanent authority to confer academic degrees — including the *licentia docendi* (license to teach) and the insignia of master and doctor — to qualified candidates at the University of Salamanca. It also lifts the previous restriction (imposed by Alexander IV in 1255) that had excluded the Universities of Paris and Bologna from recognizing Salamanca degrees, thereby granting full *ius ubique docendi* (the right to teach anywhere) to Salamanca graduates.

## Historical Context

By the early 14th century, the University of Salamanca — founded around 1218 by Alfonso IX of León and confirmed by Alfonso X of Castile in 1254 — had grown into one of Europe's leading *studia generalia*. Pope Alexander IV had recognized it in 1255 and granted its graduates the right to teach at any university *except* Paris and Bologna. In the decades that followed, jurisdictional disputes arose over who held the authority to confer degrees: the cathedral's maestrescuela had exercised this role from time immemorial, but the lack of explicit papal sanction invited challenges. King Alfonso XI of Castile, together with the university rectors and the city council, petitioned John XXII to settle the matter. The pope, then residing in Avignon during the period of the Avignon Papacy (1309–1377), issued this bull to strengthen the university's institutional framework and put its degree-granting authority beyond dispute.

## Key Themes

- **Papal confirmation of academic authority**: The bull formally delegates the power to confer degrees to the maestrescuela, acting on behalf of the Holy See, thereby placing the university's academic governance under ecclesiastical jurisdiction.
- **The *ius ubique docendi* (right to teach anywhere)**: By removing the Paris and Bologna exception, John XXII gave Salamanca graduates the same universal teaching license enjoyed by the older universities, a mark of full institutional recognition.
- **Church and university integration**: The document reaffirms that the university had been "subject from ancient times" to the scholastic jurisdiction of the cathedral, embedding the *studium* within the Church's hierarchical structure.
- **Royal and papal cooperation**: The bull shows the interplay between secular and ecclesiastical authority — the king petitions, the pope grants — in the founding and governance of medieval universities.
- **The pursuit of knowledge as a public good**: The preamble praises learning as essential for human life, echoing the classic medieval understanding that wisdom and science benefit the commonwealth (*respublica*).

## Significance

The bull *Summa providit altitudo consilii* is a foundational document for the University of Salamanca. By definitively assigning degree-conferral authority to the maestrescuela (a role analogous to a university chancellor), it established the constitutional model that would govern the university for centuries — degrees continued to be granted in the name of pope and king until 1830. The lifting of the Paris-Bologna restriction signaled Salamanca's elevation to the first rank of European universities. The document also exemplifies the medieval pattern of papal involvement in higher education, a tradition that shaped the Catholic Church's relationship with universities well into the modern era. Today, it remains a key source for understanding the history of academic governance, the *ius ubique docendi*, and the Church's role in the development of the Western university.
