## Summary

*Grande Munus* (1880) is Pope Leo XIII's encyclical extending the feast of Saints Cyril and Methodius to the universal Church. It recounts the missionary labors of the two Greek brothers who evangelized the Slavic peoples, invented the Slavic alphabet, translated Scripture into the vernacular, and sought (and received) papal approval for the use of Old Church Slavonic in the liturgy.

## Historical Context

Written in the third year of Leo XIII's pontificate (1880), the document responds to petitions from bishops at the First Vatican Council (1869–70) to extend the cult of Cyril and Methodius Church-wide. Leo also had pastoral concern for Slavic Catholics amid shifting political landscapes in Central and Eastern Europe. The encyclical coincides with the millennium of Methodius's mission and reflects Leo's wider interest in fostering Catholic unity in the East.

## Key Themes

- **Papal primacy and missionary authority** — Cyril and Methodius received their commission, episcopal consecration, and liturgical approval from Rome, not Constantinople, underscoring that unity with the Apostolic See is constitutive of Catholic mission.
- **Legitimacy of vernacular liturgy** — The encyclical defends the use of the Slavic language in worship by citing Scripture, the permission of Pope Adrian II, and the confirmation of Pope John VIII, later reaffirmed by Benedict XIV.
- **Cyril and Methodius as bridge-builders** — The brothers are presented as a link between Eastern and Western Christianity, having gone forth from Constantinople but completed and justified their mission in Rome.
- **Civilizing mission of the Church** — The Gospel is credited not only with salvation but with *humanitas et cultus* — leading the Slavic peoples from barbarism to humane, civilized life.
- **Marian and papal continuity** — The encyclical catalogues the solicitude of popes from Nicholas I to Clement XIV for various Slavic peoples, portraying the papacy as a continuous protector of those evangelized by Cyril and Methodius.

## Significance

*Grande Munus* formally established the feast of Saints Cyril and Methodius (July 5) in the universal Roman calendar, raising their veneration beyond local observance. It stands as a landmark document on the legitimacy of vernacular liturgy a century before the Second Vatican Council, and it articulated a vision of Slavic Catholic identity that remained influential throughout Leo XIII's pontificate and into the twentieth century. John Paul II later made Cyril and Methodius co-patrons of Europe (1980), explicitly building on Leo's foundation.
