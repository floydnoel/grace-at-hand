# Guiding Principles Of The Lay Apostolate · English editorial summary

## Summary

An address by Pope Pius XII to the Second World Congress for the Lay Apostolate (1957), which distinguishes the lay apostolate from the hierarchical apostolate, affirms that laypeople are not passive members of the Church, insists that the "consecration of the world" is essentially the work of the laity, and surveys concrete fields for apostolic action — the parish, the press, radio and film, the working world, and the mission territories of Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

## Historical Context

Written in 1957 at the height of the Cold War, the address reflects a world shaped by atheistic communism's spread, rapid decolonisation across Asia and Africa, explosive population growth and industrialisation in Latin America, and the rise of mass media. The Church faced a severe shortage of priests in many regions, while Marxist movements were making inroads among workers and intellectuals. The first World Congress for the Lay Apostolate had been held in 1951, and the intervening years had seen a surge of national and regional congresses. Pius XII here consolidates that momentum, responding both to theological questions about the nature of lay cooperation and to urgent practical needs on multiple continents.

## Key Themes

- **Distinction between hierarchical and lay apostolate**: The power to consecrate, teach, and govern belongs to pope and bishops; laypeople who receive a mandate cooperate with the hierarchy but remain lay. Even a lay catechist teaching with a canonical mandate exercises a *lay* apostolate, not a hierarchical one.
- **No passive laity**: All members of the Church are called to be active in building up the Mystical Body. The term "emancipation of the layman" is misused when it distorts the proper relationship between the teaching Church and the learning Church.
- **Consecration of the world is the layperson's task**: Priests cannot do this work. Only laypeople immersed in economic, social, and political life can penetrate the temporal order with the Christian spirit.
- **Principle of subsidiarity**: Ecclesiastical authorities should entrust laypeople with tasks they can perform as well as or better than priests, allowing them freedom and personal responsibility.
- **Catholic Action is not a monopoly**: The organised lay apostolate takes many forms; Catholic Action should not claim exclusive legitimacy. Pius XII floats a possible terminological and organisational reform to federate all recognised lay apostolate movements.
- **Formation is essential**: Lay apostles need intellectual, spiritual, and social formation — competence in theology, economics, politics, and social teaching, as well as tact and apostolic spirit. This formation should begin in childhood, fostered by parents.
- **Fields of apostolate**: The document gives detailed attention to the parish, the press, publishing, radio, cinema, television, the industrial working world, the European Coal and Steel Community, Latin America (where a priest shortage is acute), and the Asian and African missions. In every case it calls for competent, organised, and courageous lay action.
- **Cooperation with non-Catholics**: Laypeople should work with neutral and non-Catholic organisations when doing so serves the common good, and should play an active role in international organisations.
- **Two objectives: preserve and win over**: The Church will not yield ground to atheistic communism, but will fight with the weapons of Christ.

## Significance

This address is one of the most comprehensive magisterial treatments of the lay apostolate before the Second Vatican Council. It anticipates and lays groundwork for the Council's Decree on the Apostolate of the Laity (*Apostolicam Actuositatem*, 1965) and chapters of *Lumen Gentium* on the laity. Its affirmation that the consecration of the world belongs essentially to laypeople — not to the clergy — gave theological dignity and direction to lay Catholic movements worldwide. Its nuanced treatment of Catholic Action, its insistence on subsidiarity within the Church, and its global vision spanning Latin America, Asia, and Africa mark it as a foundational document for modern Catholic ecclesiology and lay spirituality.
