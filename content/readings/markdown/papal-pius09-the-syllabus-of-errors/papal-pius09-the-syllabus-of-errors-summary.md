## Summary

The Syllabus of Errors (1864) is a papal document by Pope Pius IX that catalogues and condemns 80 propositions drawn from modernity — including rationalism, liberalism, socialism, secular education, separation of church and state, and challenges to papal authority. It was published as an appendix to the encyclical *Quanta Cura*.

## Historical Context

Issued on December 8, 1864, the Syllabus responded to sweeping political and intellectual changes across 19th-century Europe: the rise of liberal nationalism, the unification of Italy (*Risorgimento*) which had stripped the Papal States of territory, the spread of Enlightenment rationalism, and growing demands for church–state separation. Pius IX, having begun his pontificate as a relative liberal, hardened his stance after the 1848 revolutions and the loss of papal temporal power.

## Key Themes

- **Rejection of rationalism and naturalism** — truths of faith cannot be subordinate to human reason alone
- **Defence of Church authority** — the Church is a perfect, independent society with rights conferred by Christ, not the state
- **Condemnation of religious indifferentism** — no man may freely choose any religion; salvation is found only in the Catholic Church
- **Separation of church and state** — the idea that the Church ought to be separated from the State is listed as an error (error 55)
- **Sovereignty over education** — civil government must not control schools, seminaries, or curricula to the exclusion of ecclesiastical authority
- **Temporal power of the Pope** — abolition of the Papal States would not benefit the Church (error 76)
- **Rejection of modern liberalism** — the Pope cannot reconcile with "progress, liberalism, and modern civilization" (error 80)

## Significance

The Syllabus was one of the most controversial papal documents of the 19th century. It was widely denounced across Europe as a declaration of war on modernity. In Catholic theology, it remains a definitive statement of the Church's opposition to theological liberalism, though subsequent ecumenical councils (especially Vatican II, 1962–65) would later adopt a more conciliatory stance toward religious liberty and church–state relations. The document is still frequently cited in traditionalist Catholic circles as a benchmark of orthodox teaching.
