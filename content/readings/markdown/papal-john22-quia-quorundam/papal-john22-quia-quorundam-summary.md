## Summary

*Quia quorundam* (1324) is a papal bull in which Pope John XXII defends his earlier constitutions *Ad conditorem canonum* (1322) and *Quum inter non nullos* (1323) against critics — mainly Franciscan Spirituals and their allies — who argued that those constitutions contradicted definitive teachings of previous popes. John XXII rejects the claim that earlier pontiffs (Honorius III, Gregory IX, Innocent IV, Alexander IV, Nicholas III) had defined as a matter of faith that Christ and the Apostles possessed only the *simplex usus facti* (mere factual use) of goods without any legal right (*ius*). He argues that Christ and the Apostles did indeed have a right of use (*usus iuris*), and that to claim otherwise would make Christ's actions unjust — a blasphemous conclusion. He declares that anyone publicly teaching or defending the contrary position has fallen into heresy.

## Historical Context

The bull sits at the climax of the Franciscan Poverty Controversy, which divided the Franciscan order and embroiled the Avignon papacy. Earlier popes had confirmed the Franciscan Rule and (in Nicholas III's *Exiit qui seminat*, 1279) endorsed a formulation of apostolic poverty involving mere *usus facti*. But John XXII, facing a radical wing (the Spirituals) that used this teaching to challenge ecclesiastical property and papal authority, reversed course. *Ad conditorem canonum* (1322) had already declared that the Franciscans could not have even *simplex usus facti* without some accompanying right, and *Quum inter non nullos* (1323) had declared it heretical to assert that Christ and the Apostles owned nothing either individually or in common. *Quia quorundam* is a lengthier theological and historical defence of those rulings, answering critics who claimed that earlier papal definitions were irrevocable.

## Key Themes

- **Limits of papal irrevocability**: John XXII rejects the notion that a Pope cannot revisit or clarify the definitions of his predecessors on matters of faith and morals, especially when those definitions concerned discipline and religious rules rather than articles of faith.
- **Christ's right of use**: The central theological claim is that Christ and the Apostles, in using temporal goods, possessed a legitimate *ius* (right), not merely a factual use without right; otherwise their actions would have been unjust.
- **Nature of the "keys"**: John XXII argues that the spiritual "key of knowledge" is not rightly separated from the "key of power"; both are powers of binding and loosing, and knowledge as such is not a key.
- **Franciscan rule and earlier confirmations**: He examines the wording of earlier papal confirmations and declarations and argues that none of them actually defined apostolic poverty as consisting *only* in mere factual use; they simply confirmed the rule without making that specific doctrinal determination.
- **Heresy and authority**: Those who publicly defend the condemned position are declared heretics and are to be shunned; the bull reasserts papal authority to define doctrine and punish dissent.

## Significance

*Quia quorundam* completed John XXII's dismantling of the legal and theological framework that had underpinned the Franciscan doctrine of apostolic poverty since Nicholas III. It provoked lasting opposition: the Minister General of the Franciscans, Michael of Cesena, and the philosopher William of Ockham fled to the court of the Holy Roman Emperor Louis the Bavarian, where they wrote tracts denying John XXII's authority and eventually arguing for a conciliarist or imperial check on papal power. The controversy contributed to the development of late medieval political thought, especially theories of property, authority, and the limits of papal jurisdiction. Though John XXII's position was maintained by subsequent popes, the episode left a deep mark on the Church's teaching about evangelical poverty and the relationship between spiritual perfection and property rights.
