## Summary

*Paterno Iam Diu* (Latin: "For a Long Time, Paternally") is a papal encyclical issued by Pope Benedict XV on November 24, 1919, calling on the Catholic Church worldwide to organize emergency aid for the starving children of Central Europe in the aftermath of the First World War. The Pope directs bishops to hold public prayers and gather alms, food, medicine, and clothing on the feast of the Holy Innocents (December 28), and he personally contributes 100,000 lire as an example.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was written just over a year after the armistice that ended World War I (November 1918). Central Europe — including parts of the former Austro-Hungarian Empire, Germany, Poland, and neighboring regions — lay devastated by four years of war, economic collapse, and a blockade that continued after the fighting stopped. Famine and disease were widespread, and children were the most vulnerable. Benedict XV, who had spent his entire pontificate (1914–1922) trying to halt the war and alleviate its suffering, here turns from peacemaking to direct humanitarian action. The reference to earlier aid for Belgian children reflects the continuity of his relief efforts.

## Key Themes

- **Papal universal fatherhood** — Benedict grounds the appeal in the duty of the Pope as *pater universalis* ("universal father") to care for all in need, especially the innocent.
- **The innocence of children** — Children are described as "altogether innocent" of the war, making their suffering especially tragic, and they represent "the germs of future generations."
- **Christian charity as a public witness** — The encyclical frames humanitarian relief as a concrete expression of the Gospel, recalling Christ's own infancy in the poverty of Bethlehem.
- **Catholic social solidarity** — Benedict urges bishops worldwide, not just those in affected regions, to mobilize their flocks; the response is intended to be a "noble competition of charity."
- **Ecumenical and humanitarian appeal** — Though addressed principally to Catholics, the encyclical explicitly invites "all who have the sentiments of humanity" to participate.

## Significance

*Paterno Iam Diu* is one of the earliest papal documents to make large-scale international humanitarian relief a central concern of the Church's public mission. Alongside Benedict XV's earlier relief efforts for Belgian children (1915–1916) and his 1920 encyclical *Pacem, Dei Munus Pulcherrimum* on postwar reconciliation, it marks a shift toward the modern papal role as a moral voice in global humanitarian crises. The encyclical also foreshadows later Catholic social teaching on the preferential option for the poor and the protection of children. Though less known than Benedict XV's peace notes, it exemplifies his conviction that the Church must not only condemn war but actively repair its human wreckage.
