## Summary

*Decet Quam Maxime* (1769) is a papal apostolic letter from Pope Clement XIV to the bishops of Sardinia, condemning financial abuses in ecclesiastical curias — demanding payment for ordinations, benefices, sacraments, dispensations, visitations, burials, and the distribution of holy oils — and prescribing detailed regulations to eliminate simony and restore free administration of spiritual goods.

## Historical Context

The document was issued in the wake of the Council of Trent (1545–1563), which had already prohibited payment for ordinations and benefices. Despite repeated conciliar decrees and a 1678 ruling by Innocent XI, abuses persisted, especially in Sardinian dioceses. Clement XIV (r. 1769–1774) was acting at the request of Charles Emmanuel III, King of Sardinia, who sought papal authority to overcome local resistance rooted in "ancient and inveterate custom" and the claim that fees were necessary for the support of curial staff.

## Key Themes

- **Simony and avarice** — the core vice addressed; the document repeatedly links financial exaction in spiritual matters to simony and condemns even the *suspicion* of avarice among clergy.
- **Gratuitous administration of sacraments and orders** — ordination, confirmation, confession faculties, marriage, and burial must be freely given; only small, regulated fees for notaries and chancellors without salary are permitted, and nothing may go to the bishop or his household.
- **Conciliar and papal authority** — the letter grounds its rulings in the Council of Trent, earlier popes (Innocent XI, Gregory XIII, Benedict XIV), and Sacred Congregation decisions, asserting that no contrary custom, however ancient, can justify abuse.
- **Distinction between offering and exaction** — spontaneous pious offerings (e.g., candles at funerals) are lawful, but demanding them under any pretext is forbidden; the line between voluntary gift and coerced payment is strictly policed.
- **Episcopal vigilance** — bishops are the primary enforcers; they are praised for their piety but warned that they must purge abuses in their curias, and the pope supplies his authority to strengthen their hand.

## Significance

*Decet Quam Maxime* is a landmark in the long Catholic struggle against simony in pastoral administration. It synthesizes Tridentine reform discipline into a practical code for a specific kingdom, anticipating later universal codifications of canon law. Its insistence that "no custom, however ancient" can justify corrupt practice, and its meticulous regulation of even small notarial fees, made it a reference point for subsequent curial reform and for the principle that spiritual goods must never be bought or sold.
