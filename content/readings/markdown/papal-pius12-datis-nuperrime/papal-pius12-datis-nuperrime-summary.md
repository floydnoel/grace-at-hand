## Summary

*Datis Nuperrime* is a brief apostolic letter issued by Pope Pius XII on November 5, 1956, lamenting the Soviet Union's violent suppression of the Hungarian Revolution. The Pope condemns the bloodshed, calls on the perpetrators to recognize that a people's right to freedom cannot be crushed by force, and invokes God's justice against those who persecute the innocent. He exhorts all peoples to pursue peace grounded in justice, liberty, and love.

## Historical Context

The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 began as a peaceful student protest on October 23 and quickly escalated into a nationwide uprising against the Soviet-imposed communist government. By early November, Soviet tanks and troops had poured into Budapest to crush the rebellion, killing thousands of civilians. On November 4 — the day before this letter — the Soviet military launched a massive assault code-named "Operation Whirlwind" to retake the capital. Pius XII, who had already written an encyclical (*Laetamur Admodum*) on November 1 expressing hope for Hungary, followed up with *Datis Nuperrime* once news of the brutal crackdown reached Rome. The document is one of several Cold War-era papal statements condemning communist oppression of religious and civil liberties.

## Key Themes

- **Condemnation of unjust violence:** The Pope declares that any violence and bloodshed unjustly caused is never to be tolerated.
- **Divine justice:** Citing the story of Cain and Abel, Pius warns that the blood of the Hungarian people cries out to God, who occasionally punishes unjust rulers in this life.
- **Rightful freedom:** The letter affirms that a people's legitimate desire for freedom cannot be extinguished by military force.
- **Pastoral solidarity:** The Pope identifies with the suffering Hungarian people, calls all Christians to prayer for the dead, and imparts his blessing especially to Hungary.
- **Christian human dignity:** The response to oppression is not retaliation but peace grounded in justice, liberty, and love.

## Significance

*Datis Nuperrime* stands as one of Pius XII's most direct Cold War condemnations of Soviet aggression. Though it did not alter the geopolitical outcome of 1956, it gave moral and spiritual voice to a crushed nation and reinforced the Church's post-war teaching on human rights, religious freedom, and the limits of state power. Alongside his contemporaneous encyclical *Laetamur Admodum* and his Christmas radio message that year, it forms part of the papal witness to the persecuted Church behind the Iron Curtain and anticipates later papal teaching on solidarity with oppressed peoples.
