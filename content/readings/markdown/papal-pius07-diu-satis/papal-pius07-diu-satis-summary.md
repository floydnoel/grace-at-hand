## Summary

*Diu Satis* ("Long Enough") is Pope Pius VII's first encyclical, issued May 15, 1800, calling the Church and the world to return to Gospel principles after the devastation of the French Revolution. It is an exhortation to bishops to guard Catholic doctrine, resist the "false philosophy" of the Enlightenment, protect the young from corrupting influences, and uphold the Church's laws, liberty, and property against revolutionary assaults.

## Historical Context

Written just two months after Pius VII's election in Venice (the conclave having been held there because Rome was occupied), the encyclical responds to the crisis unleashed by the French Revolution: the Church had been despoiled of its property, religious orders suppressed, Pius VI driven into exile and died a prisoner, and the papacy itself seemed near collapse. Napoleon was rising to power, anti-clericalism raged across France and Italy, and the "false philosophy" of the Enlightenment was blamed for upending the social and religious order.

## Key Themes

- **Papal primacy and the indestructibility of the Church** — the See of Peter remains the rock on which the Church is founded, and attacks on the papacy are attacks on Christianity itself.
- **Return to the Gospel as the only cure for society** — quoting Augustine, Pius argues that if rulers and citizens actually lived by Christ's teaching, every social ill would be healed.
- **Guarding the deposit of faith** — bishops must rigorously vet clergy, prevent false teachers from preaching, and burn books hostile to Christian doctrine.
- **Protection of youth** — the Revolution's success depended on corrupting the young; bishops must control seminaries, colleges, and the formation of children.
- **Church liberty and property** — kings must not interfere with Church law or seize Church goods; those who despoil the Church imperil their own souls.
- **Unity of bishops with the Pope** — a call for collective vigilance and resistance against a worldwide conspiracy to destroy Christian teaching.

## Significance

*Diu Satis* marks the first papal response after the trauma of the Revolution and sets the combative, counter-revolutionary tone that would define the 19th-century papacy. It reasserts papal authority at a moment when it seemed all but extinguished, anchors the Church's resistance in a return to Gospel simplicity rather than political alliance, and foreshadows later condemnations of secularism, indifferentism, and state encroachment (e.g., *Mirari Vos*, *Quanta Cura*, *Syllabus of Errors*). It is also a personal testament of a pope determined to emulate the fortitude of his predecessor Pius VI.
