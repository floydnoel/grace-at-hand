# On Psychotherapy And Religion · English editorial summary

## Summary

This 1953 address by Pope Pius XII to the International Congress of Psychotherapy and Clinical Psychology affirms the legitimacy of depth psychology and psychotherapy as sciences while insisting they must operate within the moral and metaphysical framework of Catholic teaching. The Pope neither condemns psychoanalysis nor endorses it uncritically, instead laying out four principles — man as a psychic totality, a structured unit, a social unit, and a transcendent unit oriented toward God — that should govern the Christian practice of psychology.

## Historical Context

Written in the aftermath of World War II, the address reflects a period when Freudian and Jungian psychoanalysis were gaining wide influence in Europe and the United States, often in tension with traditional religious anthropology. Pius XII had already addressed moral limits of psychiatric methods in 1952; this 1953 speech complements that earlier intervention by focusing on the psychologist's fundamental attitude. The Cold War context, with competing materialist and spiritualist worldviews, also forms a backdrop — the Pope is careful to neither reject modern science wholesale nor concede ground to deterministic or reductionist models of the human person.

## Key Themes

- **The soul retains primacy**: Deep psychic dynamisms (instincts, subconscious drives) are real forces but are subordinate to the spiritual soul with its intellect and will; therapists must not treat man as merely a bundle of determinisms.
- **"Essential" and "existential" man are not opposed**: Existential, concrete man is not exempt from the ontological and metaphysical laws of human nature; a "personalist" ethic that sets aside natural morality is erroneous.
- **Sexual education and psychoanalysis**: Cautions against "complete sexual initiation" as therapy and against the view that unconscious sexual conflicts can only be resolved by making them fully conscious; indirect treatment often suffices.
- **The limits of analytic disclosure**: Secrets, especially the seal of confession and professional secrets, must never be divulged — even for therapeutic gain.
- **Legitimate self-love is not pathology**: Unconditioned extroversion is not the norm; charity toward neighbor presupposes proper love of self, as Christ taught.
- **Religious dynamism is not the source of faith**: The subconscious "gravitation toward the infinite" may be a real psychic phenomenon, but natural and supernatural knowledge of God comes from clear reason and revelation, not unconscious impulse.
- **Guilt is not merely psychological**: A genuine sense of real fault cannot be cured by psychotherapeutic means; only contrition and sacramental absolution address the fault itself.
- **Material sin cannot be counseled**: Even when subjectively inculpable, material sin disorders man's finality; a therapist may never advise it as a therapeutic expedient.

## Significance

The address is one of the earliest authoritative Catholic engagements with psychoanalysis and remains a touchstone for Catholic anthropology in psychology. It stakes out a middle path — neither rejecting modern depth psychology as inherently corrupt nor uncritically absorbing its presuppositions. The distinction between ontological and psychological perspectives, the insistence that the soul's centrality is a real (not merely theoretical) fact, and the refusal to separate "essential" from "existential" man all anticipate later personalist developments in Catholic thought. The speech is still cited in Catholic bioethics and pastoral psychology as a framework for integrating clinical practice with Christian doctrine.
