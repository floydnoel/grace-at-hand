## Summary

*Spiritus Paraclitus* ("The Spirit of the Comforter") is a papal encyclical issued by Pope Benedict XV on September 15, 1920, to mark the fifteenth centenary of the death of St. Jerome. The document uses Jerome's life, scholarship, and teaching as a lens to reaffirm Catholic doctrine on Sacred Scripture — its divine inspiration, absolute inerrancy, historical trustworthiness, and supreme authority — while urging clergy and laity alike to engage in devout, daily reading of the Bible.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was written in the wake of the First World War and amid rising theological controversy over biblical interpretation. Modernist and rationalist tendencies had gained ground in both Protestant and Catholic circles, with some scholars arguing for a distinction between "religious" and "profane" elements in Scripture, limiting inerrancy to the former. Pope Leo XIII's landmark encyclical *Providentissimus Deus* (1893) had already defended biblical inerrancy, but by 1920 the debate had intensified. Pope Pius X had also established the Pontifical Biblical Commission (1902) and the Biblical Institute in Rome (1909) to guide Catholic exegesis. Benedict XV wrote both to honor St. Jerome's 1500th anniversary and to confront what he saw as a creeping "itching for novelty" that threatened to undermine Catholic teaching on the Bible.

## Key Themes

- **Divine Inspiration and Inerrancy**: Scripture has God as its author; inspiration extends to every word and phrase, and error is incompatible with it. The "primary/secondary" distinction proposed by some modern critics is firmly rejected.
- **St. Jerome as Model**: Jerome's ascetic dedication to biblical study, his mastery of Hebrew and Greek, his labor on the Latin Vulgate, and his humble submission to the Church and the Chair of Peter are held up as the ideal for all Catholic exegetes.
- **Historical Truth of Scripture**: The Bible's historical narratives are not merely "relative" truths reflecting popular opinion, but records of events as they actually occurred. This applies especially to the Gospels, whose eyewitness testimony must be accepted as faithful and entire.
- **Love of Scripture as Love of Christ**: "Ignorance of the Bible means ignorance of Christ." The ultimate goal of biblical study is not academic mastery but spiritual transformation — knowledge of and intimacy with Christ.
- **Role of the Clergy**: Priests are duty-bound to study Scripture assiduously so they can preach with power and defend the faith. "A mere holy rusticity only avails the man himself."
- **Literal and Spiritual Interpretation**: Exegesis must begin with the literal or historical sense, and only then may it seek deeper spiritual meanings — never abandoning or contradicting the literal foundation.
- **Submission to the Church**: Jerome's example of deferring to the Roman Pontiff in doctrinal disputes is presented as the rule for all Catholic interpreters.

## Significance

*Spiritus Paraclitus* stands as a key document in the modern Catholic magisterium on Scripture, reinforcing and extending the teaching of *Providentissimus Deus* at a moment when historical-critical methods were challenging traditional understandings of biblical truth. It anticipated later controversies over biblical inerrancy and historicity that would resurface at the Second Vatican Council and beyond. The encyclical also cemented St. Jerome's reputation as the Church's "Greatest Doctor" for biblical studies and helped inspire renewed lay reading of Scripture through the Society of St. Jerome. Its insistence that the literal sense is the foundation of all interpretation remains a cornerstone of Catholic exegesis.
