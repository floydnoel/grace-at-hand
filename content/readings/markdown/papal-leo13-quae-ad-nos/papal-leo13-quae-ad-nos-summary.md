## Summary

*Quae Ad Nos* is an apostolic letter issued by Pope Leo XIII in 1902, addressed to the bishops of Bohemia and Moravia (roughly today's Czech Republic). The pope expresses sorrow over reports of widespread defections from the Catholic faith in those regions and urges the bishops to hold a synod to coordinate a unified response. He also warns against partisan divisions among the clergy, which weaken the Church's ability to defend the faith.

## Historical Context

The letter was written near the very end of Leo XIII's long pontificate (1878–1903), in the 25th and final year of his reign. Bohemia and Moravia were then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw rising nationalism, secularism, and anti-clerical movements across Central Europe. A significant "loss of faith" movement (often linked to the *Los-von-Rom* — "Away from Rome" — agitation) was drawing Czech-speaking Catholics away from the Church, fueled by nationalist resentment against the German-speaking hierarchy and by broader rationalist trends.

## Key Themes

- **Pastoral vigilance**: The pope does not doubt the bishops' efforts but insists the gravity of the crisis demands redoubled action ("the magnitude of the danger persuades Us to goad the willing").
- **Episcopal unity and collegiality**: He orders a joint meeting of all the bishops of Bohemia and Moravia to deliberate with one mind and to submit their decisions to Rome for approval.
- **Clerical unity**: He expressly condemns "partisan zeal" dividing the clergy, calling it a critical weakness at a moment when every force must be united for defense.
- **Roman primacy**: Decisions from the proposed synod must be referred to the Apostolic See for sanction, affirming Rome's supervisory role over local churches.
- **Solicitude for the laity**: The entire document is framed as the care of a shepherd ("Pastors") for a flock under attack by "enemies of the faith."

## Significance

*Quae Ad Nos* is a relatively short but pointed example of Leo XIII's pastoral governance during a period of Catholic retrenchment in Central Europe. It illustrates the papacy's strategy of strengthening episcopal coordination and suppressing internal clerical divisions in the face of secular nationalist movements. While not a major doctrinal encyclical, it is historically valuable for understanding the Church's response to the *Los-von-Rom* crisis and for demonstrating Leo XIII's emphasis on hierarchical unity as the first line of defense against large-scale apostasy.
