## Summary

*Mit Brennender Sorge* ("With Burning Anxiety") is a papal encyclical issued by Pope Pius XI in 1937, addressed to German bishops and Catholics. It openly condemns the Nazi regime's violations of the 1933 Reichskonkordat and its systematic persecution of the Church, while rejecting Nazi ideology — particularly its racial theories, deification of the state, and hostility to Christianity.

## Historical Context

Written in German (a rare departure from Latin), the encyclical was drafted in secret and smuggled into Germany for Palm Sunday distribution in 1937. It followed the 1933 Reichskonkordat, a treaty the Vatican had signed with Hitler's government hoping to protect Church rights — an agreement the Nazis almost immediately began violating. By 1937, the regime had suppressed Catholic organizations, closed confessional schools, arrested clergy, and promoted neo-pagan racial ideology. The encyclical was read from every Catholic pulpit in Germany, provoking a fierce crackdown.

## Key Themes

- **Rejection of state idolatry**: Condemns the elevation of race, people, or state above God as a form of idolatry.
- **Defense of natural law**: Affirms that human laws contradicting divine law have no moral authority.
- **Christ as sole foundation**: Rejects any "national church" or attempt to place mortal leaders alongside Christ.
- **Church unity and papal primacy**: Defends the universal Church against proposals for a national German Church.
- **Parental rights in education**: Affirms parents' primary right to educate children in their faith, denouncing state coercion in schools.
- **Freedom of conscience**: Declares that laws impeding the profession and practice of faith are against natural law.
- **Faithful endurance**: Encourages clergy and laity to heroism in the face of persecution, promising that liberation will come.

## Significance

The encyclical was the boldest public condemnation of Nazism by any major institution during the pre-war period. It remains a landmark Catholic social document for its articulation of natural law, religious liberty, and the limits of state authority. It prefigured later developments in Catholic teaching on human dignity and religious freedom, and stands as a powerful witness to the Church's resistance against totalitarian ideology — as well as a continuing reminder of the cost of that witness.
