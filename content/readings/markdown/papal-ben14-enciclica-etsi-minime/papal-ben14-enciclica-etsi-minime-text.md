ENCYCLICAL ETSI MINIME OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Venerable Brothers, Patriarchs, Primates, Archbishops, and Bishops. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. Although we have no doubt that all those entrusted with the care of souls—and you above all, Venerable Brothers, elevated to the office of the Apostolate and established by God in the dignity of the prelacy—devote your primary concern to ensuring that the Christian people, nourished in a wholesome manner with the rudiments of the Faith at the pasture of heavenly doctrine, may be happily guided along the path of the Lord’s precepts, following you who hold high the torch, we cannot, however, refrain from urging you, through the exhortations of Our authority and Our paternal love, to promote with greater zeal the most sacred and salutary work of Christian doctrine, removing the obstacles that stand in the way of the salvation of souls. 1. But since we are addressing those who know the law and exhorting the prudent Bishops of the Churches—who lack neither piety nor the resources of Sacred Scripture—we deem it superfluous to reiterate with numerous arguments that, to attain heavenly happiness, to believe in a confused and indistinct manner in the Mysteries revealed by God and taught by the Catholic Church. This heavenly doctrine, transmitted by God and received through listening, must be received from the voice of a legitimate and faithful teacher, in such a way that its fundamental truths are explained individually and presented to the faithful as truths to be believed—some by necessity of means and others by necessity of precept. Although we affirm that one is justified through faith—since faith is the principle and foundation of salvation, enabling us to ultimately reach the longed-for future City—it is equally clear that faith alone is not sufficient. One must know the path and remain steadfastly on it—that is, the precepts of God and the Church, the virtues to be cultivated, and the vices to be carefully avoided. 2. Since all of this is contained in the first rudiments of the Catholic Faith—or, as is commonly said, in Christian doctrine—it is the specific duty of the bishops to ensure that it is explained in all dioceses and in every place in a clear and methodical manner; nor may they, without tacit reproach of conscience, neglect this duty; indeed, they must devote all their care and diligence to this supremely necessary work. We do not believe that this task is assigned to the Bishop in such an exclusive manner as to require his constant presence in the teaching of Christian doctrine, to question the children in person and to explain the Mysteries of the Faith we profess. We are well aware that the burden of apostolic service weighs heavily on the commitment to pastoral care. We have come to fully understand, having previously led the Church of Ancona and then that of Bologna, how the Bishop, who wishes to fulfill his duties completely, is tossed about by countless and varied concerns, like waves. The Bishop will fulfill this duty by—even at times other than during the Pastoral Visit—occasionally being present where sound doctrine is taught to Christians, questioning boys and girls about what they have heard, and explaining in his own words the Mysteries of our Religion. This commitment on the part of the Shepherd will prove greatly beneficial to the flock entrusted to him, and his example will inspire others to cultivate the vineyard of the Lord of Hosts with all their strength. 3. This way of tending to the Church was regarded almost as a law, not only by the ancients but also by more recent bishops inscribed in the register of the Blessed, such as Charles Borromeo, Francis de Sales, Turibius, and Alexander Sauli. Some of them (as their writings attest), finding themselves occupied and hindered by more serious duties, and unable to fulfill it personally, appointed a Vicar chosen from among the Canons or Priests to this grave duty of theirs so that, having taken on this pastoral ministry, he might instruct adolescents in the basic truths of the Faith and in the duties of Religion. 4. The Bishop’s example will therefore be of the utmost importance and highly beneficial to the spiritual growth of souls if, as we have said above, he carries out this duty in all parishes, at all times, and especially while traveling throughout the Diocese. But as everyone can imagine, he cannot be assisted by sufficient resources. Therefore, to achieve this goal, he must ensure with the utmost diligence that the zeal and diligence of those he has chosen as his vicars for such a praiseworthy and meritorious task do not wane. 5. First and foremost, there are two obligations imposed by the Council of Trent upon those who care for souls. The first requires that on feast days they deliver a sermon on divine matters; the second that they instruct children and anyone ignorant of the Divine Law in the rudiments of the Faith. If, on the appointed days, parish priests deliver the required homily, let it not deafen the ears with persuasive words of human wisdom, but—with words suited to the listeners’ capacity—let it instill the Spirit in their hearts; if they proclaim a Mystery—especially during the season when the Church commemorates it—sowing seeds that inspire virtue and reject vice, especially the most serious vices that ravage the people with the greatest disgrace; if, during these very days, they nourish the children—like newborn infants—with the milk of the Doctrine, questioning one or the other, dispelling doubts and uncertainties; if, finally, following the Apostle, they devote themselves to reading, exhortation, and teaching, so that the believer may become perfect and equipped for every good work, it is reasonable to believe that the result will meet expectations and that a people pleasing to God and doing good may easily arise. 6. It is also amply demonstrated by experience that the efforts of the parish priest alone are not sufficient, since one person cannot teach everyone when the number of people exceeds the teacher’s capacity. Nevertheless, the Bishop who devotes himself wholeheartedly and with all his zeal to the good of the Church entrusted to him will never lack the appropriate remedies. In fact, he may turn to those preparing for the Tonsure, to those advancing toward the dignity of the Priesthood by ascending the steps of the Minor and Major Orders, and, finally, to those who are actively seeking ways to secure ecclesiastical benefices. The Bishop will remind them, with authoritative and stern words (and let his actions back up his words), that he will never consent to the Tonsure, once the required age has been reached, or to the conferral of Minor Orders, but above all the Major Orders, to anyone who has neglected to assure parish priests of their availability to teach Christian doctrine. Let him therefore distribute all these clerics among the individual parishes of his city and diocese, assigning some of them to specific churches as well. Furthermore, let him make it known—and guarantee by his promise—that in the assignment of parishes and other benefices in accordance with the law, the zeal and diligence shown by the clerics in this work will carry weight and importance; in this way it will be evident that the task of teaching has not been assigned solely to the head of the diocese, but that many must be available for it, so that all together may contribute to the fulfillment of his mission. 7. To all this must be added that, through the Sacred Apostolic Constitutions—and especially the seventh issued by Leo X, our predecessor of blessed memory—appropriate provisions have been made so that both schoolteachers, in instructing their pupils, and devout women, in teaching young girls (under the urgent guidance of the Bishop), may nourish and strengthen them with sound and pure doctrine, as if it were vital nourishment. It is also well established that the Bishop himself can and must strongly urge the preachers to impress upon the ears and hearts of parents, during their sermons, the importance of instructing their children in the truths of our Religion; if they are not up to the task, it will be necessary for the children to be brought to church, where the precepts of Divine Law are taught. In many places (and where it does not yet exist, let it be introduced), the pious and praiseworthy custom has also taken root of assisting the parish priest in carrying out this task, through the efforts of laypeople—both men and women—who, taking it upon themselves to offer their service for Christian instruction, listen to the boys and girls as they recite from memory the Lord’s Prayer, the Hail Mary, the Apostles’ Creed, and all the other prayers. In other localities, congregations have been established for the purpose of teaching Christian doctrine; their founding was rightly showered with well-deserved praise by Pius V, of blessed memory, in his Constitution beginning with the words “Ex debito,” which urges that such congregations be promoted with the utmost diligence in all dioceses. All these initiatives, directed toward the same goal, if carefully nurtured, will give everyone the confident assurance that, even though the number of workers is small and the harvest is plentiful, there will be no shortage of those who will break bread for the children who implore it. 8. It is also well known that not only children and those of more mature age remain ignorant of divine matters, but also men and the elderly themselves are largely unaware of sound doctrine, either because they never learned it, or, having learned it long ago, forgetfulness has gradually erased it. The provident diligence of the bishops can remedy this evil as well, if their collaborators make it their duty to apply the prepared remedies with care. 9. Let us turn our attention to those in early childhood: many ask to be admitted to the Holy Eucharist and Confirmation. Few, in truth, fail to display this firm resolve, as if it were an irresistible desire. The Bishop, therefore, should admonish the parish priests and vigorously order them not to admit anyone to the Sacrament of the Eucharist nor to issue the so-called Confirmation Certificate to anyone who does not know the fundamentals of the Faith and Doctrine and the value and power of the Sacrament. In this way, it seems that proper care can be taken for young children. 10. If, on the other hand, we speak of adolescents, since each one receives his or her own gift from God, it is well known from experience that some set out on the path of the ecclesiastical life, while others on that of the secular life. We have already addressed the former when we spoke of those who wish to be admitted to Holy Orders. It seems only one thing can be added: it would be appropriate and of great benefit if, when those who present themselves for the examination are questioned, the Bishop were to ask first and foremost about the essence contained in Christian doctrine. Indeed, experience, the teacher of truth, has made it evident that some of them, though adorned with elegant and pure Latin and abundantly educated in the various sciences and well-versed in all matters concerning Holy Orders, when questioned on Christian doctrine, responded in an unsatisfactory and entirely irrelevant manner. 11. If we then turn our attention to those who spend their lives in the world, it is evident that the majority of them are headed toward marriage. Indeed, they cannot be united in marriage if the parish priest, as is his duty, has discovered—by asking specific questions—that the man and woman are ignorant of what is necessary for salvation. The Bishop can hardly tolerate such great and disastrous ignorance; let him remind the shepherds of souls of their duty and, should they fail to comply, punish their negligence. 12. All people, of every age and social condition, are accustomed to cleansing the defilements of the soul through the Sacrament of Penance. The Bishop shall therefore ensure that the priest who hears confessions hold as certain and unchangeable that sacramental absolution is not valid when imparted to those who do not know what is indispensable by virtue of the means of grace, and that people cannot be reconciled with God through this sacrament unless, first, the fog of ignorance has been dispelled and they have been led to the knowledge of the Faith. The Confessor must also be aware that absolution must be deferred to a later time for those who, through their own fault, do not know what is indispensable by precept. In this case, the penitent may still be absolved if he acknowledges his guilt for this non-insurmountable ignorance, asks God for forgiveness, and sincerely promises the confessor that he will strive, with God’s help, to learn even what is required by precept. 13. If, therefore, pastors adopt this method of forming the Christian people, and if they ensure that their counsel, efforts, and intentions must be directed toward the proposed method, it is reasonable to hope that the flock, through faith and good works, will progress over time to such an extent that it may be transformed into a dwelling place of God in the Holy Spirit. But since this is a matter of the utmost importance—and no other has been established that is more useful for the glory of God and the salvation of souls—no one should be surprised if countless obstacles are continually placed in the way. 14. Sometimes there are small and humble churches scattered throughout the countryside, some near and others quite far from the parish church, where, on feast days, heads of households go with their children to listen to the priest as he celebrates the Sacred Mysteries. This means that they are almost never present in their parish and cannot hear any teaching on the Mysteries of the Faith, the Precepts, or the Sacraments. The bishop must remedy this situation with the full weight of his authority. First, with regard to the small churches near the parish church, he must issue a specific decree prohibiting the celebration of Mass there until the parish priest has himself celebrated Mass, delivered the homily, and fulfilled the remaining duties of his office. In this way, the parish church will be attended by a multitude of the faithful who will flock there. As for the small churches located far from the parish church, however, since it is very difficult—due to the distance between the locations—for parishioners, having ruled out the nearest church, to undertake a long and arduous journey, especially during the winter when rivers overflow, to reach the parish church and attend the Divine Offices there, the Bishop shall decree—under penalty of severe sanctions—that the priests assigned to those churches teach the people the fundamental tenets of Christian doctrine and explain the Divine Law. However, the parish priest must be admonished not to place too much trust in the work of others, but to ascertain for himself the state of affairs when children are to be administered the sacraments of the Eucharist and Confirmation, and others request the sacrament of Matrimony. 15. Cities, too, present specific challenges. It often happens, in fact, that in certain churches—especially those of religious orders—festivities are celebrated with solemn rites and large gatherings of people. For this reason, if catechism classes are held in the parish church early in the morning or immediately after lunch, no one—or very few people—will attend, and they will cite the scheduled time as an excuse. If times more convenient for the people are not chosen, experience confirms that the people will flock to the church where the feast day is solemnly celebrated and, drawn by the liturgical pomp, will neglect Christian doctrine—not without grave harm to their souls. Since it is not possible to establish a definitive and general rule in this regard, we wish this task to be left to the diligent parish priest, who, taking into account the nature of the place, the circumstances, and the people, and after weighing the overall situation, find a way to harmonize the celebration of the feast day with Christian doctrine, so that one may not be a stumbling block to the other. If the Regulars and the Exempt Orders oppose this and, despite being admonished by the Bishops, feel authorized to compromise the teaching of Christian doctrine, We grant the local Ordinaries Our authority, which extends to the Exempt Orders, and Apostolic solicitude will not lack other means to prevent parish churches from being deprived of the consideration they deserve. 16. It could prove highly beneficial for the education of the Christian people to appoint visitors—some of whom would tour the city and others the diocese—to conduct thorough investigations into all matters, so that the Bishop, having been informed of the merits of each pastor, may decree rewards or punishments. 17. Following in the footsteps of Pope Clement VIII and our other predecessors, we exhort in the Lord and strongly recommend that, in transmitting Christian doctrine, the booklet written by Cardinal Bellarmine at the behest of Clement himself be used. After being carefully examined by the Congregation specifically appointed for this purpose and approved, Pope Clement himself ordered that it be published, with the most worthy intention that everyone thereafter adhere to the same and only method of teaching and learning Christian doctrine. Nothing is more desirable than this uniformity, nothing more appropriate or more useful for preventing errors from creeping in among the many varieties of catechisms. If, in any given place, it should become necessary—due to specific local needs—to use another booklet, great care must be taken to ensure that it contains nothing and that nothing is introduced into it that is at odds with Catholic Truth. Care must also be taken to ensure that the dogmas of the Faith are explained in a simple and clear manner, adding any necessary parts that may have been omitted and eliminating the superfluous. A concise and unambiguous teaching method is usually of great use for simpler questioning when assessing the children’s progress. 18. This booklet must also contain the Acts of Faith, Hope, and Charity, composed in a sound and competent manner. If this is not the case, once they have been perfected, they should be printed in the correct form. These Acts are best conveyed with few rather than many words, provided that through them the full force and nature of the virtue are revealed. Since for those who profess the Christian faith, the habit and practice of frequently uttering these Acts are of the utmost necessity, so that their use may not be confined to narrow limits nor restricted by anyone to a modest number each year, the Bishop, concerned for his own salvation no less than that of others, shall issue prudent directives so that in the parishes of the city and the diocese, the pastors, immediately after the celebration of the festive Mass, kneeling before the altar, recite the aforementioned Acts of the Virtues in a clear and intelligible voice, taking care to precede the congregation, who are to repeat the words they pronounce. In this way, the faithful, almost without realizing it, will learn them by heart and will form the habit of engaging in this pious practice not only on feast days but also on the remaining days. 19. These salutary guidelines for instructing the flock, which we have wished to bring to your attention, Venerable Brothers, through this Apostolic Letter of Ours, may be recognized by each of you as consistent with Our pastoral exhortations, already published when, with paternal love, We cared for the Church of Bologna, Our bride. These guidelines are, moreover, drawn from the Pontifical Constitutions, whose validity is attested to by the testimony and example of renowned bishops. Since we know from experience that they will prove of the greatest benefit, we exhort and urge you with all possible fervor, and we implore you, by the merciful heart of Our God, to devote yourselves with a resolute and steadfast spirit, in accordance with the task entrusted to your pastoral ministry, to the implementation of the foregoing, carefully considering that all the effort, commitment, and attention devoted to this end will be rewarded by God, the giver of every good thing. We cordially impart to you the Apostolic Blessing. Given in Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on February 7, 1742, in the second year of our Pontificate.
