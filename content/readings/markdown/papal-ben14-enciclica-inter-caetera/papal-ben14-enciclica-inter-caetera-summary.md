## Summary

Inter Caetera addresses two principal abuses: revelry on the last night of Carnival runs so late that it spills into Ash Wednesday — people go directly from dances and games to Mass in their costumes, receive ashes, and then sleep through the morning of Lent, while accusing any bishop who objects of being indiscreet. The second is the growing practice of holding masquerades, street dancing, and public performances by acrobats and charlatans on Sundays and feast days, which the Pope regards as a profanation of holy time. Benedict instructs the bishops to suppress these excesses with vigour and offers a Plenary Indulgence to encourage the faithful to replace Carnival licence with prayer, confession, communion, and visits to the Blessed Sacrament during the pre-Lenten weeks.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was issued eight years into Benedict XIV's pontificate (1740–1758), a period marked by the Pope's characteristically learned and moderate style of governance. Carnival in 18th-century Italy was a season of widespread public revelry, masquerading, and theatrical performances, often lasting from Septuagesima Sunday through Shrove Tuesday. The document reflects a perennial tension in Catholic moral teaching: the Church tolerated Carnival as a customary outlet before Lent but insisted that festivity must not descend into sacrilege or the desecration of feast days. Benedict XIV, a scholar-pope deeply versed in canon law and liturgy, wrote at a time when the Papal States still exercised direct temporal authority over central Italy, and he addresses the encyclical specifically to the bishops of that territory, who had complained to him about the growing disorder.

## Key Themes

- **The sanctity of feast days and the liturgical calendar.** The Pope insists that feast days are set apart for worship, not for public entertainments, and he abhors the idea that Carnival revelry should encroach upon the sacred season of Lent, especially Ash Wednesday.
- **The distinction between toleration and approval.** Benedict XIV distinguishes carefully between what the Church *tolerates* (Carnival amusements, permitted reluctantly to avoid greater evils) and what it *commands* or *approves*. Toleration does not justify extending or abusing the tolerated practice.
- **The duty of episcopal vigilance.** Bishops must punish abuses without human respect; the excuses of custom or predecessor tolerance are not valid defences. The Pope cites a Catholic university's censure of the "Feast of Fools" to show that past toleration does not bind present reformers.
- **The proper ordering of popular piety.** The Pope offers an alternative to Carnival licence: tridua of Eucharistic exposition, sermons, visits to churches and hospitals, and the reception of the sacraments — and grants a Plenary Indulgence to those who participate.
- **Continuity with patristic and conciliar tradition.** The encyclical draws on St. Basil, St. Augustine, St. Charles Borromeo, the Council of Trent, Pope Clement XI, and earlier synodal legislation, anchoring its discipline in a long tradition.

## Significance

*Inter Caetera* is a representative document of Benedict XIV's pastoral jurisprudence: firm in principle, moderate in tone, and rich in theological and canonical citation. It exemplifies the 18th-century Catholic approach to regulating popular religion — seeking not to abolish Carnival outright but to contain its excesses and channel popular energy toward devotion. The encyclical is also noteworthy for its Plenary Indulgence concession, a characteristically Baroque instrument for encouraging sacramental practice. While the document addresses a specific local problem in the Papal States, its principles — the priority of the liturgical calendar, the distinction between toleration and approval, and the bishop's duty to correct even deeply rooted customs — remain relevant to Catholic teaching on the ordering of festivity and the sacred.
