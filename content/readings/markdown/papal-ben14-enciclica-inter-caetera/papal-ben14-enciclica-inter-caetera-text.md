ENCYCLICAL INTER CAETERA OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Archbishops and Bishops of the Papal States, on the Bacchanalia. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. Among the many matters that trouble Us, when We are compelled to tolerate Carnival festivities in Our Papal States, there are two in particular about which certain zealous bishops of the aforementioned States have complained to Us, expressing their just grievances either verbally or in writing. One consists in the fact that, for the most part, the all-night parties, dances, and games on the last night of Carnival go on so long that they even encroach upon the beginning of the first day of Lent; Thus it sometimes happens that people, leaving the dance, the games, and the all-night revelry, go—though without masks but still wearing the costumes in which they were disguised—to church to attend Mass and receive Ash Wednesday ashes, and then return to their homes, sleeping in their beds at least throughout the morning of the first day of Lent; nor do they fail to accuse the bishop of being indiscreet if he complains—and even more so if he seeks to punish such excesses. The second: that in some cities of the Papal States, an abuse introduced a few years ago is either being introduced or is being sought to be maintained—namely, that people go out in costume even on feast days, and that, much more so on feast days than on weekdays, acrobats, charlatans, and other such people perform their acts in the squares before large crowds, and that, finally, on feast days themselves, street dancing is more frequent than on weekdays. As for the first point, there is absolutely no need to dwell on it at length; it suffices to mention it for everyone to be horrified by it. And since the standard defense of those who advocate for vice and excess usually consists in claiming that many other predecessor bishops—who were upright men—knew about it and said nothing (leaving aside the truth of the matter, which is mostly asserted but not substantiated), the response from one of the renowned Catholic universities should suffice for everyone. When asked to give its opinion on a certain scandalous festival called the “Festa de’ Pazzi,” which had been introduced in some dioceses and defended on the grounds that it had been tolerated by so many predecessor bishops, not only issued a condemnatory censure against it, but in response to the opposition raised, added: “Do not listen to the insidious words of men who say that our predecessors, who were considered notable figures, permitted this: it is enough for us to live as they did. Undoubtedly, such an argument is diabolical, such persuasion infernal: you do not know whether the end of their lives was good or bad.” The censure of this University is set forth in full, as they say, in the Appendix to the Works of Peter of Blois (p. 782), and in the treatise written in French by John Savaron against masks (pp. 32 ff.). And so we earnestly urge each one of you, O Venerable Brothers, in whose dioceses this excess is either being attempted or is already in force, not to spare any part of your zeal, so that it may not be introduced where it has not yet been introduced, and where it has been introduced, it may be eradicated, severely punishing, without regard for human sentiment, those who invent or uphold it: for this is not the way to begin Lent, as Saint Basil exclaimed in Homily 2 on Fasting, published in Paris in 1722: “Of those things whose beginning is rejected, the whole must undoubtedly be rejected as well” (Volume II, p. 13); and: “This day is the vestibule of fasting. Whoever is defiled in the vestibule is not worthy to enter the sanctuary” (p. 15). It is too contrary to the respect due to the Churches and to the Sacred Rite of receiving Ash Wednesday ashes to enter the Houses of God in masquerade attire—even without a mask—and to approach the Holy Altar in the same manner to receive the sacred ashes from the priest’s hands, with the admonition to reflect on death. Turning now to the second of the aforementioned points, We do not intend here to protest or preach against Carnival, nor to deliver any historical dissertation against it. If We wished to do so, We could easily do it by transcribing what St. Charles Borromeo so wisely wrote on this subject in his \*Libro Memoriale\*, printed in the seventh part of the \*Acts of the Church of Milan\*, when, in thanksgiving for the city’s deliverance from the plague, he exhorted the people with zeal and learning to cease celebrating Carnival; and by including in this letter of ours what was eruditely compiled by the monk De L’Isle in his new \*Treatise on Fasting\*, Book VI, Chapter 6, where, in discussing the Bacchanalia of the pagans, he demonstrates how many disorders in our Carnival are contrary to the principles of Christianity. We shall content ourselves with saying that, albeit reluctantly and to avoid greater evils, the Church merely permits and tolerates Carnival revelry, as the famous and pious theologian Thomas Stapletone concludes, after well-founded reasoning, in his Eighth Oration \*Contra Bacchanalia\*, Volume 2 of his works, on page 556: there is, moreover, a clear distinction between things that are commanded and things that are tolerated. “It is one thing what we teach, another what we uphold; another what we are compelled to punish, and while we punish, we are compelled to tolerate” (St. Augustine, \*Against Faustus\*, Book 20, Chapter 21); from which it follows that there is no room in matters that are merely tolerated for extending the scope or exercising arbitrary discretion. We have reestablished in this city of Rome, as instituted by Our worthy Predecessors, a system whereby—despite the brief duration of Carnival—masks are not to be worn in any way on the days of the festival or on Friday. We wish the same to be observed throughout the rest of Our State. Nor will We fail to make this decision of Ours known to the Cardinal Legates of the Provinces and to the local prelates and governors. It is also Our desire that on feast days there be no charlatans or acrobats in the squares; always keeping before Our eyes the words of the Prophet Isaiah in Chapter 1: “My soul hates your new moons and your festivals; they have become a burden to me; I can scarcely bear them” (Is 1:14), and those of the Prophet Malachi in chapter 2: “I will scatter the dung of your festivals in your faces” (Mal 2:3). But since one cannot always immediately obtain from men what one would like—and what it would be right to obtain—we hereby declare that we do not wish to see charlatans or street performers in the squares on feast days, nor in other places in the cities and castles, neither in the morning nor in the afternoon. For if in any city or place there is some harmful custom that must be tolerated even on feast days, this shall never be permitted in the morning; care shall also be taken to ensure that such activities do not occur in the afternoon; and since this cannot be achieved without serious disruption, the aforementioned or similar entertainments must never be permitted to take place except after Vespers and the instruction in the Christian faith; regarding this, we shall not fail to issue the appropriate orders to the Cardinal Legates and the Governors. This is, in the aforementioned state of affairs, the system of St. Charles Borromeo in his First Provincial Council, Part I, under the heading “On the Observance of Feast Days,” combining it with what he decreed in the Third Provincial Council under the same heading; and this is also what the blessed memory of Clement XI emphasized in two of his Circular Letters, one dated January 11, 1719, the other dated January 4, 1721, which are the eleventh and twelfth in his Bullarium on page 533. And while We affirm Our willingness to follow in the footsteps of St. Charles, as marked by Our aforementioned worthy Predecessor, We also affirm that, although We have thus far spoken of the prohibition of certain things, We have not intended, nor do We intend, to lift the prohibition on so many others, which are found in the Canons, in the Apostolic Constitutions, and in the Provincial or Diocesan Councils, concerning Carnival festivities, and particularly with regard to ecclesiastical persons; keeping in mind the provision of the Council of Trent (session 22, chapter 1), which, after discussing the canonical provisions prohibiting clergy from luxury, feasts, dancing, and card games, addresses the bishops as follows: “If they have learned that some of these provisions have fallen into disuse, they should seek to restore them to practice as soon as possible and ensure that they are strictly observed by all, regardless of any custom, so that they themselves may not suffer the just punishment for having neglected to correct their subjects, by God’s vengeance” (Conc. Trid., sess. 22, chap. 1). Anyone with even a passing knowledge of ecclesiastical discipline will readily know that there were certain ancient and infamous superstitions among the Gentiles, which took place on the first day of January in honor of Janus and the goddess Strena, in which Christians would sometimes take part, indulging in drunkenness and games, with men dressing as women and women as men. Nor did the Holy Fathers fail, in their sermons and even in the Councils of the Church, to denounce this intolerable abuse, and to establish severe penalties against the offenders, a matter which We have discussed at length in Our work \*On the Feasts of the Lord\*, when We dealt with the feast of the Circumcision of Jesus Christ Our Redeemer. Once this wicked custom was abolished, another was unfortunately introduced, namely, certain public indulgences during the weeks of Septuagesima, Sexagesima, and Quinquagesima, during which the Holy Church presents to us the principal Mysteries of our Redemption, to prepare us well for penance during the season of Lent, as We also demonstrated in Our Fourteenth Notification in Volume I of those We published, when we were in Bologna governing that Church; and this is precisely Carnival, which is described as follows by a renowned bishop, Monsignor Graziani, in his Synod of Amelia, held in the year 1595, on page 150: “From this it followed (so much did this evil custom corrupt us) that the days between Septuagesima and Lent—which the Holy Mother Church regards with great veneration as somber and mournful—have, due to an intrusive merriment marked by licentiousness, and a love of merriment, have not only been turned into frivolous and disorderly revelry, but have almost been consecrated to a sort of collective madness; and this licentiousness was carried to such an extent that it dragged down the laws themselves, the magistrates themselves; and what no well-ordered government would grant even to any private citizen is now entirely sanctioned by public authority, and the people, almost forgetting the name by which they are praised, have degenerated to the rites and customs of the profane”; which is why the famous Ghislain Busbecq, who served as ambassador in the mid-sixteenth century from Emperor Ferdinand I to Suleiman II, wrote that, having been a Turkish ambassador in a Christian country during Carnival, upon his return to Turkey, he reported that at a certain time of year—namely, Carnival—Christians went mad, and by virtue of a certain powder sprinkled on their heads, they came to their senses: “It has been taken as a fact that a Turk, who at that time had come to us as an ambassador on matters of state, upon returning home reported that Christians on certain days were raving mad, until, after being sprinkled in the church with a special kind of powder, they came to their senses and were healed.” The Church did not stand idly by but opposed this public disorder as best she could; she always resorted to prayers and works of piety, begging God to suspend the scourges against sinners and, through His mighty help, to provide a way to remedy such a great evil; and among the other devotions directed toward this end was the one undertaken by certain religious orders to abstain from eating meat and to subsist solely on dairy products, either from Septuagesima Sunday, or from Sexagesima Sunday, or from Quinquagesima Sunday, until Ash Wednesday; therefore, Carnival is called “meatless,” and in the Mozarabic Mass, the Sunday of Sessagesima is referred to as the Sunday on which meat must be eliminated. Other devotions have been introduced during the Carnival season for the same purpose by men of good will, and among many others, one of the most solemn and public is that introduced by Saint Philip Neri in this city of Rome—the visit to the seven churches—which Bacci describes in his biography; Our Predecessors, to inspire the Faithful during these days to receive the Sacraments frequently, have distributed the Treasury of Holy Indulgences: “With a new but very salutary measure, the Supreme Pontiffs, drawing upon the Church’s Treasury, have encouraged the faithful to receive the sacraments with great devotion during those three days (referring to the days of Carnival), and, judging by how things are going so far, there is hope that the devil, driven away from there, will be forced to seek out other wicked deeds” (Teofilo Raynaudo, Works, vol. 16, p. 412, no. 41). Therefore, O Venerable Brothers, take this path yourselves, and by your example invite others to do the same. Do not neglect, especially during these days of Carnival, your participation in the Divine Offices, the public celebration of Mass, visits to churches and hospitals, and invite clergy as well as God-fearing laypeople; for this is a good and approved form of devotion. Ensure that the Venerable Image is displayed for a triduum in one or more churches, with the Blessing given each evening, either during the week of Septuagesima, or during that of Sexagesima, or during that of Quinquagesima, or during all three; and by this present Circular Letter We grant a Plenary Indulgence, which You are to publish in the customary manner; and since it is directed toward a pious purpose, it is not precluded by any other Plenary Indulgence that the church in which the Venerable is displayed might hold by other titles, to anyone who, having confessed and received Communion, visits the Most Holy Sacrament on each day of the aforementioned three-day periods, as set forth above, praying with a sincere heart to His Divine Majesty according to the intention of the Holy Church, which is that set forth above. Our Apostolic Ministry required that We write this Letter to you. Your example, since you are so close to Us, will move those who are farthest away to turn to Us; and We shall not fail to offer each petitioner all Our help, extending to him and his diocese the indulgences granted to you. Finally, embracing you with all Our heart, We bestow upon each of you, Venerable Brothers, and upon the flock entrusted to each of you, the Apostolic Blessing. Given in Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on January 1, 1748, the eighth year of Our Pontificate.
