## Summary

*Pascendi Dominici Gregis* (Feeding the Lord's Flock) is a papal encyclical issued by Pope Pius X on September 8, 1907, condemning a movement he called "Modernism" — a set of theological and philosophical tendencies he describes as "the synthesis of all heresies." The document systematically lays out the errors of Modernism across philosophy, theology, history, criticism, apologetics, and church reform, and prescribes remedies to root it out from seminaries, universities, publications, and the clergy. It is one of the most forceful disciplinary encyclicals in modern Catholic history.

## Historical Context

The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw an intellectual crisis in the Catholic Church as biblical criticism, evolutionary thought (Darwin, Spencer), historical-critical methods, and Kantian and neo-Kantian philosophy spread through European universities. Some Catholic scholars sought to reconcile faith with modern science and history, arguing that doctrine evolves, that religious truth is grounded in subjective experience rather than objective reason, and that Church structures should adapt to democratic norms. Leo XIII (1878–1903) had cautiously opened the Church to modern scholarship, especially in biblical studies, but Pius X saw these currents as an existential threat. The encyclical was drafted under the guidance of Cardinal Joseph Sarto (who became Pius X) and the theologian Umberto Benigni, and was preceded by the decree *Lamentabili Sane Exitu* (July 1907), which condemned 65 Modernist propositions. *Pascendi* was the hammer that followed.

## Key Themes

- **Agnosticism and vital immanence**: Modernists, the encyclical argues, confine reason to phenomena and place religious knowledge in a subjective "sense" or "experience" arising from an inner need for the divine, denying that God can be known through rational argument or external revelation.
- **Evolution of dogma**: Modernists hold that dogmatic formulas are mere symbols that must change as religious consciousness develops; Pius X insists dogma is a fixed deposit of faith whose meaning is unchanging.
- **Separation of Christ of history from Christ of faith**: The encyclical condemns the distinction between a merely human "historical Jesus" and a "Christ of faith" constructed by believers, calling it destructive of the Incarnation.
- **Subjectivism in faith and experience**: Faith is reduced to a private, immanent religious feeling rather than intellectual assent to revealed truth.
- **Rejection of Church authority**: The document attacks Modernist calls for democratization, decentralization, lay empowerment, and subordination of the Church to the State.
- **The Church as a society divinely instituted, not a vital emanation**: Against Modernist claims that the Church and sacraments evolved from the collective religious consciousness of believers, Pius X reaffirms their direct institution by Christ.
- **Primacy of scholastic philosophy (especially Thomas Aquinas)**: The pope mandates Thomistic philosophy as the foundation of all sacred sciences, and orders the removal of modernist-leaning professors.
- **Establishment of "Councils of Vigilance"**: Each diocese is to form a secret council to monitor and report on Modernist activity.

## Significance

*Pascendi* became the defining document of the anti-Modernist campaign of the early 20th century, often called the "Modernist crisis." It imposed strict censorship, an oath against Modernism (mandated in 1910 for all clergy), and a climate of suspicion that suppressed intellectual exploration in Catholic theology for decades. The encyclical effectively ended the careers of prominent Catholic modernists such as Alfred Loisy and George Tyrrell. Its condemnation of historical-critical methods and evolution of dogma created a sharp divide between Catholic theology and mainstream academic biblical scholarship that only began to heal with *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) and the Second Vatican Council (1962–65). In the long view, *Pascendi* is remembered both as a decisive reassertion of traditional Catholic orthodoxy and as a painful chapter in the Church's engagement with modernity.
