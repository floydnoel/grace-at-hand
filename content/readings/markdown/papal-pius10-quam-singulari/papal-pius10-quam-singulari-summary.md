## Summary

*Quam Singulari* is a 1910 decree by Pope Pius X that lowered the age of First Communion to the "age of discretion" (around seven years old), mandating that children be admitted to the Eucharist and Confession as soon as they can distinguish the Eucharistic bread from ordinary bread, rather than waiting until ten, twelve, or fourteen as had become common practice.

## Historical Context

The decree came amid a long-running liturgical and pastoral dispute over the proper age for First Communion. After the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required annual Confession and Communion "after reaching the years of discretion," a custom arose in many regions postponing First Communion to later adolescence — often ten to fourteen years old. This delay was aggravated by Jansenist influences, which treated the Eucharist as a reward for the worthy rather than medicine for the weak. Pius X, who had already promoted frequent and daily Communion in 1905, aimed to remove these barriers, restore early Communion, and protect children from spiritual neglect.

## Key Themes

- **Age of discretion as the sole criterion** — Both Confession and Communion bind once a child attains the use of reason, typically around age seven.
- **Eucharist as remedy, not reward** — The decree explicitly rejects the Jansenist view, reaffirming the Council of Trent's teaching that the Eucharist is an antidote against daily faults and mortal sin.
- **Minimal prerequisites** — Full doctrinal knowledge is not required; only the ability to distinguish the Eucharistic bread from ordinary bread and a basic devotion suffice.
- **Pastoral responsibility** — Parents, confessors, pastors, and teachers share the duty to prepare children and bring them to Communion.
- **Condemnation of abusive practices** — Forbids barring children who have reached discretion from Confession, absolution, Viaticum, or Extreme Unction, and rejects infant burial rites for those old enough to reason.

## Significance

*Quam Singulari* transformed Catholic sacramental practice worldwide by establishing seven as the normative age for First Communion, a standard that remains universal today. It completed Pius X's Eucharistic reforms (following *Sacra Tridentina Synodus* on frequent Communion) and struck a lasting blow against Jansenist rigorism, reinforcing a pastoral, child-centered approach to the sacraments. The decree is now considered a cornerstone of modern Catholic catechetical and liturgical practice.
