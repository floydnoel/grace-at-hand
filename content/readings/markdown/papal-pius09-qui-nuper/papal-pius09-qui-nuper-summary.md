## Summary

*Qui Nuper* is an encyclical issued by Pope Pius IX on 18 June 1859, responding to the revolutionary upheavals sweeping across Italy that had encroached upon the Papal States. The pope reproaches the rebellion, laments that some papal provinces have withdrawn from his rule to align with the Italian government hostile to the Church, and declares that temporal sovereignty is necessary for the Holy See to exercise its spiritual authority freely.

## Historical Context

Italy in 1859 was in the throes of the *Risorgimento* — the nationalist movement for unification. The Second Italian War of Independence (April–July 1859) saw Piedmont-Sardinia and France defeat Austria, triggering uprisings across central Italy. Several territories of the Papal States rebelled and sought annexation to the emerging Italian kingdom under Victor Emmanuel II. Pius IX, who had begun his pontificate as a liberal but turned conservative after the 1848 revolutions, issued this encyclical to reaffirm the necessity of papal temporal power and to rally the bishops in prayer and support.

## Key Themes

- **Temporal sovereignty of the Holy See** — Pius IX insists that the Church must possess independent temporal authority to carry out its spiritual mission without interference from secular powers.
- **Reproach of rebellion** — The pope condemns the revolutionary movement and those subjects who "withdrew from Our paternal rule," framing the revolt as unjust and ungrateful.
- **Spiritual consolation in crisis** — In the midst of turmoil, the pope turns to prayer, citing Moses and Aaron interceding for Israel (Numbers 16) as a model for the bishops.
- **Unwavering fidelity to the apostolic office** — Pius IX vows to endure any danger rather than betray his oath as pope, positioning himself as a steadfast defender of the Church against its enemies.

## Significance

*Qui Nuper* stands as an early salvo in the final confrontation between the papacy and Italian unification. It articulates the argument for temporal power that would be reaffirmed just a decade later — after the loss of Rome in 1870 — in the more famous encyclical *Respiciens* and at the First Vatican Council. The document captures the Church's fraught transition from temporal ruler to purely spiritual authority, a defining drama of 19th-century Catholicism whose echoes persisted well into the 20th century with the Lateran Treaty of 1929.
