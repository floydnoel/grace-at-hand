# Gravissimum Supremi

**La Santa Sede**

ENCYCLICAL GRAVISSIMUM SUPREMI OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Venerable Brothers, Archbishops, Bishops, and Ordinaries of the Kingdom of Naples. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. The weighty ministry of the Supreme Apostolate, which was conferred upon Us without merit, requires above all two things: first, to lead those peoples to embrace the Holy Religion who have never received it or who, after having received it, lost it through some wretched, unfortunate misfortune; second, that the Religion itself, once acquired, be diligently preserved in those places where it is kept intact by Divine Providence. Furthermore, by the term “Religion” We do not mean only those truths which We must necessarily hold by faith in order to attain salvation, but also those principles which must be manifested in deeds to demonstrate a way of life and customs in keeping with the Christian Religion and to attain, after the journey of this life, blessed happiness in heaven. 1. Indeed, our predecessor Roman Pontiffs, in fulfilling this duty, chose in every age men distinguished for their piety and learning to spread the Catholic Faith throughout every continent; following their example, in accordance with the limitations of our own strength and the difficulties of the times, we have maintained the same course. Secondly, the Roman Pontiffs always took great care to restore moral discipline and holiness, which had declined in certain dioceses; they did not consider the zeal of the bishop alone, nor his organizational efforts, to be sufficient. In fact, they either sent Apostolic Visitators to those dioceses or employed other remedies that seemed more appropriate. We, too, have followed the same approach whenever complaints from the faithful reached our ears, so that through our zeal we might mitigate the noted negligence before presenting the matter to the Supreme Judge. In fact, We have often appointed Visitors endowed with papal authority to restore the former discipline wherever necessary. We sent many messages to individual bishops—to many or to all—to urge them to be more zealous; We have taken other measures and initiatives, which it is unnecessary to list here. 2. Furthermore, We cannot conceal that We have sought out upright citizens in your cities, but We have found that many were astray, especially in the mountains, far from your dioceses, and leading lives devoid of all virtue. Even if they do not stray from the Faith—as We hope—they nonetheless, through their corrupt morals and the consequences that follow, draw divine wrath upon themselves and hasten toward death without first having shown worthy fruits of penance. 3. This requires, above all, your diligence and Ours, so that in such a grave situation we may not appear idle and sluggish. Therefore, We have long reflected on the appropriate remedies, and first of all We have entrusted Ourselves to God, the Father of light; then We have directed Our prayers to the Blessed Virgin, on whose feast day We have composed this letter. Then We deemed it appropriate to exhort you to carry out with determination, for the good of your dioceses, what We have set before you. 4. During this time, We ourselves, in more modest roles for several years, have served as Promoter of the Faith—a office whose proper function is, after careful examination, to weigh the virtues and merits of those to be numbered among the saints— and during that time, for several years, we were a member of the Secretariat of the Sacred Congregation of the Cardinals Interpreters of the Council of Trent, who, by virtue of their office, strive in every way to root out the corruption that plagues the dioceses. Furthermore, having come to know at that time, through familiar association, many bishops distinguished by their profound learning and zeal for piety, and having also held, prior to the Pontificate, the see of Ancona and having been transferred to the see of Bologna (whose administration we carried out in conjunction with the Papal See), we remained there for over a decade. Having learned from long experience, we have come to understand that to correct the corrupt customs—which are beginning to creep in, are already rampant, or, having become entrenched over time, occupy too large a portion of the dioceses— nothing is more beneficial than imploring the help and strength of others—that is, to send out Sacred Missions everywhere, especially to those areas that are most remote from the city. 5. Missionaries are rightly compared to the Apostle John and his colleagues, who were called from another boat to lend a hand to Peter and Andrew, who were struggling at sea or, because of the abundance of their catch, pull their nets ashore, as we learn from the Gospel of Luke, which Maldonato comments on as follows: “The Shepherds of the Church, when they alone are not sufficient for the office imposed upon them or simply accepted, must call upon others from whom they may receive help.” Jansen had noted the same thing before Maldonato (Evangelical Concordances, chap. 25). 6. When we served as Promoter of the Faith, we examined the virtues and miraculous deeds performed by the servants of God Giovenale Ancina, Bishop of Saluzzo; Cardinal Roberto Bellarmino, Archbishop of Capua; Alexander, first Bishop of Saulo and then of Pavia, whom We solemnly declared blessed; and finally by Saints Vincent de Paul and John Francis Regis, whom Our Predecessors in the Papacy duly inscribed in the register of the Saints. Therefore, reflecting on the outstanding achievements of these eminent men, incredible glory was bestowed upon the first three in their care for souls, above all because they made every effort to ensure that the Sacred Missions were carried out in their dioceses. The two we mentioned last were found to be filled with love for God and for their neighbor; and above all, Saint Vincent de Paul felt such charity that he established the Congregation of the Missionaries, and he himself, as long as his health permitted, carried out these same missions. John Regis, too, publicly demonstrated that he was consumed by the sacred fire of charity, with which he did not hesitate to brave the most rugged and difficult mountains to instruct populations ignorant of Christian doctrine and customs, whenever the bishop or his deputy requested it. 7. Likewise, you are well aware that it is the custom of bishops, at set times, to report to the Sacred Congregation, Interpreter of the Council of Trent, regarding their own churches and to clearly set forth their condition. Therefore, as We ourselves were Secretary, whenever it was reported that Missions had been convened in a diocese by order of the Congregation itself or of the Supreme Pontiffs, we highly commended these decisions in the replies—which were customarily sent to the bishops—and we did not fail to urge them to continue this praiseworthy initiative. Not once were We ordered to rebuke bishops who did not call upon the pious missionaries to revive the languishing piety among the people—as they themselves reported—and to promote discipline among the clergy, and in both cases to curb the readiness to sin coupled with scandal. 8. The history of the deeds of Benedict XIII—who rendered meritorious service to Us, is an ornament to your nation, and served for many years as Archbishop of the Church of Benevento—has been published. Likewise, the life of Cardinal Inigo Caracciolo, who held the see of Aversa as a shining example of virtue, and that of Bishop De Cavalieri, who administered the Church of Troia with great piety and religious zeal, have been published. If you have never seen these books before or should decide to read them in the future, you will immediately understand the great fruits that the missions they organized in their respective dioceses brought to them and to the people entrusted to their care. We, in truth, have read these accounts carefully and have followed with great pleasure the publication of all that those most celebrated men often explained to Us while they were still alive. In fact, Benedict XIII, when he was Pope, always made use of our work. The others whom We have now mentioned did not need to speak with Us even once, and We gave them letters to facilitate their mission. 9. Finally, We ourselves have discovered the usefulness and necessity of the Sacred Missions, whenever We conducted them in the Diocese of Ancona, during the time it was entrusted to Us, and when We personally administered the Church of Bologna. And even now we take great care to ensure that these same Missions are organized by the one who, in accordance with the regulations, succeeded us and followed the guidelines We prescribed. At that time, We saw that what the Jesuit Paolo Segneri—an orator, writer, and man renowned for his Missions—wrote was indeed true: “During the Missions, one may call upon as many worthy preachers as there are who, inspired by pious exercises, are zealous for the sacrament of confession; by their example, they draw others to practice the same virtue. Greater fruit is derived from those Missions if the people attend more frequently; for this reason, the intensity of the fire increases all the more when more coals are gathered in the same place.” 10. Finally, it may be said that this remedy, which aims to correct the vices of the people, is neither new nor uncertain, nor was it invented by Us. It is an ancient remedy, most suitable for curing these ills and perhaps the only one, since so many bishops renowned for their piety have used it with great success in their dioceses. We ourselves have tried it many times, as have you, who have undoubtedly re-educated the people entrusted to you through the Sacred Missions. 11. But so that this remedy may not lack effectiveness, we must pray fervently to God, for “it is not the one who plants who is anything, nor the one who waters, but God who makes it grow” (1 Cor 3:7). Therefore, we must choose missionaries who are eminent in doctrine and who instruct the people with care. Since we rightly suppose—and we write this not without tears and sadness—that many souls among those entrusted to us and to you have fallen into perdition, I will say, as theologians put it, that they were entirely ignorant of the necessary truths due to a lack of the proper means. Missionaries must be called who, after admonishing the people for their sins and scandals, will demonstrate their gravity and malice through their sermons and will be able to rebuke them forcefully. We know this from the testimony of Saint Mark, chapter 3, where Christ chooses the Apostles to send them out to preach (Mk 3:15). The same is inferred from the Acts of the Apostles, chapter 6, where they attest that the preaching of the Word is a special task entrusted to them: “It is not right for us to neglect the Word of God in order to wait on tables” (Acts 6:2). St. Paul teaches the same thing in 1 Corinthians: “Christ did not send me to baptize, but to preach the gospel” (1 Cor 1:17), and in 2 Timothy: “I solemnly charge you before God and Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead: preach the Word; be ready in season and out of season” (2 Tim 4:1–2).” 12. Furthermore, missionaries, through the way they live their lives and by their example, must inspire the people to virtue. “In every situation, set an example by doing good works,” says the Apostle himself to Titus (Titus 2:7). Saint Luke, in the Acts of the Apostles, testifies that Christ the Lord “began to act and teach” (Acts 1:1). Finally, it is necessary that the Missionaries devote themselves completely to God, harboring no desire for vainglory while they attend to instructing the people, nor any hope of gain, however modest. We know, in fact, that the Missions bore great fruit when Saint Charles held the see of Milan. This is also happening now through the work and virtue of the Oblates, whom he founded and who are called “of Saint Ambrose.” In fact, among other things, they have prescribed this: not to cause inconvenience to anyone and not to be moved by any reason to accept anything as a gift, as is clearly stated in the Acts of the Church of Milan (printed in 1599, par. 5, p. 841). Turn your thoughts to Saint John Chrysostom, who in Homily 46 on Matthew says that the whole world was led by the Apostles from error to the truth, from old superstition to embracing the Christian truth—not because they had raised the dead to new life, but because they had freed the soul from every passion and from avarice: “What, in fact, makes them appear great? Their contempt for money and glory, their freedom from all the cares of life; if they had not possessed these things, even if they had raised the dead, not only would they have been of no benefit to anyone, but they would have been judged to be charlatans” (John Chrysostom, Homily 46 on Matthew). 13. The city of Naples is home to a large number of clergymen who are highly regarded for their piety, learning, and experience in missionary work. The congregations of the Archiepiscopal See led by Father Pavone, and those of priests who take their name from Saint Gregory—known as the Pious Workers—are full of such men. Furthermore, there is no shortage of residences for priests who belong to the Congregation of Saint Vincent de Paul. The harvest is plentiful, and there are enough workers, provided they are deployed as required by the needs and welfare of the population. 14. You are to rely on Our Beloved Son, Cardinal Spinello, Archbishop of Naples, under whose responsibility everything is to be carried out; for this reason, We are sending him a Letter in which We ask him to take charge of this area and grant him the authority to appoint, in such an important matter, other assistants or those who will act on his behalf, since we are well aware of the heavy burdens he bears and the sorrows he has faced and continues to face, so that the Lord’s Vineyard may be properly tended. If any of you request the Missions, go to the Cardinal himself, who will designate suitable priests as needed; he will determine their number and set the timeframe so that the Missions may be carried out to completion. Indeed, wise as he is, he will understand that not everything can be done at the same time. 15. The missionaries need extraordinary faculties, which we will gladly grant by drawing from the treasury of the Church, so that they may successfully carry out this most important work. We shall indicate these faculties to Cardinal Spinello, so that the missionaries, by going to him or to other representatives, may easily obtain what is most conducive to the glory of God and the salvation of souls. 16. We understand the difficulties that will prevent the missionaries from traveling to the Samnites and the Calabrians. However, since the Dominican Fathers and the Jesuits are present in those places, their Generals, by Our order, shall convene the Provincials so that they may select some of their own men to carry out the missions there, without any compensation from the clergy or public administrators while they are conducting the missions. Their names shall be communicated to Cardinal Spinello, to whom the bishops of Sannio and Calabria shall turn, so that the missions may be carried out regularly in their dioceses—as we have no doubt will occur in the other dioceses that are not far from the city of Naples. 17. But any such note would seem pointless to us, given the outstanding piety and religious devotion of our most beloved Son in Christ, Charles, King of the Two Sicilies, were we not to urge you to ask the King himself to generously exercise his authority, if necessary, to ensure that the Missions are conducted regularly. In fact, We know from experience—and this is certainly no secret to you—that nothing has ever been proposed to him in the Kingdom that could not, above all, serve the glory of God. 18. We bring this Letter to a close, lest it seem a bit too long, by presenting to you the example of the Holy King Jehoshaphat. The priests, as ministers, went out to meet the victorious King and “taught the people of Judah from the Book of the Law of the Lord; they traveled through all the towns of Judah and instructed the people” (2 Chr 17:9). Nor was the work of the priests enough for the king; he himself went out to the people of Beersheba “as far as Mount Ephraim and called them back to the Lord, the God of their fathers” (2 Chr 19:4), as is recounted in chapter 19. Resolve to imitate him; and do not merely send priests throughout the dioceses, but travel through them yourselves whenever more serious matters permit it. In this way, the people, moved by your presence and your virtue, will be more eager to take up the path of the Lord. If you must endure some inconvenience, you will more easily move God’s heart under these circumstances, so that He may not make you pay for the negligence with which you failed to visit the diocese—remaining at the See—when it was necessary: something we know for certain has happened to some of you. And the measures of Apostolic Providence will not fail to address this evil that has dragged on over time. In the meantime, we will not fail to remember you and your flock whenever we celebrate at the altar. To you and to the people entrusted to your care, we heartily impart the Apostolic Blessing. Given in Rome, at Santa Maria Maggiore, on September 8, 1745, the sixth year of Our Pontificate.
