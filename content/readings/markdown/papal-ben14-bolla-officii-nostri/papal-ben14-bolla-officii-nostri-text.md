BULL OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV OUR OFFICES Bishop Benedict, Servant of the Servants of God. For perpetual remembrance. The duties of our Office not only require us to scrupulously observe and enforce the Laws of the Sacred Canons and the Constitutions of our Roman Pontiff Predecessors—in the interpretation of which no doubt or ambiguity can be found— but they also require Us to take steps to cut off and remove, by issuing appropriate declarations, a burgeoning crop of doubts concerning some of those laws. 1. Indeed, Our Predecessor Pope Gregory XIV, of blessed memory, bearing in mind the providential sanction of Canon Law—by which, precisely, under the title “De homicidio voluntario, vel casuali,” Chapter I, the ancient precept of the Law dictated by God in Exodus had been renewed: “If anyone intentionally and treacherously kills his neighbor, you shall drag him away from my Altar and put him to death,” through his Constitution, issued on May 24, 1591, in the year of the Incarnation of the Lord, which begins: “Cum alias,” he decreed that ecclesiastical immunity was not to be granted at all to those who had treacherously killed their neighbor and had then taken refuge in churches and sacred places. Furthermore, Pope Benedict XIII, our predecessor of venerable memory, who enhanced our prestige, published, in the year of the Incarnation of the Lord 1725, on June 8, a Constitution beginning with “Ex quo divina,” which excluded and denied the benefit of ecclesiastical immunity not only to those who had committed murder treacherously (as mentioned above), but also to those who had killed their neighbor with premeditation. Finally, our recent predecessor, Pope Clement XII, of blessed memory, in his constitution beginning with “In supremo justitiae solio,” published in the year of the Incarnation of the Lord 1734, on January 1, decreed that the benefit of the aforementioned immunity should in no way be enjoyed by those who had committed murder in a brawl, provided that the murder was not accidental or the result of self-defense. 2. Furthermore, the same aforementioned Predecessors, Benedict and Clement, added several other crimes to those already included in Canon Law and the Gregorian Constitution; they established that anyone who committed them would be excluded from the aforementioned right of asylum and from the privilege of taking refuge in churches and sacred and religious places; they resolved, through declarations and definitions, various issues and controversies raised by scholars interpreting the aforementioned law and the Apostolic Constitutions dealing with local immunity, as can be inferred from the spirit of those same Constitutions and also from one of our Institutions that we published while governing the Archdiocese of Bologna (for the instruction of that people entrusted to Our care) and which is printed in the second volume of the Edizioni Italiche, no. 21, while in the Latin edition it bears the number 61. 3. Furthermore, it is established how an Ecclesiastical Curia, in the case of a crime thus contemplated, proceed to apprehend the offender from the place of sanctuary and transfer him to its own prisons, since the aforementioned Predecessor Benedict had established that, regarding the nature of the crime in question and the accused person, there must be a sufficient number of those indications that are usually deemed sufficient to order the apprehension. In order to be legally able to hand over and deliver the prisoner to the ministers and officials of the Secular Curia, Benedict had decreed that the preliminary investigation conducted against the prisoner must reveal evidence which, in accordance with the rules of law, is termed “ultra torturam”; Subsequently, his other predecessor, Clement XII, declared more broadly that whenever it appeared to the ecclesiastical judge—from evidence gathered not through means other than torture but solely through torture—that the crime in question had been committed by the prisoner, he himself could release and hand over the prisoner to the Secular Curia. Moreover, these same Predecessors did not wish for Ecclesiastical Authority to be diminished in any way as a result of the foregoing, nor for any opportunity to be taken to undermine justice. Rather, they established that such criminals could never be arrested in an immune place without the bishop’s authorization and without the intervention of an ecclesiastical official appointed by the bishop himself; and that such prisoners could never be entrusted to or handed over (even when the aforementioned indications are present) to officials of the secular curia, except in accordance with that law (to be observed under penalty of the gravest censures) by which the prisoners themselves must be returned to the Church or to the immune place, until such indications are clarified and refuted in the course of the trial. 4. Since those norms which our aforementioned Predecessor Clement added to the sanctions of common law and of the Gregorian and Benedictine Constitutions, did not extend at all beyond the borders of the temporal domains of the Apostolic See, it seemed appropriate to us to extend them also to other regions whose princes might request it. Therefore, in the Concordats that were concluded both in the year 1741 with our most beloved son in Christ, Charles, illustrious King of the Two Sicilies, and, in the following year 1742, with our other son, equally dear to us in Christ, Charles Emmanuel, the illustrious King of Sardinia, we granted that the aforementioned provisions and the others contained in Clement’s aforementioned letter should be in force within the dominions of those same kings; we subsequently extended and expanded the Concordats to include the domains of other princes who requested them, by publishing a specific letter (an example of which can be found in the first volume of Our Bullarium, Constitut. 88), which begins: “Alias felicis.” 5. Indeed, since all these measures adopted by Our aforementioned Predecessors and by Ourselves were not sufficient to eliminate or completely prevent all the issues that usually arise in the courts—whether concerning the nature of homicides to be considered in relation to prisoners, or regarding the manner of applying the aforementioned Constitutions in the various cases of such homicides and other crimes committed by prisoners, We judged that this further task was reserved to Us: not to tolerate that, in the face of difficulties and doubts, the rules governing intervention in such matters—rules by which it is very often necessary to weigh and carry out the procedural acts of both ecclesiastical and secular judges. Therefore, after having promptly weighed every issue and having heard many of Our Venerable Brother Cardinals of the Holy Roman Church and other authoritative figures and experts in Canon Law and criminal proceedings, by Our decision and by virtue of the full Apostolic Authority We exercise, We have decreed to set forth Our own views and those of Our Predecessors and to restate certain matters as follows. 6. First of all, while the aforementioned Constitution of Our Predecessor Gregory, which begins with “Cum alias,” enumerates those crimes that must be considered excluded from the benefit of ecclesiastical immunity and mentions only lay offenders, and our other predecessor Benedict also spoke only of them in the aforementioned Constitution beginning with “Ex quo Divina,” it pleased our praised predecessor Clement XII to extend and broaden his Constitution beginning “In supremo Justitiae solio,” to clergy of every rank and order who have committed murder with premeditation, so that they, in any case, may not enjoy ecclesiastical immunity; provided, however, that the trial for the murder they committed be recognized by their competent ecclesiastical judge and that the guilty parties—if found guilty before the death penalty was imposed—be punished after due consideration has been given to the prescriptions of the Sacred Canons. 7. Furthermore, since doubt was raised as to whether this expansion and extension by Our Predecessor Clement also includes teachers of Regular Orders and their students, We, to remove all doubt on the matter, have decided and declared that it shall include each and every one of any Order, Congregation, Company, and regular Institute—whether Professed members of such societies or those affiliated in any manner, regardless of rank or status—even if they have been granted some privilege by those Orders or any other express and distinct personal mention regarding themselves or their Orders—shall be subject to the provisions of this and previous Constitutions. Should it ever happen (God forbid) that someone were to commit premeditated murder, that person shall be excluded from the benefit of ecclesiastical immunity, in accordance with the provisions of the Constitutions of Benedict and Clement. 8. And since the aforementioned predecessor Clement, by a special decree published on October 5, 1736, expressly declared that even women who had committed such a crime (to be considered among those excluded from the benefit of local immunity in accordance with the aforementioned provisions) were to be judged on an equal footing with men, and that the provisions of his Letter beginning “In supremo Justitiae solio” must be included absolutely on an equal footing, We, in accordance with the spirit of this Constitution of Ours, approve and confirm the aforementioned declaration and endow it with the full force of an inviolable law, according to which cases that arise must be judged. We likewise decree and confirm, based on the provisions of Clement himself and of this Constitution of Ours, that soldiers, too, are included in all and every provision, and that they may not be favored or granted any military privilege in this matter. 9. Furthermore, since in the aforementioned Constitution of Our Predecessor Clement, all and every person—both laypersons and clergy—who are under investigation, on trial, prosecuted in absentia, or convicted “on account of and in connection with a homicide, even if committed during a brawl, with weapons or with tools suitable by their nature for killing, provided that the homicide was not accidental or due to self-defense,” there have often been discussions as to whether one should be considered excluded from the benefit oflocal immunity should be considered excluded from the benefit of local immunity: one who did not commit murder accidentally or in self-defense during a brawl, but rather with a stick or a stone—which, of course, are not weapons, if they can be defined as such, and in any case are not usually considered weapons by their nature capable of killing, We therefore, in clarifying this doubt, have established that any murderer—whether man or woman, layperson or secular or regular clergy of any Order—who has killed another person even with a stick or a stone, shall in no way be entitled to ecclesiastical asylum, when it is established by the circumstances of the crime that his or her act—even if committed during a brawl, not by accident or out of necessary self-defense—was carried out out of hatred and with the intent and will to cause harm. This definition of Ours is in accordance with Divine Law as found in the Book of Numbers, where, after designating places of refuge for those who have unintentionally shed the blood of their neighbor, it continues as follows: “If anyone strikes another with a sword and the wounded person dies, he is guilty of murder and must himself die. If he throws a stone and the blow kills, he shall be punished in the same way. If anyone struck with a club dies, he shall be avenged by the blood of the striker. The relative of the slain shall kill the murderer; as soon as he is captured, he shall kill him. If a man, out of hatred, strikes another, or throws something at him from ambush, or, as an enemy, strikes him with his hand and kills him, the striker shall be guilty of murder; the relative of the slain man, as soon as he finds the murderer, shall slit his throat. But if, by chance and without hatred or enmity, anyone has committed any of these murders, and this has been proven before the people, and the matter is debated between the guilty party and the blood relative, the innocent person shall be rescued from the avenging hand and, by decree, shall be returned to the city where he had taken refuge, and there he shall remain until the High Priest, who was anointed with the Holy Oil, dies” (Num 35:16–25). 10. It also happens in cases of homicide (which is not the case with other reported crimes) that the person who has been beaten or struck does not die instantly but rather survives for a few hours or a few days. Meanwhile, the assailant, having taken refuge in a church or another place of sanctuary, enjoys the right of asylum—which cannot be revoked on the grounds of murder—as long as the person he struck remains alive; while from that same place of sanctuary, he never ceases to anxiously watch over the wounded person’s life, if he realizes that the victim may live for a long time, he does not leave his refuge under any circumstances; but when he learns that the victim has died as a result of the wound inflicted upon him, he flees, evading the surveillance of the magistrates, and thus successfully secures his own safety and escapes his deserved punishment. Since those entrusted with the administration of justice have informed Us that such incidents occur frequently—and certainly not without serious harm to public peace, given the hope of impunity that troublemakers harbor, convinced that they can escape in the same manner— those same magistrates have asked Us to eliminate such an evil through the appropriate measures of Our foresight. 11. Therefore, on the advice of the aforementioned Brothers of Ours and other wise men, We hereby judge and decree that, when a violent offender takes refuge in a church or other sacred or religious place, if the physicians summoned to examine the wound report “that there is a grave danger to life,” then the violent offender shall be delivered to prison, after having been removed from the sanctuary in accordance with due process; and that, by virtue of this law, he shall be returned to the church if the wounded person survives beyond the time established by law and, furthermore, shall suffer the same penalties to which (in the aforementioned Letters of Benedict and Clement) are imposed on those who refuse to return the offender entrusted to them on the basis of sufficient evidence to justify his torture, after the offender has clarified the evidence in his defense. 12. Indeed, in the aforementioned Constitution of the Predecessor Gregory concerning the authority to request the extradition of offenders from an immune place in the cases provided for, it was established that such authority belongs solely to Bishops and other Prelates superior to Bishops, and not to those inferior to Bishops, even if the others are Ordinaries or belong to no diocese. Thus, should a criminal case occur in a place that is excluded and not subject to any diocese, then such a matter shall be entrusted to the nearest Bishop, as was also confirmed by Our other Predecessor Benedict in his aforementioned Constitution; We, too, approve and confirm this same rule by virtue of the present Letter. But since We have received numerous complaints on this matter—which certain ecclesiastical curias have raised on behalf of officials of the secular curia—and since they have agreed to delay the apprehension of offenders, in the cases in question, for longer than is permissible, We earnestly urge and, with due regard, order that those Venerable Brother Bishops and other superior prelates of the Churches take steps to remove every cause for complaint regarding such matters, and whenever offenders are involved in the cases provided for, that they not refuse to gather the appropriate evidence against them; and if they acknowledge that such evidence, requested in accordance with the foregoing, are indeed sufficient, they should decide as soon as possible to remove the offenders from their place of refuge, so that they may be detained either in episcopal prisons in the name of the Church, or transferred with due precautions to the prisons of the Secular Curia, where they will be confined in the name of the same Church. For one cannot invoke the duty of ecclesiastical zeal to impede the course of justice—as prescribed by the Apostolic Constitutions against troublemakers— but rather to affirm and uphold the immunity of the Church and all other ecclesiastical places, as well as personal rights, when the provisions of sacred laws are breached and violated. However, since We believe that such delays occur especially when the aforementioned proven crimes are committed not in cities but in dioceses, in places remote from the seat of the Ecclesiastical Curia, for this reason, through this very Letter, We grant authority and inform the aforementioned bishops and other senior prelates that whenever they are consulted by the lay Curia regarding cases of this kind, they may entrust to their vicars forane or to other ecclesiastical persons appointed to this function by the superiors themselves, the task of gathering the legally necessary evidence for the arrest, so that, once such evidence has been examined, the Superiors themselves, in accordance with the law, may be able to arrest the offenders as soon as possible. 13. And certainly all these provisions which We have thus far announced, defined, and prescribed—both in this City of Ours, as well as in Bologna, Ferrara, Benevento, and in all other cities, lands, and places directly or indirectly subject to Us and to the Holy Roman Church—and which require special and distinct mention—as well as in the ecclesiastical and secular curias of those places, including baronial ones, and likewise in other kingdoms, provinces, and possessions to which the aforementioned Constitutions of Our Predecessors were extended and expanded—either through special concessions (as stated above) or by means of Concordats— (to the extent that they are appropriately distinguished, but in harmony with those same Concordats, from which We in no way intend to derogate), We decree that they be respected in every case and have decided that they shall have perpetual validity and that they shall take effect and be fully enforced in all individual Dominions, Kingdoms, and Places, both by the Judges of the Ecclesiastical and Secular Courts, by the Magistrates, by the Officials, by the Ministers, and by all those who have and will have jurisdiction pro tempore in this matter. 14. What was prohibited in the aforementioned Constitutions of Our Predecessors, under threat of the most severe penalties—namely, that neither the Secular Courts nor their magistrates, judges, or officials may capture, remove from churches, monasteries, and sacred places, or imprison—even in the cases provided for—a criminal without the intervention of the ecclesiastical authority, or that they dare or presume in any way to declare that the aforementioned persons have committed crimes provided for in the Constitutions of those same Predecessors (whereas this authority, as has been stated, is recognized as belonging solely to the Bishops), We hereby confirm and order, with conviction, authority, and insistence, that they—and all those to whom this duty pertains now and in the future—must always respect these regulations without exception. We have decided and decreed that anyone who acts contrary to this and all transgressors shall incur the very same penalties set forth in the aforementioned Constitutions, considering that among the transgressors must also be included those who, in defiance of ecclesiastical authority and canonical sanctions, neglect the prescribed recourse to ecclesiastical superiors, presume to besiege places of sanctuary, and forbid that food be brought to those who seek protection in a sacred refuge, or otherwise compel them to surrender to the secular authorities. We therefore decree and declare that those who have dared to do so, whether they are fleeing from proven crimes or are under investigation for unproven ones, shall incur all and each of the penalties and censures provided for those who violate Ecclesiastical Immunity in accordance with the law and the penalties announced and prescribed in the aforementioned Apostolic Constitutions. Certainly, we are not unaware that violent actions also occurred frequently in the past, but at the same time we know that the Church has always condemned and prohibited them; and this is sufficiently demonstrated by those Statutes that were compiled at several Provincial Councils toward the end of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries of the Church. For in them are recorded the sieges of churches, the withholding of food, and other measures by which those who had taken refuge in churches were forced to surrender; all who dare to attempt such actions shall be struck down by the sword of anathema. And We, therefore, who cannot and will not abandon the spirit of the Church—so often openly manifested regarding the aforementioned matters and consistently upheld by Our Predecessors—remaining faithful in every respect to that spirit, judge and declare that all and every individual who has dared to use violence in this manner against those under the protection ofecclesiastical immunity, in addition to the other penalties and censures prescribed and established—and to be applied without further declaration against those who violate that same immunity, as has been stated—shall be deprived of and deemed unworthy of every benefit and privilege of ecclesiastical refuge and asylum, both in the churches and places of sanctuary that they have thus violated, and in other churches and sacred and religious places; and they are to be judged in the same manner as all “those who commit violence against refugees or capture them by force and carry them away from the Church or from another place of sanctuary”; our praised Predecessor Benedict XIII, in his aforementioned Constitution, indeed declared that “they may not and must never enjoy not only the immunity of the Church they have violated, but also that of any other Church.” And We have judged that the aforementioned individuals are also to be counted among the violent. 15. Finally, since, as we have learned, a certain custom has become established among the Curias of our Temporal Domain and of not a few neighboring Domains, by which those who have committed crimes of a certain nature in one or the other of these domains and have taken refuge within the borders of the other are captured in the Curia nearest to the domain where the crime was committed and handed over to that same Curia. And when, as has been reported to Us, delays sometimes occur in such transfers because those offenders have sought refuge in the lands to which they fled, under the protection of a church or some other place of sanctuary, then We, wishing to appropriately reconcile the principles of ecclesiastical immunity with the proper administration of justice and the peace of the State, have resolved and ordered that if such fugitives have committed a crime of the kind covered by the aforementioned Apostolic Constitutions and within the temporal dominion of the Roman Church, the evidence (required to proceed with their arrest) be gathered and compiled by the Diocesan Bishop or by the Bishop nearest to the place where the crime was committed. That evidence shall then, without delay, be transmitted to the Bishop of the other place where the offender has found asylum, so that, through his authority and with the intervention of a clerical official, his extradition from the place of asylum may be carried out. The same procedure must be followed if a crime of this nature is committed in the aforementioned domains of other princes, where the custom of the aforementioned surrender is in force: this, whether by virtue of the Gregorian and Benedictine Constitutions, by reason of the Concordat with the Apostolic See, or by extension of another Constitution of Clement XII expressly cited by the Apostolic See. We decree and order that the Bishop, who has jurisdiction over the matter by virtue of the place where the crime was committed, shall ensure that the aforementioned evidence necessary for the arrest is gathered and shall transmit it to the Bishop of the Ecclesiastical State in whose territory the offender choseasylum, so that, by the authority of this Antistite—subject to the principles that must be observed—the offender may be extradited from the place of refuge and, if the law so requires, be handed over to the external Curia that made the request. We wish, however, that in each of the aforementioned cases—which concern only the crimes described above—the laws and procedures of both the Constitutions of Clement and the Concordats be strictly observed, both, of course, when the offender is detained in prisons in the name of the Church, and also when, only the bishops have issued a negative judgment, until all other ecclesiastical judges declare (based on evidence, as mentioned, sufficient to warrant torture) whether the crime in question should be counted among those described or not; and finally, even in the case concerning the obligation to return the offender pursuant to the aforementioned Constitutions, until such time as he, in the course of the trial, has refuted and mitigated the evidence against him. 16. Finally, we decree that this Letter and its contents shall have perpetual validity and full and complete effect in the aforementioned places and domains and, with particular regard, in any place and among any people; that it be respected by all and by each individual to whom it is addressed and to whom it may be addressed in the future, as circumstances may require, under the threat of the prescribed penalties and censures, which transgressors shall immediately incur; and thus, and in no other way, as set forth in the preamble, we decree that it be applied by all Ordinary and Delegated Judges, including the Cardinals of the Holy Roman Church, the Legates a latere, all Congregations of Cardinals, and the Nuncios of the Apostolic See, as well as by the secular Curias, by magistrates, and by anyone else who is or will be vested with any office or power—even if worthy of special note and mention—and by all those who, in any other way, have the power and authority to judge and interpret; and any judgment issued by anyone or any authority—whether knowingly or out of ignorance—that directs otherwise shall be null and void. 17. Notwithstanding the foregoing and all other Apostolic Constitutions, Rules, and Ordinances, as well as every ecclesiastical or secular law, edicts, writings, customs, and traditions—even those of time immemorial; privileges, indulgences, faculties, persons of every sort worthy of special mention and distinguished by whatever and however exalted an office or authority, and even those burdened by whatever most urgent causes there may be, or the Orders of Regulars—including the Mendicant Orders, the Military Orders, and even the Order of St. John of Jerusalem—or Monks, or the Congregations of Regular Clerics, the Societies and Institutes—including the Society of Jesus, and all others existing under any form or denomination, and by any clause or decree—even those similar in purpose and scope of authority—whether granted by the Consistory or in any manner contrary to the foregoing, even if confirmed and renewed on multiple occasions: To all of these and to each one individually—the concepts, form, and purposes of which, even if implicit and reserved, are hereby fully expressed in this Letter —we, from this office, with full knowledge and authority, in accordance with the foregoing, hereby derogate; and we decree that they be derogated from, rejecting any contrary exception. If it has been granted to some, either collectively or individually, by this same See that under no circumstances, or except in certain cases, and for various reasons, they may not be excommunicated, suspended, or interdicted, except by Letters from this same See, they shall make full and substantiated mention of said Indult, word for word. 18. Furthermore, so that this Letter and its contents may be made known to all, and so that no one may dare to claim ignorance of the matter, we decree that the Letter be published at the doors of the Church of St. John Lateran, the Basilica of the Prince of the Apostles in Rome, the Apostolic Chancery, the General Curia of St. Innocent, and in the other customary and habitual places of the City, by means of Our couriers, as is customary; we wish that copies of it be posted, so that, once published and posted in this manner, each and every person—to whom it is addressed and will be addressed in the future—may have access to it, as if it had been personally communicated and notified to each of them; We also decree that copies thereof, including printed ones, provided they are signed in person by a public notary and bear the seal of a person holding ecclesiastical office, shall be accorded the same trust in every place as would be accorded to this Letter were it presented and shown in its original form. 19. Therefore, no one is permitted to violate this page of Our declarations, definitions, ordinances, affirmations, prohibitions, mandates, decrees, and will, or to oppose it through any reckless act. If anyone should dare to do so, he will incur the wrath of Almighty God and of the Blessed Apostles Peter and Paul. Given at Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on March 15, in the year 1749 of the Incarnation of the Lord, the tenth year of Our Pontificate.
