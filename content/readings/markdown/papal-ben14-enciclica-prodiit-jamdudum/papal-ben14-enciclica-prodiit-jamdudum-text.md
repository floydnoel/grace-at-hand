# Prodiit Jamdudum

**La Santa Sede**

---

> *Note: This document was translated from the original Italian into English. Translation provided by DeepL (July 2026).*

ENCYCLICAL PRODIIT JAMDUDUM OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Archbishops and Bishops of the Papal States. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brother, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. On January 1, 1748, We sent an Encyclical Letter to all the Archbishops and Bishops of Our Papal States to provide the necessary remedy for three most serious problems that unfortunately occurred during the Carnival season. The first was that on the last night of Carnival, the vigils, dances, and games went on so late that they encroached upon the beginning of the first day of Lent. Thus, it sometimes happened that, after the dance, games, and vigils, people would leave to go directly—though without masks on their faces and yet still wearing the costumes in which they had been disguised—to church to attend Mass, receive Ash Wednesday ashes, and then return home to sleep in their own beds at least throughout the morning of the first day of Lent. The second drawback was that in some parts of the Papal States, the wearing of masks was permitted even on Fridays and other holy days of obligation. The third drawback was that in some parts of the same State, even after lunch—during the hours of Vespers and Christian Instruction—acrobats were permitted to perform their acts in the squares and public streets. We indicated the necessary remedies for the aforementioned scandals in the aforementioned Letter (No. XLV in Volume 2 of Our Bullarium), and what was ordered therein is today fully confirmed by Us, entrusting its observance to the Cardinal Legates, the Governors of the State, and every Bishop in his own diocese. 1. However, this is not the only reason why We deemed it necessary to send this Encyclical to Your Fraternities. There is another reason pertaining to the current year, namely the Carnival of 1751, the last day of which coincides with the Vigil and Fast of Saint Matthias the Apostle. So that every Bishop of Our State may be fully informed, we shall add the following, reporting on what has transpired subsequently in this regard. 2. Before the current year 1751 began, from various places, including the most distant regions of Italy, we received letters from various bishops and prelates who—considering that this year the vigil of Saint Matthias coincided with the fast on the last day of Carnival—after explaining that scandals resulting from the violation of the fast would be inevitable, public, and perhaps even widespread, since, unfortunately, the revelry of Carnival knows no bounds, especially on the last day and the night of the last day of Carnival—they proposed a corrective measure: namely, to move the vigil of Saint Matthias this year, bringing it forward to the preceding Saturday, the last day of the week of Sexagesima. They supported this suggestion with the opinion of certain authors, who teach that the Bishop, by virtue of his ordinary authority in the diocese, when a grave and legitimate cause exists, may transfer fasts and even holy days of obligation from one day to another; individually, by virtue of his episcopal authority, out of fear of scandal and public violation of the precept of fasting—since the vigil of St. Matthias falls on the last day of Carnival—he may transfer it to the preceding Saturday. 3. We did not believe we should disregard the aforementioned request or ignore it, allowing the situation to run its course unchecked without taking remedial action. We, being fully convinced—based on the reports we have received of Carnival excesses—that if we left things as they are and did not take steps to moderate them, the fast prescribed for the eve of Saint Matthias would be neglected, and perhaps even publicly so; considering that if we were to punish the offenders, the punishment would fall only on a few wretched individuals, while the powerful would remain immune—either because there would be a way to ensure their transgressions did not reach the ears of their superiors, or because, even if the facts were brought to their attention, the prudence of the flesh would not prompt them to take measures regarding such matters and thereby spoil the good relations so necessary for good governance with the powerful figures of the secular world, we responded as follows. 4. First, there is a well-founded—not unfounded—fear that the fast—which this year falls on the eve of Saint Matthias, the last day of Carnival—will be overlooked and neglected. 5. Second, it is not unusual that, when a legitimate reason arises, the vigil and the fast are moved to some other preceding day. This is evident in the case of the vigil of the feast of St. John the Baptist: since that vigil falls on the Thursday of Corpus Christi, the fast is moved forward to the preceding Wednesday, in accordance with Constitution 232 \*Cum evenire\* of Pope Urban VIII, without any mention of the vigil being made on that Wednesday in the Office or in the Mass, in accordance with the decree of the Congregation of Rites approved by the blessed memory of Clement XI, which is the ninth in his \*Bollario\* among the decrees of that Congregation. This is also the case for the solemn feast of some principal patron saint of the city or diocese; in fact, if a fast falls on the day of the feast, it is brought forward to the preceding day, as Cardinal Gaetano rightly observes in his \*Summa\* (entry “Jejunium. Quoad tertium, De rationabili”), as do Sanchez (Book 5, Consilior, Chapter I, Doubt 5, No. 27) and Leandro (Oper. Moral., p. 3, treat. 5, dis. 10, quest. 39). 6. We have not addressed the case in which, if a solemn feast falls on a Monday and that feast is simultaneously a vigil and a fast, the fast is transferred to the preceding Saturday, according to the text of the Decretal in the chapter “Ex parte,” \*De observantia jejuniorum\*; for We know that, for other, more weighty reasons, and to counter the heresy of the Manicheans—who, as enemies of the true Resurrection of Christ, fasted on Sundays in defiance of it—the Catholic Church forbade its followers from fasting on Sundays, as Silvio rightly observes (2.2., D. Thomae quaest. 147, art. 5, quaest. 8). 7. But, considering the other cases mentioned above, we reflected, thirdly, that if the fast was brought forward so that the feast could be celebrated with appropriate joy, free from the sadness of fasting, all the more reason could there be to bring the fast forward so as not to expose it to the danger of scandalous, public, and almost universal irreverence; all the more so since, upon examining the matter closely, it should not seem unreasonable to maintain that the same reasoning applies almost equally to the aforementioned cases and to ours; for perhaps in those cases as well the fast was brought forward, not so much to exclude the sadness from the solemnity as out of fear that said fast might not be observed on a day of public rejoicing. 8. To the three points mentioned was added a fourth, which consisted in determining whether—since the fast of Saint Matthias must be brought forward to the Saturday of Sexagesima—this could be done by the bishops in their dioceses, by virtue of their ordinary authority, without any need for Our Pontifical authority. We reflected that the authors held two opinions. One view grants full authority to the ordinary authority of the bishop in his diocese, provided there is a grave and legitimate cause, considering as a grave and legitimate cause the evident danger of violating the fast, which would unfortunately occur on the last day of Carnival if fasting were required on that day. This is the opinion of Gobat in his \*Sacramental Theology\* (Treatise 5, Case 3, Section 2, in Appendix No. III, et seq.). La Croix (Vol. I, Book 3, Part 2, No. 1273) repeats and confirms this in Vol. 2, Book 6, No. 2083, when he adds that, although in 1694 the Congregation of Rites decreed that fasting should not be brought forward to the last Saturday of Carnival when the vigil of St. Matthias falls on the last day of said Carnival (as can be seen in Decree 92 included in the Bullarium of Innocent XII), that decree is also quoted in full by the Salmaticensi in their \*Corso Morale\* (Volume 5, Treatise 23, Chapter 2, Point 5, n. 93); however, this must take place when the Bishop deems it expedient for the good of souls to bring forward the fast, but he must never order it except for just reasons. 9. The other opinion, opposed to bringing the fast forward, maintains that the bishop cannot order it in his diocese even if the eve of St. Matthias falls on the last day of Carnival. Thus, Teofilo Raynaud writes with great force (Heteroclit. Spiritual., vol. 15, p. 273, n. 34); Merati (Ad Gavantum, vol. 2, part I, sect. 7, chap. 4, n. 23, § Si Vigilia); Cavalerio (Ad Decreta Congregationis Sacrorum Rituum, Part 2, Vol. 1, p. 134); Diana (In Edit. coord., Vol. 3, Treat. 3, Quest. 88, n. 3); Leandro (Part 3, Treatise 5, Provision 10, Question 42); Monacello (In Formula Legal., Part 2, Title 16, Formula 4, n. 12); Ferraris in the \*Bibliotheca Canonica\* (vol. 4, under the entry “Jejunium,” n. 26). 10. It cannot be denied that this second opinion is the most common and also the most reasonable and best-founded, since it would not be very difficult to adequately refute the arguments put forward by the aforementioned authors to support their claim if the vigil with fasting for the feast of Saint Matthias had been prescribed by Innocent III in the chapter \*Ex parte,\* \*De observantia jejuniorum\*, which settled the controversy raised by certain individuals who argued that the vigil and fast in honor of Saint Matthias should not be observed, on the grounds that although he had been an Apostle, he had not been called directly by the voice of Christ. Whether, before Innocent III, Pope Alexander III—when asked by those who, even at that time, observed the vigil with fasting for the feast of Saint Matthias how they should proceed in a leap year—replied that the vigil should always be celebrated on the day preceding the feast, except in the case where it fell on a Sunday: “The feast of St. Matthias, according to the custom of the Churches, is always preceded by the vigil, so that neither in a leap year nor in any other way may another day be interposed between the vigil and the solemnity; in which case, unless the Vigil falls on a Sunday, fasting is to be observed,” as stated in the chapter “Quaesivit,” paragraph “Festum,” \*De verborum significatione\*, a Decretal of the same Pontiff, also cited in the Appendix to the Third Lateran Council (chap. 22, p. 1718, vol. 10 \*De’ Concilj\* of the Labbean collection), and likewise in Martene’s \*De Antiquis Ecclesiae Ritibus\* (vol. 3, book 4, chap. 31, no. 23). It is easily understood that the Ordinary authority cannot transfer it to the Saturday preceding the vigil of St. Matthias, even if it falls on the last day of Carnival, since the Ordinary authority cannot derogate from or make provisions regarding matters established by the Roman Pontiffs and by Canon Law, as We have demonstrated in Our treatise \*De Synodo Dioecesana\* (Book 7, Chapter 30). 11. Thus have we replied to the bishops and prelates who asked us whether they could move the vigil of Saint Matthias to the preceding Saturday; we added, however, that since Our pontifical authority extends to a degree that ordinary authority does not, We were happy to grant them permission this year to bring forward the vigil of Saint Matthias with fasting, having it celebrated on the Saturday of Sexagesima, thereby separating it, for this occasion, from the day immediately preceding the feast of Saint Matthias; since we were, unfortunately, convinced that, had it been held on the last day of Carnival, a violation of the Ecclesiastical Precept would have been almost inevitable, and since we are also bound to assist Our Brothers in keeping offenses against God out of their dioceses. 12. It is well known to all that, ever since the time of the Apostles, those wholesome gatherings called Agape—so well described by Tertullian in his \*Apologeticus\*, chapter 39—have been held in the churches. It is also well known that those who refrained from attending them were excluded from the communion of the faithful. For this reason, the conduct of the Eustathian heretics was also condemned at the Synod of Gangra, canon II. It is also well known that, due to the disturbances that began to arise during the Agape feasts, they were prohibited by the Council of Laodicea, canon 28, and by the Third Council of Carthage, canon 30. Anyone who reflects with due attention on the aforementioned course of ecclesiastical discipline—which, in the wake of the disturbances that occurred, had no hesitation in abolishing an ancient apostolic custom—should not be at all surprised if, in the face of irreparable disturbances, the day of fasting is changed, not permanently, but only this once, though the obligation to observe it on the substitute day remains. We could have cited many, many other examples of even more significant exceptions to ecclesiastical precepts found in the annals of the Church. But we have preferred the example of the Agapi, as more suitable and appropriate to our case, since in both instances the issue at hand is the vice of gluttony. 13. In responding to the aforementioned bishops and prelates, we also instructed them to exhort secular and religious clergy not to take advantage of the early observance of the fast, but to fast on the actual eve of St. Matthias, that is, on the last day of Carnival, since they should not be considered to be immersed in the excesses of Carnival as laypeople generally are. 14. This is what We have replied to those who have appealed to Us to learn how they should act this year on the last day of Carnival, which coincides with the vigil of Saint Matthias. Now, by this Letter of Ours, We notify the Archbishops and Bishops of Our temporal State of all this, so that if any of them should fear that the precept of fasting might be violated in their city or diocese on the last day of Carnival, they may avail themselves of the remedy indicated, in the manner and form set forth above, granting them full authority in this regard. Furthermore, if they have no such concern, they should allow matters to take their natural course. As We commend Ourselves, Venerable Brother, to your prayers, We bestow upon you and the flock entrusted to your care the Apostolic Blessing. Given in Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on January 30, 1751, the eleventh year of Our Pontificate.
