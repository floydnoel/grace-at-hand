## Summary

*Prodiit Jamdudum* (1751) is an encyclical of Pope Benedict XIV addressing a pastoral and disciplinary complication: the last day of Carnival that year coincided with the Vigil and fast of Saint Matthias the Apostle. Fearing that the obligatory fast would be universally disregarded amid the revelry, Benedict—after consulting bishops and weighing canonistic opinion—grants local ordinaries in the Papal States authority to transfer the fast of St. Matthias forward to the Saturday of Sexagesima week, while urging clergy to observe it on the proper day. The document also reaffirms an earlier 1748 encyclical that regulated Carnival abuses (late-night vigils encroaching on Ash Wednesday, mask-wearing on Fridays and holy days, and public acrobatics during Vespers).

## Historical Context

The encyclical was issued on 30 January 1751, during the pontificate of Benedict XIV (1740–1758), a period of vigorous administrative and liturgical reform. The 18th-century Carnival in Italy was notorious for excess—elaborate masking, all-night dancing, and public entertainments that frequently bled into Lent. The specific trigger was the rare calendar coincidence of Carnival Tuesday falling on 23 February, the vigil of St. Matthias. Earlier, in 1748, Benedict had already tried to curb the worst abuses; *Prodiit Jamdudum* extends that regulatory impulse to the fasting discipline itself. The document also internalises a live debate among canonists: whether a diocesan bishop could, on his own authority, move a universal fast to prevent public scandal. Benedict sides with the stricter view (that ordinary authority cannot override a papal or conciliar prescription) but exercises his own supreme authority to grant a temporary dispensation.

## Key Themes

- **Pastoral prudence over rigid rubricism** — Benedict acknowledges that a fast certain to be ignored does more harm than good; transferring it preserves the substance of the precept while avoiding scandal.
- **Hierarchical reservation of power** — The Pope insists that bishops lack ordinary authority to move a fast established by canon law, but he supplies that authority by papal indult for this one occasion.
- **Prevention of scandal** — The central rationale is the *probable* public violation of a Church precept; the encyclical is a case study in how far a pope may go to avoid contempt for ecclesiastical discipline.
- **Continuity with earlier reform** — The encyclical opens by confirming a 1748 letter that already addressed Carnival’s encroachment on Lent, showing Benedict’s consistent attention to liturgical order.
- **Distinction between clergy and laity** — Clergy and religious are exhorted to keep the fast on the proper day regardless, implying they are not presumed to share in Carnival’s excesses.

## Significance

*Prodiit Jamdudum* is a compact example of the 18th-century papacy’s practical, casuistic style of governance: it does not announce new doctrine but solves a concrete disciplinary problem by balancing canon law, episcopal consultation, and papal discretion. Its treatment of the bishop’s inability to transfer a universal fast—and the Pope’s willingness to do so by special indult—illustrates the post-Tridentine understanding of the diocesan ordinary’s subordinate relationship to the Holy See. The document is also of interest to liturgical historians for its detailed citation of earlier decrees (Urban VIII’s *Cum evenire*, decrees of the Congregation of Rites under Clement XI and Innocent XII) and for its window into the pre-modern Church’s struggle to maintain fasting discipline against the social pressure of Carnival.
