## Summary

*Saeculo Exeunte Octavo* is an encyclical by Pope Pius XII, issued June 13, 1940, to commemorate the eighth centenary of Portugal's independence. While ostensibly a celebration of Portuguese nationhood, the letter is primarily a missionary exhortation — Pius XII uses the anniversary to call the Portuguese Church and faithful to renew their zeal for evangelization, especially in Portugal's African, Asian, and American colonies, where vast populations remained unreached by the Gospel.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was published in the second year of Pius XII's pontificate, just months after the outbreak of World War II. Europe was in crisis, and Pius notes that "more than a few European nations have been lost to the Church" amid the upheaval. Against this dark backdrop, Portugal — under the authoritarian *Estado Novo* regime of António de Oliveira Salazar — stood as a stable, traditionally Catholic power with an extensive colonial empire. The letter capitalizes on the patriotic energy of the centenary celebrations to channel it toward the Church's missionary project, urging Portugal to reclaim the role it played during the Age of Discovery as a bearer of Christian civilization.

## Key Themes

- **Missionary vocation of Portugal** — The encyclical traces Portugal's national identity to its origins in the Crusades and its providential role in spreading the faith overseas, citing Camões, Vasco da Gama, and St. Francis Xavier as exemplars.
- **The primacy of evangelization** — Portugal's colonial enterprise is framed as having been, at its best, ordered toward the propagation of the faith; missionary zeal and national greatness rise and fall together.
- **Urgency of the harvest** — Pius repeatedly invokes the vast number of souls still awaiting the Gospel in Portuguese territories, calling the shortage of laborers a crisis.
- **Clerical and lay responsibility** — The pope exhorts priests to join the Missionary Union of the Clergy, calls for mission formation in seminaries, encourages the faithful to pray, give alms, and offer their sons for the missions.
- **Indigenous clergy** — Following the model of Goa, Pius urges the development of native-born priests and religious sisters in each territory, rather than relying solely on European missionaries.
- **Holiness of missionaries** — Missionaries must be men of God formed in solid doctrine and virtue, not merely enthusiasts; their example matters more than their words among non-Christian peoples.
- **Marian intercession** — The Rosary and the Blessed Virgin (especially under the title of Fatima, where Mary appeared in Portugal in 1917) are commended as sources of protection and vocational inspiration.

## Significance

The encyclical stands as one of the great missionary documents of the mid-twentieth century, continuing the vision of Benedict XV's *Maximum Illud* (1919) and Pius XI's *Rerum Ecclesiae* (1926). It is notable for explicitly linking national identity and colonial responsibility with the Church's universal mission, and for its insistence on developing indigenous clergy decades before the Second Vatican Council would make *indigenization* a central theme. While its language reflects a pre-conciliar understanding of mission as the extension of Christendom, its core pastoral concerns — the shortage of evangelizers, the need for inculturation, and the primacy of spiritual over temporal motives — remain enduring.
