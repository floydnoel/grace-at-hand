## Summary

*Quas Primas* ("In the first") is Pope Pius XI's 1925 encyclical instituting the Feast of Christ the King. It argues that Christ's kingship is not merely metaphorical but a real, threefold authority — legislative, judicial, and executive — over all individuals, families, and nations. The document calls for the annual liturgical celebration of this feast to combat secularism and remind the world that Christ must reign in public life, not only in private devotion.

## Historical Context

Written at the close of the 1925 Holy Year (a jubilee year, coinciding with the 1600th anniversary of the Council of Nicaea), the encyclical responds to rising secularism, anti-clericalism, and nationalism in post-World War I Europe. Pius XI saw society as having "thrust Jesus Christ and his holy law out of their lives," leading to political instability, class conflict, and a weakening of moral authority. The feast was originally set for the last Sunday of October (moved to Christ the King Sunday after Vatican II).

## Key Themes

- **Real kingship of Christ** — Christ is King by both natural right (hypostatic union) and acquired right (Redemption)
- **Threefold power** — Christ holds legislative, judicial, and executive authority over all humanity
- **Social reign** — Christ's kingship extends beyond individuals to families, states, and the whole social order; civil authorities must govern in his name
- **Spiritual kingdom** — Christ's kingdom is not of this world but has authority over worldly affairs; it is entered through faith and baptism
- **Remedy for secularism** — The feast is a direct response to anti-clericalism and the exclusion of religion from public life
- **Liturgical catechesis** — Annual feasts teach the faithful more effectively than doctrinal pronouncements alone

## Significance

*Quas Primas* shaped 20th-century Catholic social teaching by asserting Christ's sovereignty over the temporal order, a theme echoed in subsequent papal documents. The Feast of Christ the King became a permanent fixture of the Roman Rite (moved in 1969 to the last Sunday of Ordinary Time, concluding the liturgical year). The encyclical remains a key reference point for Catholic discussions of the relationship between Church and state, the social kingship of Christ, and the Church's critique of secular modernity.
