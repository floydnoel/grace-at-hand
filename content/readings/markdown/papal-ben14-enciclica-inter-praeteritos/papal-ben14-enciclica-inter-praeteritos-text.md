ENCYCLICAL INTER PRAETERITOS OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV

To the Penitentiaries and Confessors appointed in Rome for the Holy Year.

Pope Benedict XIV. Beloved Son, greetings and Apostolic Blessings.

1\. Among the efforts we have made—and continue to make—to ensure that the upcoming Holy Year is celebrated properly and that the faithful may reap the spiritual fruits of the Holy Indulgence, one of the most significant has certainly been reading books that deal with the Holy Year Jubilee. And there are quite a few of them! In reading them, we have observed that some address and resolve controversies by relying solely on what their predecessors have written, and by resolving the issues according to what seems convenient to them, and in line with their own understanding, without paying any heed whatsoever to the Constitutions of the Supreme Pontiffs, in which the Holy Year Jubilee is proclaimed to the faithful, and in which certain passages are included that clarify the issues that had arisen previously. There are also other authors who carefully consider the words of the Papal Bulls, taking care to weigh them thoroughly, and subsequently regard as overcome and removed the difficulties raised by those who either wrote before the Constitutions—in which the appropriate words are included—were issued, by virtue of which the aforementioned difficulties are resolved, or who, if they wrote afterward, did so without giving due consideration to the wording of the Apostolic Constitutions.

The coming Holy Year—if it pleases the Almighty God to grant us life until that time—will be the third Holy Year that We have seen celebrated in Rome: and We well remember the disputes and questions that were hotly debated among the Confessors of Rome, and later among the Confessors outside Rome, when, once the celebration of the Holy Year had ended here, the Jubilee was extended to places outside Rome. We have endeavored, in the Constitutions we have drafted concerning the Holy Year, to include those words which—regarded as highly valuable not only by Us but also by the Doctors who wrote before Us—eliminate the difficulties previously raised.

We have also made some statements regarding certain issues that remained undecided and that could not be considered resolved solely by the wording included in the Constitutions. But we do not presume to have dispelled all doubts that may arise in the minds of men with an overly subtle and metaphysical disposition—a quality that, while useful for speculative matters, is equally unhelpful for moral matters. We hope, however, to have made some progress, which may perhaps be pursued more extensively by others less occupied than we are.

And so that what we have done and thought may be of use to others—and particularly to confessors and spiritual directors—we address to you, our beloved sons, this Encyclical Letter of ours, thereby fulfilling the promise We made in Our Consistorial Address delivered at the Consistory held on May 5 of this year, 1749. And so that any confusion may be avoided and matters may proceed in the proper order, we will deal separately with each of the Constitutions concerning the Holy Year that we have published to date.

Part I

(From the Apostolic Constitution, beginning with “Peregrinantes,” proclaiming the Universal Jubilee of the Holy Year, published on May 15, the Feast of the Ascension of the Lord, 1749)

2\. Our Constitution contains the following words: “Vere pœnitentibus, et confessis, sacraque Comunione refectis.”

We were the first to combine Confession with Communion, counting it among the works required and prescribed for obtaining the Indulgences of the Holy Year. We explained the reasons for this addition in our Consistorial Address delivered at the Consistory held on May 5, 1749. We do not believe that anyone could raise the question of whether the term “Holy Communion” refers to Sacramental Communion or Spiritual Communion; for the Sacred Council of Trent, Session 13, Chapter 8, mentions three kinds of Communion: namely, Sacramental Communion alone, which is that of those who receive it while in a state of mortal sin; Spiritual Communion alone, which is that of those who ardently desire to partake of the Eucharistic Bread with living faith, “quae per dilectionem operatur,” and who experience its fruit and benefit; and finally, both sacramental and spiritual Communion, which is for those who “first prepare and instruct themselves so that, clothed in the wedding garment, they may approach the Divine Table.” By the term “Holy Communion,” we have understood and continue to understand both sacramental and spiritual Communion; this is the obvious meaning of the words, and we have never believed that it could occur to anyone that the term “Holy Communion” might be understood to refer solely to the sacramental form; as was also rightly pointed out by Father Passerino (De indulgentiis, questions 43 and 44), even though he wrote after the condemnation of the proposition issued by Alexander VII on September 7, 1665. “Whoever voluntarily makes no Confession satisfies the precept of the Church,” but he wrote before the condemnation of the proposition made by the Venerable Servant of God Innocent XI on March 2, 1679: “One satisfies the precept of annual Communion by sacrilegiously partaking of the Lord.”

3\. In the famous Constitution of Boniface VIII, which begins “Antiquorum” under the title “de poenitentiis et remissionibus,” in the Common Extravagances, where he proclaims the Jubilee of the Holy Year, the following words are found: “to those who are truly penitent and have confessed, or who will truly repent and confess,” and in Clement VI’s Extravaganza \*Unigenitus\*, in the same book and under the same title, the following words are found: “to those who are truly penitent and have confessed.” These words have at times given rise to serious controversy over whether Confession “in voto” is sufficient to obtain the Indulgence, or whether Confession “in re” is required; which is the same as asking whether, to obtain thethe Indulgence, it is necessary to actually confess one’s sins to a priest, or whether it is sufficient to repent of them through a true act of contrition, with the intention of confessing them, while postponing Confession to another time—and especially to Easter, at which time every faithful is obliged to receive Holy Communion.

4\. The issue is raised but not resolved by Cardinal de Lugo (“de poenit.”, disp. 27, sect. 6, n. 64); We find it formally discussed. Ludovico Bolognino, a renowned canonist, in his treatise \*De Indulgentiis\*, tends to hold that the act of contrition with the aforementioned intention is sufficient. Felice Sandeo, also renowned for his writings on canon law, in his treatise \*De indulgentia plenaria\*, leans toward the same opinion; but he states that the opposite view is more certain. The works of Bolognino and Felino are printed in the treatises commonly known as the \*Magni\* (Volume 14, published in Venice in 1584, p. 145 verso, and p. 158 verso). The distinguished theologian Silvestro, in his \*Somma\* “in verb. Indulgentia n. 20,” after showing a preference for the practice of the Confession of Vows, recommends the current Confession for obtaining indulgences; and the Venerable Cardinal Bellarmine holds the same view (Volume 2, Controversiarum, Book 1. “de Indulgentiis,” chap. 13, Tertia quaestio). Santarelli (Variar. resolut. question 76, from n. 7 through n. 23), very carefully compiles the authors who support both opinions, and requires a current confession to obtain the indulgence of the Holy Year. And to the authors he has listed, we will add Gio. Battista Pauliani (Book 3, Chapter 2, p. 147 ff.), who wrote on the Holy Year Jubilee proclaimed by Julius III and who maintains that a current confession is required in accordance with the words “vere poenitentibus et confessis.”

5\. The renowned Navarro, who had once held the view that a vow of confession was sufficient, moved by the authority of Cardinal Gaetano in his treatise on the Jubilee (Notabil 18, no. 1 et seq. and no. 6), abandoned that view and became an unwavering advocate of the opinion that requires an actual Confession, citing the great benefit he knew had resulted from the practice of the opinion requiring an actual Confession. Father Viva (de Jubilaeo, quaest. 8, art. 3), who wrote on the occasion of the Holy Year Jubilee proclaimed by Innocent XII, and Father Costantini in his Treatise on the Holy Year (§ 2, art. 5), published by Benedict XIII, consider that the words “vere poenitentibus” include contrite sinners, but that by adding the words “et confessis” to “vere poenitentibus,” the latter prescribe the current Confession.

6\. It can be said that this opinion has become the position of the Holy See: for when the question was raised in the Congregation for Indulgences as to whether, since missionaries residing in the lands of the infidels lawfully celebrate Holy Mass—provided they have made an act of contrition for some sin committed— since there is no confessor with whom they can confess, and since the faithful must not be left without Mass on a feast day, they should be considered capable of obtaining the indulgences granted “vere poenitentibus et confessis” by making an act of contrition without first going to confession, the said Congregation replied that they were not eligible, but that the Supreme Pontiff should be asked to make them eligible by a new grace, so that, being contrite but not having confessed—in the absence of a confessor—they might remain entitled to obtain the said indulgences. And our predecessor Clement XII approved the resolution and granted the grace. The documents are cited in the work of Father Theodore, Consultor of the said Congregation of Indulgences (de Indulg., part 1, chap. 11, art. 5, at the end).

7\. By the expression “vere poenitentibus et confessis,” we have understood and will always understand the present Confession, which we count among the works required to be performed in order to obtain Indulgences. It might have been sufficient simply to state that this is how we have understood and continue to understand it; but we deemed it appropriate to explain our reasoning so that the foundations upon which our view rests may be recognized.

8\. The Constitution \*Peregrinantes\* continues: after prescribing a visit to the four basilicas—St. Peter’s, St. Paul’s, St. John Lateran, and St. Mary Major—it adds that this visit must be made “at least once a day, for thirty consecutive or intermittent days, whether natural or ecclesiastical days, namely, to be counted from the first vespers of one day until the full twilight of the following day, if they are Romans or residents of the City; but if they are pilgrims or otherwise outsiders, for at least fifteen such days”: with these words, properly understood—and as We understand them—two issues previously mentioned are thereby resolved.

9\. First, since, according to the Constitution, the four basilicas may be visited on alternate days—for example, one might visit the four basilicas on January 10, and another visit to the four basilicas on the 15th, so too can one visit, for example, two basilicas on one day and two other basilicas on another day, until the total of thirty visits—or, respectively, fifteen visits to each basilica—is completed.

10\. The words “saltem semel in die” can still be found in the aforementioned Decretal of Boniface VIII, “Antiquorum”: and the commentator, in explaining these words, attests that the said Pope declared in the Consistory that visits to the basilicas could be made in the manner just mentioned, that is, one basilica on one day and another on another, provided that the number of visits to each basilica be one-thirtieth for Romans and one-fifteenth for foreigners. If the fact reported by the commentator is true, we believe it must be stated that Pope Boniface did not declare that this was the intended meaning of the words “saltem semel in die,” but that he granted, by special grace, that—notwithstanding the contrary provision of his Decretal—visits to the churches could be made in the manner indicated. And in fact, those who have written in subsequent times about the Jubilee Bulls of the Holy Year—in which the words “saltem semel in die” are always found— interpreting “in die” as singular rather than “in diebus” as plural, have stated that the visit to the four churches must be made in a single day. See Navarro (De Jubilaeo, et Indulgentiis notab., 32 n. 41) and Benzonio (De anno Jubilaei, book 5, question 5). Similarly, Santarelli (Variar. Resolut. Quaest. 75, n. 1 et seq.), Lavorio (De Jubilaeo, et indulgentiis, part. 1, chap. 17, n. 5); Quarti (Treatise on the Jubilee, point 2, question 1); Passerino (On Indulgences, question 49, n. 363); Pasqualigo (De Jubilaeo, quaest. 45); Ricci (Treatise on the Jubilee, chap. 117), who further added that, when the Supreme Pontiffs visit the Basilicas during the Holy Year, all four are visited in a single day.

11\. Nevertheless, on the occasion of the Holy Year of 1700, proclaimed by Pope Innocent XII, it was deemed appropriate to submit the aforementioned point to a new examination by a special Congregation composed of various cardinals and prelates, namely, whether the visit to the four churches should take place in a single day or could be spread over several days—for example, visiting two churches on one day and two on another; and it was unanimously resolved that they must all be visited in a single day. This was also decreed in a Notification published by Cardinal Gasparo di Carpegna, who was Vicar of Rome at that time. The names of those who participated in the Congregation and its resolution are recorded by Gio. Battista Riganti in the “Regole della Cancellaria” (Vol. 4, ad regulam 53, ann. 22, to the end); the Declaration is found in print in Father Viva’s \*De Indulgentiis\* (p. 668, ed. Patavin., year 1715), and is also cited by Father Vanranst, \*Opuscul. de Indulgentiis, et Jubilaeo\* (question 7, no. 10). We, adhering to the obvious meaning of the words “semel saltem in die,” to the common opinion of the Doctors, and to the previous resolutions, declare in our Constitution \*Peregrinantes\* that we understood the visit to the four churches to be required in a single day.

12\. The second point of contention concerns the definition of the day on which, as mentioned, the visit to the four churches must take place. Some have believed that the day should be measured from midnight on the preceding day to midnight on the following day, for example, from midnight on Thursday until midnight on the following Friday. Others, however, have believed that the day should be measured from Vespers on the preceding day to Vespers on the following day, for example, from Vespers on Thursday until Vespers on the following Friday; which is the same as saying until the beginning of the night, that is, at midnight on Friday, since Compline marks the completion of the day’s prayers.

13\. This controversy was examined during the Holy Year proclaimed by Pope Clement X and was not resolved, as Febei attests (De anno Jubilaei, § 2, chap. 15, p. 241). It was subjected to further examination during the Holy Year proclaimed by Innocent XII. Riganti, in the cited passage, subscribed to the view that the day should be counted from First Vespers until Vespers of the following day. He did not fail to present very sound arguments for his position, noting quite aptly that the Holy Doors are opened on the Vigil of the Nativity of Our Lord before Vespers, and are closed on the following Vigil of the Nativity of the Lord after First Vespers, that is, near sunset. These reasons were highly regarded and embraced by some who participated in the Congregation, as well as by others whose opinions were sought by the Congregation itself. Others, however, both within the Congregation and among those consulted by it, held the view that the day should be measured from one midnight to the next. This opinion prevailed, as can be seen in Riganti’s work. In the aforementioned Notification by Cardinal di Carpegna, Vicar, it was declared that the day is to be understood as beginning and ending from one midnight to the next. But We, so that the faithful may more easily fulfill their visit to the four Basilicas during the upcoming Holy Year, have, in our Constitution, put an end to all dispute by declaring that the visit to the four Basilicas may be made in either of two ways: that is, either from one midnight to the next, or from Vespers on the preceding day until the evening twilight of the following day.

14\. Finally, the aforementioned Constitution \*Peregrinantes\* establishes that those who have set out on a journey to Rome, or who, having arrived in Rome, are unable to complete or begin their visit to the basilicas—whether because death has overtaken them or they are prevented by some other legitimate cause—shall enjoy the benefit of the Indulgence provided they are repentant, have made their Confession, and have received the Holy Eucharist.”“We wish that these same truly penitent, confessed, and strengthened by Holy Communion persons may equally share in the aforementioned Indulgence and remission, as if they had actually visited the said basilicas on the days prescribed by Us.”

15\. Pope Boniface VIII, in his oft-cited Decretal “Antiquorum,” in which he proclaimed the Holy Year, did not include the aforementioned declaration covering foreigners, that is, pilgrims; and thus a controversy arose as to whether they had obtained the indulgence under those circumstances; whereupon he was compelled to issue a formal declaration in the Consistory in favor of foreigners, as attested by the commentator on the Decretal “Antiquorum,” vers. sedquaero: aliquis vere poenitens et confessus, among the Common Extravagances. The question was then raised as to whether, without the aforementioned declaration, those who, having decided to come to Rome and having even undertaken the journey, or having arrived in Rome but having been unable to either begin or complete the prescribed visit to the basilicas—provided they were repentant of their sins and had confessed—would receive the Jubilee Indulgence. There were also those who held that they would receive it. The most authoritative scholars were of the opposite opinion, since the Indulgence is not obtained by mere intention alone, but the actual performance is necessary. Thus argues Felino in his Treatise \*De Indulgentiis\*, cited among the \*Tractatus Magnos\* (vol. 14, p. 159, on the reverse side, line 36). The aforementioned Giovanni Battista Paulini (Book 3 of \*Jubilaeo\*, Chapter 5) also defended that same position with various arguments. A similar controversy is addressed by St. Thomas (Quodlibet, 2, art. 16), where he asks whether someone who had taken up the Cross to set out for the recovery of the Holy Land—for which distinguished action he would have received a Plenary Indulgence—would receive it if, while in a state of true penance, he were to die before undertaking the journey. The Saint resolves this by stating that it is necessary to carefully examine the text of the indulgence’s grant; for if the text states that the indulgence is granted to those who take up the Cross, the person who has taken up the Cross immediately acquires the indulgence and shares in it even if he dies before setting out on the journey; but if the text of the grant of the Indulgence states that it is granted to those who cross the sea, the person who makes the sign of the cross and dies before crossing the sea does not receive the Indulgence.

16\. Pope Clement VI, who was the one who reduced the Jubilee from the one hundred years established by Boniface VIII to fifty, in his Decretal \*Unigenitus\*, under the heading “de Poenitentiis, et remissionibus,” among the “common extravagances,” was the first to put in writing—and record in the aforementioned Decretal—a statement in favor of pilgrims or foreigners, having included it in his Decretal. However, the renowned jurist Giovanni di Anania, Archdeacon of Bologna, in his treatise \*De Jubilaeo\*, under the heading “de sortilegiis,” after proposing fourteen doubts to be examined regarding the matter of the Jubilee, raises the following in no. 9: “There is doubt regarding the one who traveled only halfway and could not go any further; regarding the one who does not complete the required number of days,” and he answers this doubt as follows: “These are resolved in the preceding Bull,” which is that of Clement VI, to which he himself had previously referred.

The same declaration is found in the Constitutions and decrees of the Holy Years issued by successive Popes. And We, in order to put an end to all disputes, have gladly followed their example by including it in our Constitution \*Peregrinantes\*; with the caveat, however, that this declaration applies solely to foreigners and does not extend to Romans or residents of Rome, as was also rightly observed by Benzonio (De anno Jubilaei, Book 5, Question 10), while provisions are made for Romans and residents of Rome who are prevented or unable to visit the basilicas, in another manner, as will be seen elsewhere.

Part II

(From the Constitution that begins: “Cum Nos nuper,” dated May 17, 1749)

17\. In the first five Jubilees, there is no mention of other indulgences being suspended, nor of other powers to absolve reserved cases; and the first to issue such a suspension on the occasion of the Holy Year was Pope Sixtus IV, as can be seen in his Decretal: “Quemadmodum,” under the heading “de poenitentiis et remissionibus” in the “Estravaganti comuni,” to invite the faithful to come to Rome to obtain the plenary indulgence that he was offering them during the Holy Year; his other Successors followed his example. Some included the suspension of indulgences and faculties in the very same Bull in which they proclaimed the Holy Year Jubilee. This is what Sixtus IV did; Alexander VI did the same, but by means of a separate Constitution. The example of Sixtus IV was followed in every respect by Clement VII and Julius III, as is evident from the Jubilee Bulls. Gregory XIII was the one who reinstated the suspension with a papal bull separate from that of the Jubilee, as Alexander VI had done, and this practice has continued ever since.

Sairo, Valenza, and Salas, followed by Father Baldello (Volume 1 of \*Theolog. Moral.\*, Book 5, “de Lege,” Disp. 16, n. 8) stated that the aforementioned suspension was superfluous, since that of Sixtus IV had already been incorporated into Canon Law, as has been noted. We are well aware that we must respect not only those Decretals included in the collections compiled by order of Gregory IX, Boniface VIII, and Clement V, but also the others known as “common extravagances,” among which is the aforementioned decree of Sixtus IV. Although the collection of these was compiled by private authority, the source from which they were drawn is the same as that of all the others, since both derive from the same Apostolic Authority, as the Venerable Brother, the current Bishop of Feltre, very aptly notes in his fine work (Institution. Juris Canonici, chap. 59, n. 8), recently published. However, upon reflection, We consider that there is at least reasonable doubt as to whether the suspension of the Indulgences and Faculties imposed by Pope Sixtus IV has been revoked; hence the necessity for a new suspension of the Indulgences and Faculties, which is effected when a Holy Year is proclaimed.

18\. The suspension of the indulgences and faculties issued by Sixtus IV was formulated in the following words: “We suspend them until our approval and that of the same See; and we wish them to remain suspended during the period of our approval and that of the aforementioned See, and in the meantime they shall not be granted to anyone”; whatever is established by the clause “ad beneplacitum Sedis Apostolicae” remains in effect even after the death of the Pope who established it, and the provision does not cease unless revoked by his Successor: “if grace is granted until the approval of the Apostolic See; for then, since the See itself does not die, it will endure perpetually, unless revoked by the Successor”: which does not occur when the Pope issues an act “usque ad suae voluntatis beneplacitum,” because the grant ceases upon the death of the grantor: “If the Roman Pontiff graciously grants you permission to retain, until such time as he sees fit, the benefices you held at the time of your promotion; such a favor expires upon his death, by which his own approval is entirely extinguished.” These are the words of Pope Boniface VIII, in the chapter “Si gratiose, de rescriptis, in sexto.”

19\. Navarro, in his \*Consilia\* (Book 5, \*De poenitentiis et remissionibus consil.\*, 17), states that he has dealt with this subject in detail; and indeed, he has discussed it at length in his treatise \*De Jubilaeo, et Indulgentiis Notabil.\* (28, nos. 27 and 28, and Notabil. 33, no. 7). There, after weighing the importance of the clause “ad nostrum et Sedis Apostolicae beneplacitum,” he states that the death of Sixtus IV is certain, but that it is unknown whether he revoked the suspension before dying, or whether some other successor of his did so; therefore, it appeared uncertain and open to debate whether Sixtus’s suspension was still in effect. Navarro lived during the time of Gregory XIII. Having observed that this Pope, in suspending the indulgences of the Holy Year, had used the clause “ad Nostrum et Sedis Apostolicae beneplacitum,” he discussed this with officials of the Roman Curia, who replied that Pope Sixtus had never issued a general revocation of the suspension, nor could it be claimed that the revocation had been prompted by the year having passed, but that it could be believed that Sixtus himself, once the Holy Year had passed, had issued a specific revocation of the suspension of certain indulgences, in response to the prayers of some of the faithful. They added that Pope Gregory XIII, too, intended to proceed in the same manner; what Navarro asserts is also confirmed by Benzone (De Anno Jubilaei, Book 4, Chapter 8, Doubt 6) and by Father Teodoro (Volume 1 of Indulgentiis, Chapter 13, Article 5).

20\. But if anyone had said, or were to say, that it is true that the papal decrees formulated with the words “ad nostrum et Sedis Apostolicae beneplacitum” due to the death of the Pope who issued them, and that revocation by one of his successors is necessary—it might even be believed that Sixtus IV, after the Holy Year had passed, did not issue an express and general revocation of the suspension, but rather issued a particular one confirming certain suspended indulgences; but at the same time he had added—or might be said to have added—certain revocations, as the schools of thought maintain, which are virtual and equivalent to express revocations, and that these occurred in the case at hand, since, despite the suspension of the indulgence during the Holy Year, it has always been known from the Supreme Pontiffs that pious and religious men, after the Holy Year had passed, preached and published the suspended indulgences, included them in their books, and wrote extensively about them, without the Apostolic See ever having taken any action against any of the aforementioned acts, even though it has not failed to prohibit apocryphal indulgences, nor to identify the revoked ones through various decrees, whenever the occasion arose to do so; it would undoubtedly have, as they say, put Navarro in a tight spot, and would still put anyone who has claimed or claims to be a follower of his opinion in a tight spot.

21\. This reflection is not our own, but comes from Castro Palao in his \*Opere Moral\* (§ 4, tract. 24, disput. unic, punct. 12, § 2, nos. 12 and 13). And since nothing else can be inferred from the aforementioned reflection—or is inferred—except that the matter is at least doubtful, this was sufficient for our Predecessors—and has been sufficient for Us—to issue a new suspension of Indulgences and Faculties on the occasion of the Jubilee of the Holy Year, notwithstanding the suspension imposed by Sixtus IV, which, as has been said, was incorporated into Canon Law.

In our Constitution “Cum Nos nuper,” which we are currently discussing, we did not use the clause “ad Nostrum et Sedis Apostolicae beneplacitum,” but rather the other one: “Apostolica auctoritate suspendimus... easque, eodem Anno durante, nulli prodesse aut suffragari debere.” We have employed this clause both because Clement VIII made use of it when suspending indulgences and faculties during the Holy Year, and all his other Successors have followed his example during the times when Holy Years were celebrated, and also because it removes the difficulty previously raised regarding whether the suspension of indulgences continues or ceases after the Holy Year, as Lavorio very aptly observes (De Jubilaeo et Indulgentiis, § 1, chap. 10, n. 13) and Quarti (Treatise on the Jubilee, first point on the suspension of indulgences, question 7) so aptly observe.

22\. Anyone with even a modest familiarity with the moral theologians will undoubtedly be aware of the many issues they raise concerning the indulgences that are suspended during the Holy Year. To resolve these issues, we have provided for a general suspension in our Constitution. However, as stated therein, we have first excluded certain indulgences from this general suspension, which we have specifically identified and named; consequently, those not expressly preserved are to be understood as suspended. There was great controversy as to whether the suspension applied only to plenary indulgences, or whether it also extended to non-plenary and partial indulgences. Navarro (De Jubilaeo et Indulgentiis notabili, 33, n. 26), Benzone (De Jubilaeo, book 4, chap. 8, question 2); Lavorio (De Jubilaeo, § 1, chap. 10, nos. 3 and 4); and Viva (De Jubilaeo praesertim Anni Sancti, quaest. 4, art. 1) wrote that only plenary indulgences remained suspended. Others, however, were of the opinion that both plenary and non-plenary—that is, partial—indulgences remained suspended. Thus wrote Vanranst (Opuscul. Histor. Theologic. de Indulgentiis, et Jubilaeo, quaest. 5), Quarti (Treatise on the Jubilee, chap. 3, point 1), and Pignatelli (Treatise on the Holy Year, chap. 13, dub. 10). This gave rise to a difficulty regarding the various formulas used in the suspensions, as some contained the following words: “Omnes et singulas Indulgentias plenarias”; others: “Omnes et singulas Indulgentias.” Some reflected that, even though non-plenary indulgences were not named in the suspension formulas, one should nevertheless believe that they were included, since some Popes, during the Holy Year, had granted that certain partial indulgences be remitted and published; which they would not have done had they not intended to include them under the suspension in the future. So wrote Father Costantini (Treatise on the Jubilee of the Holy Year, § 3, chap. 7) and Father Teodoro (De Indulgentiis, vol. 1, chap. 13, art. 5, quaest. 2). To eliminate any doubt, after listing the indulgences that we do not revoke, we add the following: “All other indulgences, both plenary and non-plenary, we suspend and declare them to be suspended.”

23\. Without straying from the subject, we will note that we have excluded from the revocation those indulgences granted directly for the souls of the deceased. The controversy remained regarding those indulgences that the living can obtain with the authority to apply them as a form of suffrage for the deceased. Quarti (Treatise on the Jubilee, chap. 3, point 1, p. 198), and Pignatelli (Treatise on the Holy Year, chap. 13, question 20) held that the aforementioned indulgences were not included in the revocation. Of the contrary opinion were Viva (De Jubilaeo, question 4, article 4), Vanranst (Opuscul. Histor. Theolog. de Indulgentiis, et Jubilaeo, question 5, no. 9), and Costantini (Treatise on the Jubilee, § 3, chap. 7, p. 244). Nor can it be denied that this was the most plausible ruling, and one that was also practiced during many Holy Years. The late Abbot, later Cardinal, Michelangelo Ricci, Secretary of the Congregation for Indulgences, during the Holy Year celebrated by Clement X, in the Congregations that were held—and in which the petition of certain devout individuals was examined but not granted— that the general suspension of indulgences should not include those granted to the living with the faculty to apply them to the deceased, proved to be strongly attached to the opinion that the aforementioned indulgences are suspended during the Holy Year and included under the revocation, that is, the general suspension. The following reason is also put forward: in the indulgences granted to the living with the faculty to apply them to the dead “per modum suffragii,” two aspects may be considered; one, which is the principal aspect and concerns the living; the other, which is secondary, and which concerns the dead; since the principal part is included in the revocation, it does not seem possible for the secondary part to remain exempt.

24\. Setting aside the previous Notifications published during other Holy Years, the one published by Cardinal di Carpegna, Vicar of Rome, in the year 1675—a Jubilee Year—by order of Pope Clement X, reads as follows: “The indulgences of privileged altars for the deceased are not to be understood as suspended; rather, those indulgences are to be considered suspended which the living may obtain for themselves, so that they may grant them to the souls in Purgatory by way of suffrage.” Similar were the Notifications published during the Holy Year celebrated in the time of Innocent XII. Pope Benedict XIII also followed the same guidelines in his Notification published on January 10, 1725, the Jubilee Year. But since the devotion of that pious and devout Pope Benedict, Our Benefactor, toward the souls in Purgatory was beyond words—as is clearly evident from the sermons on Purgatory that he had published— on February 9 of that same year he published another Notification, which can be found in the “History of the Holy Years” (published by Alfani, p. 564); subsequently, on April 28 of that same year, he issued a Brief, which is found printed by Father Teodoro in his oft-cited Treatise (De Indulgentiis, Part 1, p. 375), in which he declared that the indulgences granted to the living with the faculty to apply them to the deceased were not included in the revocation he had made during the Holy Year—but only insofar as they concern the suffrage for the souls of the dead. And although it is a rule that indulgences granted to the living cannot be applied “per modum suffragii” to the dead, unless this is expressly stated in the grant, he added that during the Holy Year, so that the poor souls in Purgatory might not be left without the necessary suffrages, the living may apply indulgences for their benefit, even if the concession does not expressly grant the authority to apply them to the deceased. And We, in our Constitution, have gladly followed the example of such a great Pontiff.

25\. In Our Constitution \*Cum Nos nuper\*, not only—as has been stated thus far—are indulgences suspended during the Holy Year, but also all the faculties and indults to absolve from reserved cases, as provided for in the \*Bulla Coenae\*, in accordance with what is stated in the section “Caeteras omnes.” Just as we have proceeded with the suspension of indulgences, so too have we proceeded with the suspension of faculties and indults, which we did not intend to include in the revocation; it follows, therefore, that those not specifically excluded remain included.

In the general suspension of faculties and indults, doubts had arisen on other occasions as to whether this included the faculties vested in Regular Prelates—by virtue of their office—to absolve their regular subjects from reserved cases and to grant dispensations from certain impediments, as can be seen in the \*Viva\* (De Jubilaeo, question 4, final article, § 4); Constantine (On the Jubilee of the Holy Year, part 3, chapter 7, p. 250); and Pasqualigo (question 160). But in order to remove any ambiguity regarding the faculties exempted from the suspension, We have exempted those of “the Superior Orders of Regulars, whatever faculties the Apostolic See has granted to them regarding their regulars who are equally subject to them.”

26\. But since the Regulars do not merely enjoy the indulgences and faculties over their own regular subjects, but some of them also claim to have faculties and indulgences, granted to them by the Apostolic See and never revoked, to absolve laypeople from cases reserved to the See itself, and to be able to commute vows and grant various other things, which need not be mentioned here; a major controversy remains as to whether these are included in the suspension of indulgences and faculties that takes place during the Holy Year. In this controversy, some maintain that the faculties to absolve from reserved sins and to commute vows remain suspended, even if they were not conferred because of, or on the occasion of, the indulgences; while others distinguish between the faculties to absolve granted specifically because of, or on the occasion of, the indulgences, and those granted for other reasons; arguing that the former remain suspended during the Holy Year, but not the latter; as can be seen in Viva at the cited passage, in Costantini also at the cited passage (p. 246 ff.), in Vanranst cited (Opuscul. de Indulgentiis, Q. 5), and in many others, whom it is unnecessary to name here.

27\. Those who subscribe to the first view—namely, that the faculties and indulgences granted on the occasion of the Indulgences, or for other reasons, remain suspended—base their argument on the broad scope with which the suspensive bulls are conceived, and on the exclusion of certain faculties and indulgences from the general scope of the suspensions; as can be seen in Sorbo and Rodriguez, cited by Pasqualigo (op. cit., q. 160). Confirming this approach is a decision by Pope Julius III, who, during the Jubilee Year, suspended—for those confessors who held it—the faculty to absolve cases reserved to the Apostolic See, a privilege granted by special grace to the Fathers of the Society of Jesus, during the Holy Year then in progress, to enjoy the privilege of granting absolution in cases reserved to the Supreme Pontiff and the Apostolic See, a privilege granted to them by Paul III—certainly not because of or in connection with the Indulgences, but for other reasons. It follows from this that the aforementioned privileges had been included by the Pope in the suspension he had issued during the Holy Year. This fact is reported by Orlandini in the \*History of the Society of Jesus\*, and is contested by Febei (\*Tract. de Anno Jubilaei\*, § 2, chap. 10, p. 162); by Vittorelli (\*History of the Papal Jubilees\*, § 3, p. 371); by Alfani in \*History of the Holy Years\* (p. 311 ff.), and by Ricci (\*Treatise on Jubilees\*, chap. 38).

28\. Those who hold the second view, however, point out that the Roman Pontiffs have customarily granted certain faculties to facilitate the acquisition of indulgences and to remove obstacles. These are the only faculties understood to be suspended during the Holy Year, the desire for their suspension being motivated by the wish that, during the Holy Year, the faithful be brought to Rome to acquire indulgences; but this does not mean that the faculties granted absolutely—not in connection with indulgences, nor because of them—remain suspended. Thus, among many others, Quarti maintains this view (Treatise on the Jubilee, ch. 3, n. 2, p. 219).

Navarro, commenting on the papal bull issued by Gregory XIII during the Jubilee year suspending indulgences—even though he had easy access to the Pope, by whom he was well regarded and appreciated—reports (De Jubilaeo et Indulgentiis, note 33, sub n. 2) that, so as not to trouble him so often, he consulted with the Prelate Datary of that time—a highly knowledgeable man who was well informed of the Pope’s intentions— and asked him whether the general suspension included the faculties and indults granted for other reasons, and not specifically for the sake of or on the occasion of indulgences; he reported the following response: “It was the intention of Our Most Holy Lord to suspend all and every plenary indulgence, and all and every cause for which they were sought, granted, and bestowed; so that no indulgence that is not plenary, nor any faculty granted for the purpose of seeking a plenary indulgence, shall be considered suspended.” Another similar statement by Clement VIII is reported by Cardinal de Lugo (De Poenit., disp. 20, sect. 8, n. 145).

29\. In this controversy, whether the Bull suspending all Indulgences and the power to absolve cases reserved to the Holy See applies, in the Holy Year, only to the powers granted in connection with Indulgences, or whether it also extends to those granted without any reference to indulgences, We have always believed—and continue to believe—that the diversity of opinions arises from the diversity of the wording and clauses with which the Breves of suspension are granted; and from the fact that the authors failed to consider that suspensions are not uniform, and that one suspension may encompass all the faculties to absolve cases reserved to the Holy See, while another may encompass only some and not others. In the Decretal “Quemadmodum” of Sixtus IV—who, as mentioned above, was the first to suspend indulgences and the faculties to absolve reserved cases during the Holy Year—the following words are found: “All and every plenary indulgence, even those granted on the occasion of a Jubilee, etc., as well as the faculties, concessions, and indulgences granted by Us, by the Apostolic See itself, or by its authority, to confessors appointed with the power to absolve even in cases reserved to the Apostolic See... suspendimus.” This same formula was used by the successors Alexander VI, Clement VII, and Julius III in the Constitutions, in which they proclaimed the universal Jubilee of the Holy Year.

These are overly general terms, and there is no reason to limit the suspension to the faculties to grant absolution granted in connection with indulgences, since it must also include the faculties to grant absolution granted for other reasons; therefore, it should come as no surprise to anyone that Pope Julius III, who had made use of them, granted the Fathers of the Society of Jesus the power, during the Holy Year he was celebrating, to grant absolution in reserved cases, availing themselves of the privilege granted to them by Paul III, who had previously included it within the scope of his suspension, as mentioned above.

30\. The broad scope of the formula remained in effect until Gregory XIII, who—in the suspension of indulgences and faculties that he issued during the Holy Year he celebrated—restricted it as follows: “By the fullness of our apostolic power, we suspend… all and whatsoever plenary indulgences, even those granted on the occasion of a Jubilee, and, by reason thereof, all faculties, concessions, and indulgences whatsoever granted by Us or by the Holy See, and by its authority to any churches.” The words “et earum causa,” as they imply that the suspensive brief applies only to the faculties to absolve granted for the purpose of the Indulgence, which very well justifies the explanation given by his Datary and the opinion of Navarro, as mentioned above.

31\. The restrictive interpretation of Gregory XIII was not upheld by Clement VIII, who returned to the original, broader interpretation: “All and every indulgence, even perpetual ones, whether remissions of sins, faculties, or the power to absolve even in cases reserved to the Apostolic See... suspendimus”; his Successors followed the same course, abandoning the words “vel earum causa,” from which the restriction arose. Cardinal de Lugo, in the passage cited above, maintains that one must admit only the suspension of the faculties granted in connection with indulgences and not of those granted for other reasons, even though the words “vel earum causa,” thus asserting that Clement himself had declared: “Sed idem Pontifex interrogatus dixit eandem fuisse intentionem suam, et ad tollendum dubium, edita fuit interum eadem Bulla additis eisdem verbis,” that is, “earum causa,” We have no evidence of this change, and even if it were to exist, it would demonstrate that if the words “earum causa” are not present in the Suspensive Brief, both the faculties to absolve and others pertaining to indulgences—or to other reasons—are understood to be included within that scope. The words “earum causa” were also omitted from the suspensive briefs of Urban VIII and his other successors.

Although the aforementioned Cardinal de Lugo, continuing in his argument, states that Urban’s brief must be understood in accordance with the explanation given regarding Clement VIII’s brief, it should be noted that he does not say that Urban made such a declaration, but rather that he, the Cardinal, held this opinion: “Therefore, since there was still doubt regarding this matter, I likewise stated that the Regulars’ Faculty had not been revoked.” In fact, upon reviewing the records of Pope Urban’s Holy Year, no entry was found concerning the matter in question. Furthermore, the records of the Congregations held during the Jubilee year celebrated by Clement X were diligently examined; from which it was possible to deduce that those present were of the opinion that, since the words “et earum causa” were missing from the suspensive brief, the faculties to absolve—whether granted or not granted in connection with indulgences—were to be understood as revoked. In fact, they made every effort to have the Supreme Pontiff declare—as was indeed declared—that even during the Holy Year, the Regulars could avail themselves of the privileges to absolve their own Regular subjects in reserved cases; which they would not have done had they not assumed that Pope Clement’s suspension also included the faculties to grant absolution not granted in connection with indulgences; for it was precisely these faculties that they were requesting be preserved in favor of the Regulars.

32\. Father Costantini, in his frequently cited treatise (On the Holy Year, Part 3, Chapter 7, p. 245), puts it very well when he says that everything depends on the Pope’s intention. We have not included the words “et earum causa” in Our Constitution, as Gregory XIII did; nor have We had them reinstated in another edition of Our Constitution, as Cardinal de Lugo claims Clement VIII did; We attach great importance to the meaning of words; therefore, We declare that, except for the faculties expressly excluded, the the other faculties to absolve cases reserved to the Apostolic See—even those granted in the Bull \*Coenae\* or in connection with indulgences, whether granted at Our discretion or for other reasons. Although this should suffice to make clear, as far as the matter at hand is concerned, Our intention, We nevertheless deemed it necessary to reiterate in Our subsequent Constitution, which begins “Convocatis,” that all faculties to absolve and dispense—whether granted on account of and in connection with indulgences, or for any other reason or cause—are suspended when it comes to their use outside Rome, but not within Rome; and when it concerns their use outside Rome with regard to laypeople, but not when it concerns their use—whether outside Rome or within Rome—with regard to the regular subjects of those prelates and regular superiors who are in lawful possession of the said faculties; as also mentioned in the preceding § 24.

Part III

(From the Apostolic Constitution beginning with “Convocatis,” dated November 25, 1749)

33\. It is not Our intention here to compose a history of the Holy Years, since this has already been done by many others, and also on the occasion of the Holy Year celebrated by Pope Benedict XIII by the aforementioned Father Alfani of the Order of Preachers. Therefore, we shall refrain from recounting how Our Predecessors began to appoint the minor Penitentiaries—and in what numbers—and how others were subsequently added to them in the Basilicas of Saints Peter and Paul, St. John, and St. Mary Major, to hear confessions and absolve from their sins all those who came to visit in order to obtain the Plenary Indulgence. Nor will we speak of the appointment, made at a later date by the Cardinal Vicar of Rome, of other confessors who, in their churches, could hear confessions for the greater convenience of the penitents. The appointment of both the minor penitentiaries in the basilicas and their assistants, as well as the priests chosen by the Cardinal Vicar, was found by Us to have been established, and We can say that We observed it in practice during the other two previous Holy Years that We witnessed being celebrated in Rome. Through the Pontifical Oracle issued by the Cardinal Penitentiary, the appropriate faculties were granted to the minor Penitentiaries of the basilicas and to the Assistants; and through the same Oracle, the Cardinal Vicar granted certain faculties to the confessors whom he designated. This gave rise to a certain degree of confusion and difficulty, even though instructions were being published for the proper use of the faculties granted to both groups, and various doubts that arose were being resolved through various notifications. We have sought to bring everything together in a single Constitution, which is the one we are now discussing. In it, we have included the faculties that we grant to the minor penitentiaries of the basilicas and to their assistants in those and other churches of Rome; the instructions for the proper use of these faculties; the faculties granted to confessors appointed by the Cardinal Vicar; and clarifications regarding certain doubts raised previously, so that, as the saying goes, at a glance everyone may see what is necessary to fulfill the ministry of Penitentiary and Confessor—as designated above—during the coming Holy Year. Thus, all that remains is to exhort and enjoin, as we do, each Penitentiary or appointed Confessor to read carefully what is granted to each of them, so as not to overstep the bounds of their authority, to the detriment of their own souls and the souls of their penitents.

II

(On the Powers Granted in the “Convocatis” Constitution to the Penitentiaries for the upcoming Holy Year Regarding the Absolution of Sins and Incurred Penances)

34\. There seem to be two noteworthy aspects to the proposed topic: the first concerns penitents; the other concerns sins.

35\. There is great controversy as to whether certain members of religious orders may, during the Jubilee of the Holy Year—or during other Jubilees in which penitents are granted the right to choose a confessor approved by the Ordinary—confess to a secular confessor or to a member of another religious order, approved by the Ordinary to hear the confessions of seculars, and to do so without the permission of their superiors; since certain religious communities—or orders—have privileges granted to them by the Supreme Pontiffs, which, despite the permission granted during Jubilees to confess to a confessor approved by the Ordinary, this remains forbidden to their members unless they have the express consent of their regular superiors. It is said that such a privilege was granted by Leo X to the Minims, by Alexander VI to the Cistercians, by St. Pius V to the Preachers, and by Sixtus V to the Barnabites. Gregory XIII granted a broad privilege to the Society of Jesus in the bull that begins: “Decet Romanum Pontificem,” in which he declares: “It is not, nor will it ever be, the intention of our mind or of the Apostolic See that members of the Society, without the express permission of the Superiors of that same Society, should make use of the faculties granted in the Jubilee Cruciate Bulls and have been granted thus far, or may be granted in the future—even if those Bulls expressly permit all Regulars, including mendicant orders, to make use of such faculties.” Other Regular Orders claim to be able to share in this privilege by virtue of communication, as Father Costantini rightly observes (Treatise on the Jubilee, Part 3, Chapter 5, § 2).

36\. This issue is thoroughly discussed in the work entitled \*De Privilegiis Religiosorum\*: a work composed by a late Canonicus Regularis Lateranensis, which Father Eusebio Amort, also a Lateran Canon, has recently published, having illustrated it with diligent and accurate notes (see no. 17 of \*De potestate Regularium eligendi sibi Confessarium\*, p. 750). In the aforementioned work, it is extensively demonstrated that, during the Jubilee, the Canons Regular of the Lateran may elect as their confessor—even without the permission of their superiors—a confessor who is either a secular priest or a regular of another order, provided he is approved by the Ordinary. Father Viva, in his frequently cited treatise (De Jubilaeo, quaest. 9, art. 1), discusses the matter in general terms; he sides with the opinion opposed to the freedom of the Canons Regular and considers the opinion favorable to them to be merely probable. Natale Alessandro (De Indulgentiis, chap. 3, rule 17) protests that he cannot see how such freedom could be taken away from the Regulars during the Jubilee. Cardinal de Lugo distinguishes between the Bull of the Crusade and the Jubilee, stating that, while the former is in force, Regulars cannot choose a confessor approved by the Ordinary for Seculars without the permission of their Regular Superiors; which has also been established by many of our predecessor Supreme Pontiffs, and by Us as well in our Constitution, which begins with Apostolica Indulta (100, § 7, Bullar., Vol. 1); since the privilege of the Crusade is a permanent privilege which, if it were to apply to religious orders, would be detrimental to them; Moreover, the Regulars may choose the aforementioned confessor by virtue of the Jubilee, which is a privilege of short duration that benefits everyone and harms no one. Others, however, teach that this controversy is not justified when the Regulars go to confession to the minor penitentiaries of the basilicas of Rome, whether during the Holy Year or outside of it; both because they publicly practice this without any authorization from their superiors—which implies tacit permission from the Supreme Pontiffs—and because, since these penitentiaries are appointed to the basilicas by the Roman Pontiffs to hear the confessions of all Catholics, whether secular or regular, religious who go to them for confession are in effect going to a priest appointed by their own shepherd, since the Roman Pontiff is the Supreme Shepherd of all Christ’s sheep and of all their shepherds.

The authors who hold this view are cited by Rotario (Theolog. moral. Regular., vol. 3, book 1, chap. 1, point 1, no. 18).

This is widely attested to by Father Siro, who served for many years as Penitentiary at the Lateran Basilica, in his Treatise on the “Powers of the Minor Penitentiaries of the Basilicas of Rome” (§ 1, chap. 1, question 6). But We, without in any way prejudicing their right, but, as the saying goes, “jura juribus addendo,” and solely to provide ever greater peace of conscience, have included among the Powers We grant this Holy Year to the Penitentiaries of the Basilicas the power to absolve “any person who confesses to them, including members of religious orders, of whatever order they may be, Congregation, or Institute, even if prohibited from confessing their sins outside their own religion by the prescribed Superiors, or by their own Constitutions—even those approved by the Apostolic See—or otherwise by an Indult, Decree, or Apostolic Precept.”

37\. This is what concerns penitents. As for sins, in the Diaries of Giovanni Burcardo, Master of Ceremonies of the Papal Chapel during the reign of Alexander VI, in Bzovio’s Annals and in Alfani’s frequently cited History of the Holy Years (Book 8 of the Holy Year, 1500, p. 244, nos. 7, 8, and 9), the questions posed by the Penitentiaries of St. Peter’s to Pope Alexander VI regarding the Holy Year, which was being celebrated at that time, are recorded. Among them is the request to be able to absolve “a peccatis omnibus quantumcumque enormibus”; and the Pope, in a Constitution of his that begins “Cum in principio,” granted it, but with the exclusion of four cases, namely: conspiracy against the person of the Pope and his State; the forgery of Apostolic letters and commissions; the transport of weapons and other prohibited items to the lands of the Infidels; and the violent assault on Cardinals, Bishops, Prelates, and other senior clergy. The same exclusion was made by Pope Clement VII, during the Holy Year he celebrated, for the Penitentiaries of the Vatican Basilica themselves, in a Constitution that we have in our possession and which begins “Pastoris aeterni.” The aforementioned cases and others are discussed in a Constitution of Paul II, which begins “Etsi Dominici Gregis,” among the common Extravagantes under the title “de Poenitentiis et remissionibus”; which has led some to believe and write that the minor Penitentiaries of the Basilicas, during the Holy Year, cannot grant absolution in the aforementioned cases, with the exceptions noted. But We, to remove every difficulty, and following the example of our other Predecessors, in our Constitution, which we are now discussing, in No. IV, have granted the said Penitentiaries the authority to absolve even those censures reserved in the “Bulla Coenae” to the Supreme Pontiff and the Apostolic See, and furthermore “from all sins and transgressions, however grave and enormous they may be.” Furthermore, in No. XXXIII, we have declared that the other faculties—whether already granted or to be granted to them by the Cardinal Major Penitentiary—shall remain in full force and effect even during the Holy Year.

38\. As will be explained below, the authority to absolve from manifest heresy is never deemed to have been granted—even if the words of the Indult are general and very general—unless the heresy is expressly and individually named. In the Faculties granted by Us to the Penitentiaries for the Holy Year, the case of manifest heresy is expressly mentioned in No. VII; however, the faculty to absolve from the censures incurred for external formal heresy is restricted within certain limits, and the Penitentiary must be mindful of these limits so as not to do what he has no authority to do.

39\. The Confessor, who lacks the authority to absolve a person complicit in a vile sin or in dishonesty contrary to the same precept during a Jubilee Year, will be discussed further below. We conclude this section on the faculties granted during the Holy Year to the Minor Penitentiaries regarding the absolution of sins and censures, inviting them to read our Constitution on the Faculties of the Cardinal Major Penitentiary and the Office of the Penitentiary. From this reading, they will understand that they are prohibited—despite the broad wording of the concession and despite whatever may have been communicated to them or is to be communicated to them by the Cardinal Grand Penitentiary himself—from doing anything that is forbidden to the aforementioned Cardinal in the Constitution. For example, if the Cardinal Grand Penitentiary cannot absolve “in occultis etiam in foro coscientiae” anyone who, while the Roman Pontiff is alive, has entered into treaties, made vows, or formed pacts regarding the election of His Successor, or anyone who, through judicial astrology—whether personally or through others—has sought to ascertain the state of the Christian Republic or the life and death of the living Pope; much less can the Minor Penitentiary grant absolution. The Constitution begins “Pastor bonus” (it is No. 95 in our Bollario, Volume 1). When carefully read and considered, it can serve as a great source of guidance for the Minor Penitentiaries, to whom we further make known that, should any of the aforementioned cases arise, they must not fail to turn to the Cardinal Major Penitentiary, to whom We shall suggest and prescribe the appropriate and necessary remedies.

III

(On the Powers of Penitentiary Minors Regarding Vows, as Set Forth in the Constitution “Convocatis”)

40\. During the Holy Year celebrated by Alexander VI, as can be seen in Bzovio, “ad Annum Christi 1500, no. 5,” among the other questions that the Minor Penitentiaries posed to the Pope, the eleventh in order is as follows: “Commutatio omnium votorum, adempto continentiae, aut Religionis solemni.” But even if this had been granted to them at that time, it is certain that in subsequent times nothing else was granted to them except the authority to commute—by dispensation—all simple vows, even those reserved to the Apostolic See, into other pious works.

41\. This means that they cannot grant dispensations from the solemn vows of Charity, Poverty, and Obedience, which are solemnized through Religious Profession or through the reception of Holy Orders, since these are the only solemn vows; and since all other vows are simple vows, even if made publicly, according to the Decretal of Boniface VIII, sole chapter, “de Voto, et Voti Redemptione, in sexto,” and the common opinion of the authors at the Pontifical (In verb. Votum, In princip.): Conversely, it is permissible to commute, by dispensation, all other simple vows—even those reserved to the Holy See—into other pious works.

42\. St. Thomas (Summa Theologiae, 2.2, quaest. 88, art. 12 ad tertium) mentions only two simple vows reserved to the Pope, “continentiae et Peregrinationis Terrae Sanctae”; and the Saint likely mentioned only these two because, in his time, those were the only two reserved to the Holy See. Subsequently, the number of simple vows reserved to the Pope increased; as everyone well knows, these are the vows to enter religious life, of perpetual continence, of pilgrimage to Jerusalem, to the Shrine of St. James in Galicia, and to Rome to visit the Basilicas of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul; whether this stems from custom or from the subsequent canonical provision recorded in the chapter “Etsi Dominici” of Sixtus IV (c. 5, De Poenitentiis et Remissionibus, inter Extravagantes communes). Others, among the vows reserved to the Supreme Pontiff, add that of visiting the Holy House of Loreto; but there is no evidence whatsoever of this. Finally, others place all perpetual vows in this category, which may cause misunderstanding unless something is added; since the proposition must be understood to refer solely to the perpetual vow by which one, upon accepting the episcopate, obliges oneself to care for the souls entrusted to him, according to the response of Innocent III in chap. “Nisi cum pridem, de renuntiatione,” and the doctrine of the most authoritative theologians, as can be seen in the \*Silvio\* (2.2, D. Thomae, quaest. 8, art. 12, concl. 2).

43\. As far as we know, there has been no controversy regarding what has been said so far. The controversy that has arisen on other occasions concerns whether the Penitentiary, who has the authority to absolve from simple vows—including the five reserved to the Apostolic See—may exercise this authority when the vows have been confirmed by oath. In this matter, some have held that he may; St. Thomas teaches (Summa Theologiae, 2.2, quaest. 89, art. 8) that the bond of a vow is stronger than that of an oath. Therefore, since the Penitentiary has the authority to absolve from a vow, it must be said that he also has the authority to absolve from a vow confirmed by an oath. Others have held that this is not the case, because in a vow confirmed by an oath—since there are two distinct bonds, one arising from the vow and the other from the oath—the one who has the faculty to dissolve one does not have the authority to dissolve the other, unless it is individually granted to him; from which a strong principle arises, namely that one who has made a vow under duress is not obligated to fulfill it, but is obligated to fulfill it if it was confirmed by an oath; and likewise, one who has promised to pay usurious interest is not obligated to pay it; but remains obligated to pay if they have confirmed it by oath, in accordance with the Decretal “Debitores, de Jurejurando.”

44\. The names of those who favor one opinion or the other can be found in Leandro (Part 1, Tract. 5, \*De Sacramento Poenitentiae\*, Disput. 14, Quest. 101). Azorio, in turn (Instit. moral., Vol. 1, Book 11, Chapter 10, Question 2), sets forth the grounds for both opinions, ultimately adhering to the view that denies those with the authority to absolve from vows the power to do so when the vows have been confirmed by oath. Father Siro (De Facultatibus Minorum Poenitentiariorum, Part 1, Chapter 6, Doubt 4) maintains that the faculty granted to commute vows also includes sworn vows. We, in order to eliminate any controversy, among the faculties granted to the Minor Penitentiaries in the Holy Year (in § 8 of this Constitution of ours), have not only granted them the faculty to commute, by dispensation, all simple vows—even those reserved to the Holy See—into other pious works, but we have also added that they may do so even if these vows have been confirmed by oath: “Omnia et singula simplicia Vota, etiam Sedi Apostolicae reservata, etiam jurata, commutare, dispensando, possint in alia pia opera”; which We also did in Our aforementioned Constitution \*Pastor bonus\*, concerning the Powers of the Cardinal Grand Penitentiary, in which, when addressing the authority granted to him to commute, by dispensation, simple vows reserved to the Holy See into other pious works, We added the following words: “even if confirmed by a private oath.” In doing all this, we were guided by the example of Gregory XIII’s dealings with the Fathers of the Society of Jesus. Pope Paul III had granted their ordinary confessors—appointed by their superiors and approved by the local ordinaries—the faculty to commute vows, with the exception of those reserved to the Holy See. Since there was doubt as to whether this faculty included sworn vows, Gregory XIII, subject to the exception made by Paul III, extended the authority to include sworn vows as well, as can be seen in the compendium of their privileges “under the heading Commutatio, § 1.”

45\. It is worth noting that the faculties discussed thus far are not faculties to commute simply and absolutely, but to commute by granting a dispensation; for commutation and dispensation are distinct from one another; a dispensation removes the obligation of the vow without any other substitution; but simple commutation does not extinguish the obligation of the vow, since it transfers the subject matter of the vow to another subject matter that takes the place of the first. Father Siro, in his aforementioned work (Part 1, Chapter 6, Doubt 2), observes that what is granted to the penitent consists of a commutation mixed with some dispensation. Just as simple commutation alone would require substitution with a matter of greater or at least equal weight, so commutation mixed with a dispensation allows for some moderate disparity between the matter of the vow and the substituted matter: “Ut principale,” writes the aforementioned author, “the commutation is established as the principal, and the dispensation is joined as an accessory, so that there must essentially be a commutation, which by its nature is to a greater or equal degree, and through the accessory dispensation departs somewhat from the strictness of its own nature: therefore, the inequality of the substituted matter in the commutation of the vow must be moderate and not excessive, since the accessory dispensation must follow the nature of its principal, without significantly altering that nature, lest what is set forth as accessory take the place of the principal element—namely, by granting a greater dispensation than the substitution itself—which would be contrary to the intention of the Pontiff, who grants the Penitentiary the faculty to substitute through dispensation, not to grant a dispensation through substitution.”

46\. Two examples are particularly relevant: one from Pope Alexander III in his Decretal \*Venientis de Voto\*, in which he dispenses, for very compelling reasons, a cleric who had vowed to travel to Jerusalem to visit the Lord’s Tomb, while commuting the vow to almsgiving, and enjoining him to support a poor person at his own expense “tam in victu, quam in vestitu” for the rest of his life, provided, however, that his means were sufficient to bear this burden.

The other example comes from Innocent III in the Decretale Magnae, in which he dispenses Bishop Trecense from fulfilling a vow he had made—albeit imprudently—to travel to Jerusalem, instructing him, “that all expenses he would have incurred in going, staying, and returning be entrusted to a religious, for the necessary use of that land,” that is, the Holy Land, “to be transferred without any reduction,” and substituting for the hardship he would have endured on the journey by increasing other labors he required him to perform in the service of his flock: “Compensating labor with labor, spending more time in prayer, devoting himself more devoutly to prayer, and exercising himself more rigorously in fasting, while watching over his flock with pastoral care.”

47\. These words, which refer not merely to a substitution but to a substitution combined with a dispensation, not only serve to properly regulate dispensations and the substitution of vows in other pious works, but they also resolve a difficulty that might have arisen (and has arisen on other occasions among authors) caused by the use of the word “commutation” alone, or the word “dispensation” alone; with some maintaining that the power to commute vows does not entail the power to grant dispensations regarding them, since, as has already been stated, commutation and dispensation are entirely distinct acts; while others teach that not even from the granted faculty to grant dispensations regarding vows can one infer the power to commute them, since this is a delegated power, which must always be confined within the precise limits of the letter of the delegation. Pignatello discusses this and many other points in his \*Treatise on the Holy Year\* (chap. 19, questions 2 and 3). Also discussing the Holy Year, there have been those who have written that the faculty to commute and to grant dispensations regarding vows is understood to be granted, even if neither one nor the other was expressly stated. But these are opinions commonly rejected, as can be seen in Viva (De Jubilaeo, question 12, art. 1, n. 2), and in Costantini (On the Jubilee of the Holy Year, part 3, chap. 1), and by many others. These opinions are criticized for having included in the aforementioned § 8 and the following sections—concerning the authority granted to Penitentiaries regarding vows—the frequently mentioned words: “to commute and dispense.”

Part IV

(On the Powers Regarding the Exemptions Contained in the “Convocatis” Constitution)

48\. Anyone with some knowledge of Canon Law and Moral Theology will undoubtedly be aware of the controversies: one, whether the term “censure” encompasses the irregularity arising from a crime; and the other, whether one who incurs a censure—that is, excommunication, suspension, or interdict—while having been ordained to the minor orders and solemnly exercising the orders received, thereby incurs irregularity. The first question arises from the interpretation of the Decretal “Quaerenti, de verb. significat,” in which, when the Supreme Pontiff was asked what is meant by the term “censure,” he replied that it includes interdict, suspension, and excommunication; despite which, Gonzalez (Commentar. ad dictum textum) maintains that the term “censura” also includes irregularity “ex delicto”; however, Suarez, Molina, Trullengo, Filliuc, Reginaldo, and many others hold the opposite view. A second controversy then arises from the Decretals recorded under the title \*De Clerico excommunicato, deposito, vel interdicto ministrante\*, in which, although it is stated that one who celebrates Mass while bound by a minor excommunication commits a grave sin, he does not incur irregularity. However, one does incur such irregularity if bound by a major excommunication and, being a priest, celebrates Mass, or having been ordained to the Order of Subdiaconate or Diaconate, performs the duties of a subdeacon or deacon, provided that he knows he has incurred the censure or is not unaware of it due to gross, whether through wilful or erroneous ignorance; however, there is no norm that addresses a cleric excommunicated under major excommunication—who has not been ordained to Holy Orders but has received Minor Orders—declaring him to be irregular if he solemnly exercises the Orders he has received. Navarro, Avila, and many others are of the opinion that in such a case no irregularity is incurred. On the contrary, Pirhing (in Book 5 of the Decretals, Title 27, § 6), Anacletus (in the same title of the Decretals, nos. 21 and 22), and Fagnano (in the chapter “Si quis Presbiter,” from no. 2 to the end of “De Clerico excommunicato ministrante”), maintain that he does incur irregularity; the latter adds that this is the opinion of the Penitentiary and the Congregation of the Council.

49\. Two doubts could arise from these disputes: the first, whether—since the Penitentiaries were granted, in No. IV, the power to absolve “from any sentences of excommunication, and other ecclesiastical censures,” it is understood that they were also granted the authority to absolve in secret, and in the forum of conscience, from irregularities contracted “ex delicto,” with the exception, however, of those arising from intentional homicide; since, according to the opinion of some, irregularities “ex delicto” are included under the term “censura.”

Second, since the Minor Penitentiaries have been granted the authority to absolve priests and others who have been ordained to Holy Orders from the irregularity incurred “ob violationem censurarum,” this authority is also understood to apply to those who have been ordained to minor orders.

50\. Gonzalez, in his Commentary on the aforementioned Decretal “Quaerenti de verbor. significat,” although he follows the opinion of those who wish to include irregularity ex delicto under the term “censure,” he nevertheless protests that, if during the Jubilee the faculty to absolve from all censures is granted, this should not be understood to include the faculty to absolve from irregularity ex delicto, since this is a matter worthy of special mention and more difficult to resolve.

To resolve all these disputes, two categories were established: one for censures, and the other for irregularities. In the first category, the term “absolution” was used; in the second, the term “dispensation” was used, as this is the proper terminology derived from the Sacred Council of Trent (Session 24, Chapter 6, De Reformatione). This makes it quite clear that, regardless of the disputes, if the term “censure” is understood to mean irregularity arising from a crime, since We have separated censures from irregularities, and with regard to irregularities have granted minor Penitentiaries only the authority to grant a dispensation from the irregularity “ob violationem censurarum,” using the word “dumtaxat,” this excludes any other irregularity, whether due to a crime or a defect. And although only priests and those ordained to Holy Orders are named there, everyone can reasonably understand that, if the greater is granted, the lesser is understood to be granted as well; it is therefore not forbidden for Penitentiaries—who are granted the authority to dispense from the irregularity incurred “ob violationem censurarum” by priests or those ordained to Holy Orders, provided it is hidden—to exercise the same authority with regard to clerics ordained to minor orders.

51\. The other faculties to grant dispensations in matters of conscience, and in the secret cases referred to the minor penitentiaries on the occasion of the Jubilee, remain in effect. Regarding these faculties, there is nothing further to add except that they should carefully consider the extent and terms of what is granted to them, so as not to be found guilty before the judgment seat of God of having failed to exercise their ministry properly, to the detriment of the souls of the penitents.

52\. It can easily happen that foreigners who have come to Rome to obtain the Holy Year Indulgence are unable—either because of extreme poverty or because of a pressing need—to remain long enough to visit the basilicas fifteen times; and it is also common for Romans and residents of Rome to be unable, due to illness or some other legitimate impediment, to visit the basilicas thirty times. For this reason, in No. XX of our aforementioned Constitution, the Minor Penitentiaries were granted the authority, with regard to visitors, either to reduce the fifteen visits to the four basilicas to three, or to substitute the visits with other pious works; and as for Romans and residents of Rome, in the following No. XXI, they have been granted the authority to substitute the thirty visits with other acts of piety that can be performed by those who are unable to visit, entrusting it to the conscience of the Penitentiaries not to abuse the aforementioned powers.

53\. This authority, whether regarding foreigners or Romans, is limited to visiting the basilicas; however, it cannot and must not extend to other prescribed acts, such as confession, communion, and other prayers, which may be performed separately from visiting the basilicas. Therefore, the controversy—that is, the dispute among the Doctors—as to whether, during Jubilees, the prescribed acts of Confession and Communion may be substituted with other pious works, even for persons capable of receiving Holy Communion, is hereby settled. This controversy is addressed by Cardinal de Lugo (Disp. 27 De Poenit., sect. 7, n. 118, vol. 1); Leandro (De Sacram Poenit., tract. 5, quaest. 95); La Croix (Book 6, Part 2, \*De Indulgentiis\*, n. 1442). Costantini notes in his \*Treatise on the Holy Year\* (§ 3, chap. 4, p. 204, et seq.), not only that, since the faculty to commute is restricted to visiting the Basilicas, it cannot be extended to the commutation of other prescribed works, as we have just mentioned; but also that the substitution must not be made for other works—even if pious—to which the penitent is otherwise obligated. There is a question among the writers as to whether one can gain the Indulgence by performing certain works that one is otherwise obligated to perform; for example, if almsgiving is included among the prescribed works, is it sufficient, to obtain the Indulgence, to give alms to a poor person, giving him the amount that one would be obliged to give him by virtue of justice; or if, being obligated by a will to give alms to the poor, one who does so may count this as fulfillment of the almsgiving listed among the prescribed works required to obtain the Indulgence.

On this matter, as on all others, there are those who answer “yes” and those who answer “no,” as can be seen in Benzonio (De Anno Jubilaei, Book 5, Question 3). But the fairest opinion seems to be that an indulgence cannot be obtained through a work that one is otherwise obligated to perform, unless the grantor of the indulgence expressly states that it may be acquired through that work; which often occurs when, among the prescribed works, a three-day fast is required, and the specific days are identified—for example, the three days of the Four Seasons of September. This is the distinction made by Passerino (De Indulgentiis, quaest. 41), among many others, and Father Theodore concurs (De Indulgentiis, vol. 1, chap. 10, art. 6). But even if this were to occur under the terms specified, it is nevertheless not applicable to our present case, in which we are discussing the substitution of visits to the Basilicas with other pious works, since such a substitution cannot be made with other pious works that the penitent would otherwise be obligated to perform; since this is a matter of substitution, and it is an established rule that the substitute must be of the same quality and nature as the work being substituted. Since the visit to the Basilicas was not a work required by any precept, but rather a new obligation imposed to obtain the Indulgence, the work substituted in its place must also be of this same kind and nature.

54\. This is how wisely Costantini argues in the cited passage; he also adds that the Penitentiaries, without compromising their conscience, cannot reduce the number of visits to the basilicas or substitute them with other charitable works, unless there is an impediment or a moral inability to make the aforementioned visit.

55\. Let us conclude this entire subject with the warning given by St. Charles Borromeo in his \*Instructions to Confessors\*, § 16.

Here are the words of the Saint, which would have sufficed for our purpose—both regarding the faculty to grant dispensations and the faculty to commute vows—had we not been compelled to elaborate due to the variety and multiplicity of opinions among the writers: “The Confessor, who possesses some privilege, power, and authority to commute the vows of those who come to confession, should not commute them except for other pious works that are greater and more pleasing to God, or at least equal to them, taking careful account of the expenses, hardships, and other inconveniences they would have suffered had they fulfilled their vows.”

Furthermore, if they possess powers granted by Jubilees or privileges conferred by Apostolic Letters to absolve even the most grave sins, as well as ecclesiastical penalties and censures, they are nevertheless to be advised that they cannot grant a dispensation to those who have incurred irregularities, unless such express mention is made in the said Apostolic Letters.”

Part V

(Regarding the provisions contained in the Constitution “Convocatis,” which are necessary for the Minor Penitentiaries, so that they may make proper use of the powers granted to them)

56\. Among the warnings that begin in no. XXII, the first concerns a priest who, having been authorized to hear confessions, confesses and absolves a person with whom he is an accomplice in a vile and dishonorable sin against the sixth commandment of the Decalogue. Scholars once debated whether such an absolution was valid, and the prevailing opinion was that it was valid, since there was no law establishing its invalidity. There was also debate as to whether the absolution was lawful; and the prevailing opinion leaned toward its lawfulness, since both the penitent and the confessor abhorred the sin committed, and there was no danger of renewed consent.

57\. The bishops, in their concern for the proper governance of the souls entrusted to their care, began by considering that, whatever may be said in the abstract and from the pulpit, in practice it cannot be a good thing, but rather a harmful one, to deprive confessors—even those approved by the Synods themselves—of the jurisdiction to absolve, when it came to absolving an accomplice in a sin against the sixth precept of the Decalogue. And when it then pleased God, through no merit of our own, to elevate us to the Supreme Pontificate, they did not fail to urge us strongly to remedy this impropriety through a general constitution.

58\. We recount the history of this event in our treatise \*De Synodo\* (Book 7, Chapter 14); and at that time We issued the Constitution that begins “\*Sacramentum Poenitentiae\*” (Bullar., Vol. 1, No. 20), in which We deprived any confessor of all authority and jurisdiction to absolve a person complicit in a vile and dishonorable sin against the sixth precept of the Decalogue, in such a way that the absolution granted by him remains null and void, as if granted by one whom We have deprived of the jurisdiction to grant it.

59\. We made an exception for the case of the deathbed and for the case in which there was no other priest who could hear the confession of the dying person; subsequently, we published another Constitution, which begins with \*Apostolici muneris\* (Bullar., vol., no. 20), in which we added that if there were no one other than the complicit priest who could absolve the dying penitent, given the circumstances the complicit priest might absolve the penitent, even if there were another priest to whom, should the dying person confess, this might result in infamy or scandal to the detriment of the complicit priest or the penitent; provided that the infamy were true, and the scandal were also true, and were not merely a pretext on the part of the complicit priest.

We also imposed upon them the responsibility to take all necessary steps to remove the infamy and scandal. As for the complicit priest who hears confessions outside of the article of death and absolves the penitent; or who absolves the penitent in the article of death when another priest is present; or, when another priest is present, who supposes that if the penitent were to confess to that other priest, it would cause infamy or scandal, and therefore hears the confession and absolves the penitent; We have imposed, in the aforementioned Constitutions, the penalty of major excommunication, the lifting of which We have reserved solely to Ourselves and to Our Successors.

60\. With these decisions of ours, therefore, the long-standing questions as to whether the absolution granted by a complicit priest to a penitent who is his accomplice in a sin against the sixth precept of the Decalogue is valid or invalid, lawful or unlawful, are hereby settled. And since, at the time we issued our aforementioned Constitution \*Sacramentum Poenitentiae\*, we considered that—given the broad authority granted to confessors during Jubilees to hear confessions and grant absolution for any grave sin—the question might arise again, if the accomplice confessor were to be restored, for the duration of the Jubilee, the faculty to absolve the penitent accomplice, we declared in the said Constitution that, having been deprived by Us of the jurisdiction to absolve the accomplice, he could not, for that reason, be considered a legitimate and approved confessor: he must therefore, during any Jubilee, remain in his previous status, and thus be disqualified and deprived of the authority to absolve the accomplice in a vile and dishonorable sin. And this is what the first of the warnings we are discussing refers to.

61\. The Instruction for Penitentiaries continues with the Warnings. First, a general rule is established, stipulating that Penitentiaries may not exercise the powers granted to them during the Holy Year except with those penitents who have a serious intention of obtaining the Jubilee and who are prepared to perform the works required to attain it; it then goes on to establish two points: one is that they may not grant any commutation or dispensation outside the act of sacramental Confession, nor outside those basilicas and churches in which they are assigned to hear confessions; and the other is that, under the pretext of the penitent’s good disposition to fulfill the prescribed works and obtain the Jubilee, they must not fail to impose salutary penance during Confession.

62\. As for the general rule, what it establishes is quite clear. In fact, since all the graces granted are directed toward attaining the Jubilee and serve as preparation for it, the consequence is clear: only those who are prepared to receive the Jubilee and are seriously committed to performing the works required to attain it may make use of these graces: “Grace is not granted except in view of this end, and as a means of preparation for the attainment of the Jubilee; therefore, whoever is not disposed to fulfill and obtain the Jubilee cannot enjoy the Privilege.” These are the words of Suárez (De Religione, vol. 2, book 6, chap. 16, n. 6), who discusses the commutation of vows.

Shortly thereafter, in examining whether the obligation arising from a vow commuted during the Jubilee period comes back into effect when the penitent—even though, at the time of confession, he was willing to obtain the Jubilee—subsequently fails to perform the works prescribed to attain it (which will be discussed later), he taught (ibid., n. 11) that if there is no explicit agreement in the commutation that the prescribed works must be performed to obtain the Jubilee, there is nevertheless an implicit agreement: “I say that, even if an explicit agreement is not proven, an implicit one is sufficiently proven—as if inherent and, by the very nature of the matter, included in such an act or ministry—so that it may be carried out correctly and faithfully; nor does there seem to be any doubt that this is the presumed intention of the Pontiff granting such a faculty.”

63\. But while it is clear what is contained in the aforementioned rule, prior to our Declaration, this could not be said to be the first point at which Penitentiaries are required to exercise their faculties in the act of sacramental confession, and not outside of it. The faculties granted to confessors during a Jubilee year encompass many things, namely, the absolution of reserved sins and censures; the authority to commute or dispense vows; and the power to grant dispensations in other matters besides vows, as mentioned above. Regarding the absolution of reserved cases, it is certain that it cannot be granted outside of sacramental confession. As for absolution from censures, the question has arisen as to whether it can be granted outside of sacramental confession.

Sanchez believed that this could be done when the grant of the faculty did not include the clause “eorum Confessionibus diligenter auditis” or any equivalent clause, as can be seen in his works (De Matr., Book 8, Disputation 34, n. 50). Others, however, held more firmly to the view that absolution from censures cannot be granted outside of sacramental confession, since the faculty to absolve from censures is granted as a preliminary provision for the absolution of sins. This is the view of Suárez, Vázquez, Filliuc, and Navarro, followed by Viva (De Jubilaeo, quaest. 10, n. 4). With regard to the commutation of vows, Costantini, in his Treatise on the Holy Year (Part 3, Chapter 3, p. 192), maintains that during the Holy Year Jubilee, such commutation may be granted by those who possess the faculty, even outside of sacramental confession, since he has not found in the Notifications published during past Holy Years the clause “eorum Confessionibus diligenter auditis”; nor does it seem to him that the commutation of vows has any connection with the penitential forum, neither by its nature nor by the will of the one granting the faculty to commute. Finally, others pay no heed whatsoever to the clause “auditis Confessionibus”; which, although placed after the faculty to absolve from reserved cases and censures, and also after the authority to commute vows and grant certain dispensations, would seem to encompass all the matters previously expressed; they teach that the aforementioned clause must refer solely to absolution from reserved cases; or to absolution from censures, for which it provides a special rationale suited to the nature of those matters; but never to the commutation of vows or other dispensations, for which the rationale—which is specific to absolution from reserved cases and censures—does not apply. Bonacina discusses this at length (Oper. Moral., vol. 2, disputation 4, on the second precept of the Decalogue, question 2, point 4, paragraph 2, no. 16); Leandro (De Sacram. Poenitent., part 1, tr. 5, disp. 14, De Indulgentiis, quaest. 97); Diana (coord. ed. Vol. 7, tr. 1, ref. 300, n. 7) and Giribaldo (Tract. 7, On the Sacrament of Penance, ch. 21, On the Jubilee, dub. 6, n. 42 et seq.). But We, in order to eliminate any difficulty, have decreed in the Instruction that Penitentiaries may not grant any absolution, commutation, or dispensation “outside the act of sacramental confession,” as this seemed to Us to be necessary and proportionate to the gravity of the matter and the nature of the ministry; this puts an end to all disputes and is also in accordance with the practice of Our Penitentiary, as can be seen in the \*Thesaurus de poenis Eccles.\* (§ 1, chap. 22).

64\. The next point concerns the penance that the confessor must impose on anyone who confesses to him in order to prepare to receive the Jubilee. This is not, here, the famous question among theologians as to whether, in order to obtain the fruit of the Indulgence, it is sufficient to have made a good Confession, to fulfill the penance imposed by the confessor, and to carry out the prescribed works punctually; or whether it is also necessary to have the proper disposition to satisfy Divine Justice, as far as possible, through other acts of penance. The present point goes further, as it pertains to the penance imposed by the confessor on those who confess to him in preparation for receiving the Jubilee; for there are not only those who have maintained that, on the occasion of the Jubilee, the confessor may impose lighter penances on the penitent if he sees that the penitent is willing to obtain the plenary indulgence, but also those who hold that the confessor may refrain from imposing any penance at all. Such is the view of Cardinal de Lugo (De Poenitentia, disp. 27, sect. 2, n. 30); of Diana (Edit. coord., vol. 1, tr. 6, res. 10, n. 2), along with many others. Others have distinguished between reparatory penances and medicinal penances, arguing that the penitent who has obtained the Jubilee is not obliged to perform reparatory penances, but rather medicinal ones, as can be seen in Viva (De Jubilaeo, quaest. 5, art. 2), Costantini (Treatise on the Holy Year, part 1, chap. 4, § 2), and Leandro (part 1, tract. 5, disp. 9, quaest. 82).

Others even exempt the penitent from fulfilling the medicinal penances imposed by the confessor when they are not absolutely necessary to avoid sin; in such a case, the obligation to fulfill them does not stem from the confessor’s authority, but from natural law, as can be seen in the works of Cardinal de Lugo (De Poenit., disp. 27, sect. 2, n. 21 et seq.); Leandro (Vol. 1, tr. De Sacr. poenit., quaest. 172), and Diana (Edit. coord., Vol. 5, tr. 1, resol. 201, n. 4).

Other theologians have also spoken out against the aforementioned assertions, writing extensively on the subject; among them are Juvenin (Tract. Hist. Dogmatic, Sacram., diff. 13, q. 5, chap. 4), Pontas (Dictionario morali, entry “Jubilaeum,” case 10), and most recently Father Amort (Historia Indulgentiarum in quaestion practicis, p. 467 et seq.), who has meticulously compiled everything that can be said on the subject. Since the testimony of Father Gregory of Valencia—who certainly cannot be counted among those known as rigorists—escaped his attention, We shall add it here. He wrote: “It should be noted that neither through this form of indulgence—namely, \*De injunctis Poenitentiis\*—nor through any other, however broad and general, is the obligation relaxed by which, for the sake of the integrity of the Sacrament, the confessor is bound to impose salutary penance upon the penitent, and the penitent to fulfill it, etc. First, because that obligation is of divine law, etc. Second, because otherwise the Sacrament of Penance would be deprived of an integral part of itself, namely, the penitent’s satisfaction, which is indeed required by divine law. Third, this is confirmed by the common understanding and practice of the Church; for even in the most plenary indulgences, confessors do not neglect to require sacramental satisfaction and penance from penitents; indeed, it is even expressly required by the Popes themselves on such occasions” (Volume IV, Disp. 7, Quest. 20, De Indulgentiis, point 3, p. 1603).

65\. Regarding the first question, as well as the nature of the satisfaction—that is, the penance to be imposed by the confessor—we have spoken at length in Our Circular Letter addressed to the Patriarchs, Archbishops, and Bishops on the preparation for the Holy Year, issued at Castel Gandolfo on June 26 of this year, and we refer to that letter. It remained only to say a few words regarding the Confessor’s obligation not to neglect to impose penance, since it was claimed that the penitent who had obtained the Indulgence was not obliged to fulfill it. And since this seemed to us to be a laxity, in the Instruction we are discussing, in no. XXVI we have explained that the confessor must impose salutary penance on the penitent, even if the latter is prepared to receive the Jubilee. From this arises the penitent’s obligation to fulfill it: “Let him confess all his sins faithfully, and strive to fulfill the penance imposed upon him to the best of his ability”; these are the words of the Lateran Council, when it addresses sacramental confession in the famous Decretal “Omnis utriusque sexus, de poenitentiis et remissionibus.” It should also be emphasized that there is a necessary correlation between the authority to impose penance and the obligation to fulfill it, since it seemed incomprehensible to us that one could be or be called “vere penitens,” as one must be to obtain a plenary indulgence, if, under the pretext of that indulgence, one seeks to exempt oneself from fulfilling the penance imposed by the confessor during sacramental confession.

66\. The Instruction goes on to provide some guidelines regarding absolution from censures for those who, without permission and with malicious intent, enter the cloister of the nuns; for religious who, for the same purpose, bring women into the cloister; for those who read prohibited books without permission; and regarding dispensation from certain vows. Our intention here is merely to allude to the controversies we have sought to resolve, so that matters already settled are not brought up for discussion again. We will now state that, while granting Penitentiaries the authority to absolve from hidden censures incurred for having caused harm to someone, with the proviso that they not make use of this power, it is tantamount to saying that they do not absolve the penitent unless he has compensated the injured party, or at least, unless he promises under oath to do so before the absolution. We do not mean, as some have understood, that the “injured party” refers to the judge, but rather to the one who has suffered the harm; this is the obvious meaning of the words, as has also been well observed by Viva (De Jubilaeo, quaest. 10, art. 2); by Giballino (De censuris, disquisit. 9, quaest. 4, n. 7) and by the Salmaticensi (Cursu Theologico morali, vol. 2, tract. 10, de censuris, chap. 2, punct. 2, n. 20). Furthermore, regarding the commutation of vows involving harm to a third party—a commutation that is prohibited for Penitentiaries—Suarez writes very well on this subject (vol. 2, \*De Religione\*, book 6, chap. 15, and especially n. 7). Regarding the vows that are typically made by some who enter a religious congregation—vows that take on the nature of a contract and a mutual obligation between them and the congregation that receives them— vows from which Penitentiaries cannot grant a dispensation—Father Siro speaks very aptly on this subject in one of his manuscript works on a Bull of Innocent XII concerning the Powers of the Major Penitentiary and penal vows.

Part VI

(Regarding the Powers set forth in the “Convocatis” Constitution for Confessors appointed by the Cardinal Vicar, with respect to absolving sins and censures, commuting vows, and granting dispensations)

67\. What can be recommended here is that these Confessors carefully read the text of their powers, so as not to exceed them. They should also read the section that corresponds to the powers granted to Penitentiaries; they should take note of what was said about them above. With regard to dispensations, they should bear in mind that they have no other authority than to dispense with or substitute visits to the basilicas with other pious works, just as the Penitentiaries do; therefore, they should read what has been said in this regard concerning the Penitentiaries in numbers 52 and 53.

Part VII

(Regarding certain provisions contained in the “Convocatis” Constitution pertaining to this Jubilee; and, first and foremost, the provisions concerning those who wish to obtain it)

68\. The renowned Giovanni de Anania, who died in 1458, wrote a short treatise on the Jubilee; at the end of which he posed several questions, including the following: whether the Jubilee benefits the bishop who comes to Rome to receive it without first obtaining permission from the Pope; whether it benefits the parish priest who comes without the bishop’s permission; whether it benefits the monk who comes without the abbot’s permission; whether it benefits the husband who comes without his wife’s permission; and whether it benefits the wife who comes to Rome without her husband’s permission.

Benzonio, who has been cited several times, continued the examination of these same doubts around 1599 (De Anno Jubilaei, Book 5, from doubt 14 to doubt 18); the conclusion was that it was not lawful for the aforementioned individuals to leave their homelands to come to Rome to celebrate the Jubilee of the Holy Year without prior permission from their superiors. If they come without it, they sin; but if, having come to Rome, they confess this sin and all others with true contrition and do what is prescribed to gain the Indulgence, they gain it.

Quarti (Treatise on the Jubilee of the Holy Year, p. 67, chap. 1, point 6, question 1 et seq.) agrees with these same principles; he merely adds that a wife cannot come without her husband’s permission and consent; however, the husband may go without his wife’s consent, provided that his absence is not of long duration or causes serious inconvenience or harm to his wife and family; quite rightly, he raises his voice against the religious and parish priests who, without the necessary permissions, undertake the journey to Rome, to obtain the Jubilee of the Holy Year, since it must be understood that the general invitation extended by the Supreme Pontiff to all applies only to those who can come lawfully and under the proper circumstances. Costantini concurs with this opinion in his Treatise on the Holy Year (Part 2, Chapter 4, p. 103 ff.). In our aforementioned Encyclical Letter of June 26 of this year on the preparation for the Holy Year, we have, in accordance with the aforementioned opinions, established the response to these points, as can be read in the same document. We refer to the Encyclical Letter from which the first statement in no. XLIII necessarily follows, namely, that “personis Romam ad hoc Jubilaeum consequendum venire volentibus” does not imply the freedom to come without a license and consent, which is, moreover, a necessary requirement of the Superiors.

69\. Pope Boniface VIII, in his oft-cited Decretal “Antiquorum,” among the common Extravagantes “de poenitentiis et remissionibus,” established that Romans must visit the sacred basilicas thirty times, and pilgrims and foreigners fifteen times: “If they are Romans, at least thirty days, whether consecutive or intermittent, and at least once a day; if, however, they are pilgrims or visitors from out of town, they shall visit the same basilicas in the same manner for fifteen days.” These same words are found in another Decretal of Clement VI, which begins “Unigenitus.”

The same language was used by Pope Clement VII in the proclamation of the Jubilee through the papal bull “Inter solicitudines,” issued on December 17, 1524. Over time, the phrase “vel Urbis incolae” was added to the word “Romani,” and the word “Forenses” was removed, as can be seen in the Constitution of Gregory XIII, which begins: “Dominus ac Redemptor: For thirty consecutive or intermittent days, at least once a day, if they are Romans or inhabitants of the City; if, however, they are pilgrims, after having devoutly visited the site for fifteen days,” and in many others.

70\. Although the word “Romans” might seem beyond dispute, this was not the case; for a Roman is not only one who was born and resides in Rome, but also one who, having been born in Rome, has moved his residence elsewhere, there was doubt as to whether, when such a person came to Rome during the Holy Year to gain the Jubilee, he should visit the basilicas thirty or fifteen times. Navarro (De Jubilaeo et Indulgentiis, n. 42, p. 163) was of the opinion that fifteen visits would suffice, since in this case domicile should take precedence over origin, all the more so because a Roman who has his domicile elsewhere and comes to Rome undergoes the same inconvenience of travel as a foreigner or pilgrim. Benzonio (De Anno Jubilaei, Book 5, Question 3), on the contrary, believes he is obligated to make thirty visits: “For it is shameful for a Roman man to be weighed down by avarice, idleness, or physical infirmity, especially in that Year in which the entire City shines with joy, jubilation, generosity, and the most extraordinary festivity.”

Furthermore, the question arises as to whether the term “Roman”—and for the purposes at hand—also includes those who live in the vicinity of Rome.

71\. The disputes over the word “Romano” were followed by others concerning the words: “vel Urbis incolae.” Navarro, in the cited passage, no. 43, states that the term “incolae” in this context refers to those who have resided in Rome for a year, or at least long enough to be considered inhabitants of Rome. Quarti and Costantini offer practical interpretations. The former (pp. 126 ff.) maintains that the term “incolae Urbis” refers to those who were neither born in Rome nor native to it, but who have established their domicile there, and that therefore curials, procurators, and lawyers, and courtiers be subject to the thirty visits, and that students, merchants, and litigants who reside in Rome for most of the year be subject to the same obligation, since all of these have established, if not a true domicile, at least a quasi-domicile. The second (pp. 119 ff.), considering that pilgrims are required to make only fifteen visits due to the inconvenience suffered during the journey, while Romans and residents of Rome are required to make the greater number of thirty visits, since they have not endured the aforementioned hardship and discomfort, states that all those who have not suffered the hardship of the journey are subject to the obligation of thirty visits. Furthermore, all those who have endured the hardships of the journey are exempt from the heavier obligation and are required to fulfill the lighter one, namely, fifteen visits.

72\. In our Constitution “Peregrinantes,” in which we proclaimed the Holy Year, we used the following words: “For thirty consecutive or intermittent days, whether Romans or residents of the City; whether they be true pilgrims or other outsiders, have devoutly visited the city for at least fifteen such days.” In explaining these words in our latest Constitution, at no. XLIV, we stated—and now repeat— that the term “Romans” encompasses all those who were born and reside in Rome, or who were born and reside in the district of Rome—that is to say, in the vineyards within five miles of the City—since this is the legal meaning of the word “Roman,” as Spondano very aptly reflects (Epitome of the Annals of Cardinal Baronius, at the beginning of Year 1): “The jurists replied that those born in the surrounding areas of the City are to be understood as having been born in Rome; for Rome is considered to be wherever its surrounding buildings are located; nor should Rome be regarded as confined by the city wall, as can be understood from daily custom; for when we say we are going to Rome, even if we live outside the city.” We have said and repeat that the term “incolae Urbis” refers to all those who have come to Rome with the intention of residing there for most of the year, and all those who, while in Rome for some employment, or to find employment, even if they do not establish a true and permanent domicile, at least establish a quasi-domicile: “God chose our fathers from among the people of Israel and exalted the people when they were sojourners in the land of Egypt, and with a mighty hand He brought them out of it”: as recorded in the “Acts of the Apostles” (Acts 13:17). And the learned scholars of the Latin language say that “incolae” are those who, though born elsewhere, dwell in a place where they have chosen to live, even if they are not citizens of that place; therefore, among them must be counted courtiers, attorneys, lawyers, court officials, scholars, merchants, litigants, and other such people. This was also well noted by the ancient commentator on the Decretal “Antiquorum, de poenitentiis et remissionibus, inter extravagantes communes, in verbis Forenses,” who used the following words: “As for the courtiers residing in the Papal Curia,” Pope Sixtus replies, “they must perform the penance for thirty days. And the reason is that these individuals are neither truly pilgrims nor truly ‘forenses,’ but rather come to the Curia to stay.” All those mentioned above are therefore required to visit the basilicas thirty times.

We have declared, and hereby declare, that those who come to Rome for reasons other than to obtain the Jubilee are also required to make the same number of visits; for, leaving aside the controversy as to whether such persons may be included under the term “incolarum urbis,” it is certain that, having endured the inconvenience of the journey for another reason, they may not be eligible to receive the Jubilee unless they visit the Basilicas thirty times. The same applies to all those who, coming to Rome for the purpose of earning the Jubilee, remain there for more than six months, since they could, without significant inconvenience, visit the basilicas thirty times during that period. We have followed the example of our most recent Predecessors by eliminating the word “Forenses,” which, according to medieval language, could only refer to foreigners, as Luca Holstenio rightly observes (vol. 8, Collectionis Labbeanae Concilior., p. 138), in his commentary on the words of the ninth-century Roman Synod under Leo IV: “Tantam superfluitatem Presbyterorum forensium.” We have made use of the ancient word “peregrini,” which refers to those who travel to visit the Holy Thresholds and obtain the holy Indulgence: “Advena sum, et peregrinus apud vos” is written in Genesis (Gen 23:4). To the word “peregrini” we have added the term “externi,” which—not only according to proper Latin usage but, more importantly, according to the language of the Holy Scriptures—means a stranger or someone from another country; hence, in “Exodus, chapter 23,” we read: “You shall not swear by the names of foreign gods, nor shall such a name be heard from your lips” (Ex 23:13); and in “Numbers, chapter 1”: “Whoever of the foreigners comes near shall be put to death” (Num 1:51); and in chapter 3: “The foreigner who comes near to offer a sacrifice shall die” (Num 3:10). Returning to our subject, we have stated, and we hereby declare, that the term “peregrini” or “externi” encompasses all those who, according to the explanation just given, are not included under the term “Romani” or “incolae Urbis.”

Part VIII

(On the provisions contained in the “Convocatis” Constitution regarding the mandated works)

73\. It would undoubtedly be desirable, better and safer, if, before beginning the visit to the churches, one were to make a fruitful confession first, so as to make the visits in a state of grace, and also to make a new confession if anyone, once the visits have begun, were to fall into any mortal sin. In the “Acts of the Church of Milan, Part 7” (Milan edition of 1599, p. 1031) by the great St. Charles Borromeo, we read: “Above all, everyone must diligently observe the conditions set forth by His Holiness in the Letter granting the Jubilee, for those who do not observe them will not receive it. The first is to be truly contrite and to have gone to confession; this everyone must do before beginning to visit the churches, to ensure with greater certainty that they will attain the Holy Jubilee. For the same reason, if anyone, after having confessed and begun to visit the churches, should fall (which God forbid) into any mortal sin, must confess that mortal sin and then continue for the remaining days—whatever number of days he has left—until he has visited the four churches.”

74\. This system is endorsed—and considered more reliable—by St. Antoninus (Summa, Part 1, Title 10, Chapter 3, § 5 at the end); by Cardinal Bellarmine (De Indulgentiis, Book 1, Chapter 13, Controversies, Vol. 2); by Becano (Summa, Title on the Sacraments, Chapter 28, on Indulgences, Question 6); by Benzonio (De Anno Jubilaei, Book 5, Doubt 4, § 7), and by Pontas (In verb. Indulgentiae, Case 13). And that this has been, and remains, our earnest desire can be sufficiently understood from what we have written in the aforementioned Encyclical; from what we have done, having organized public missions in this city last summer; from what we are having done during the current Advent season, continuing until the feast of St. Thomas the Apostle, in various churches throughout Rome; all of which have as their sole purpose and objective fruitful confessions to be made by sinners before the Holy Door is opened, and consequently before the visitation of the churches begins; not to mention that this desire of ours can also be inferred from the Constitution \*Peregrinantes\*, which stipulates that Confession and Communion must precede the visit to the churches.

75\. But that is not the only difficulty. The main difficulty arises if someone begins and continues the visit to the basilicas while in a state of mortal sin; thus, a good work in itself is performed, but by a sinner. Then, according to the rite, the sinner makes a fruitful Confession and receives Holy Communion in a state of grace. It follows that he has visited the churches while in a state of sin and has completed the final act in a state of grace in order to obtain the Jubilee. The question arises as to whether the visits made previously can be considered sufficient for the purpose of fulfilling the required works.

Navarro discusses this point at length in “De Jubilaeo, note 19”; but the most common opinion is that visits made while in a state of sin are sufficient to obtain the Indulgence, provided that the final act by which the Indulgence is acquired is performed in a state of grace; temporal punishment is not remitted unless the sin is forgiven; remission of eternal punishment is obtained through Penance. This is the view expressed by the most authoritative theologians in their theological works, namely Suárez, Valenza, Cardinal de Lugo, Silvio, Estio, Juvenin, and many others. Those known as moral theologians also agree, including Filliuc, Santarelli, La Croix, Bonacina, and others who have specifically addressed the Jubilee, including Viva and Amort, as well as those who have composed and published treatises on indulgences in Rome, such as Fathers Passerino, Vanranst, and Teodoro. This view, which may be called the common opinion, has a sound foundation; since visits to churches, even when made by those who are not in a state of grace, are morally good works—though not meritorious for eternal life, and though they are the works of an enemy—they are nevertheless the works of an enemy who is on the path to reconciliation with God. In sacred antiquity, this seems to be foreshadowed in the public penances performed by those who had fallen into serious and public excesses, and who, before obtaining absolution—that is, while still in a state of sin—would engage in the performance of the works and penances prescribed for them. Having established the principle that the state of grace is absolutely necessary for the final act performed to obtain the Indulgence, and that this state is not absolutely necessary when performing the other prescribed works, the aforementioned authors invoke the public custom of the faithful, which is well-known and not disapproved of by those who grant the Indulgence; since it is impossible for him to believe that the visits—made either thirty or fifteen times on alternate days to obtain the Jubilee—are performed by anyone other than those in a state of grace. Nor are they in the least swayed from this conviction by the fact that the Bull of the Universal Jubilee stipulates Confession and Communion in connection with the visit to the Basilicas; they reply that the order of words is not an adequate rule for the order of facts; and they add that in Indulgences, the authority of the grantor, the grace of the recipient, and piety in the cause are sufficient; visits to the basilicas are pious, even if they are not made by those in a state of grace, provided they are carried out under the proper circumstances and with the appropriate dispositions to obtain habitual grace.

76\. This is a summary of the arguments put forward by the defenders of this ruling, from which we do not intend to deviate in the present case, as can be seen from what we stated in No. XLV of the latest Constitution. In fact, although on the one hand We feel an ever more ardent desire that visits to churches be made in a state of grace—either after a fruitful Confession or at least after an act of contrition— on the other hand, considering that one cannot always achieve what one desires and that it is necessary to have compassion for human frailty, We have deemed it appropriate not to exclude from the fruit of the Indulgence those who begin or continue visiting the churches without having first gone to Confession, provided that they are in a state of grace when performing the final act by which the indulgence is obtained; and provided that the visits are made with devotion. “Devote visitaverint,” reads the Bull “Peregrinantes.” In the Extravagant Unigenitus, the word “devotionis” is found; in the other, Quemadmodum, the word “reverenter.” Navarro, explaining these words, says: “This signifies that these visits to churches must be made properly and indeed correctly; for although, as has been established in accordance with common teaching, it is not necessary that they be made while in a state of grace, it is nevertheless necessary that they be morally good acts.” It is therefore necessary, in order to fulfill the prescribed duty of these visits, that the visit be made with the intention and desire to honor God or His saints; that one go to and enter the basilicas with modesty; and that one perform some act of devotion. From this it follows that if one goes to churches without any good purpose, but out of mere curiosity, and if one goes merely to take, as they say, a stroll, one does not earn the Jubilee. It goes without saying that this applies to anyone who visits a church while in a state of actual mortal sin—for example, with the intention of inducing others to sin. Vanranst, Costantini, Quarti, Viva, Pignatelli, Amort, and many others have identified these and other similar cases.

77\. The precept of Holy Confession, as it pertains to Divine Law, applies only to mortal sins and does not extend to venial sins. Since the Church, at the Lateran Council, established the time for confession as Easter Sunday, there is debate as to whether the requirement to confess mortal sins must be supplemented by the requirement to also confess venial sins. Although it is commonly said that the obligation to confess venial sins as well was not added to fulfill the Easter precept, there is no shortage of authors who, filled with zeal, hold the opposite view, and teach that anyone who has only venial sins should, at least at Easter, present themselves to the priest to let him know that, by the grace of God, they have only venial sins. See Juvenin, Du Hamel, Habert, and Pontas. We will not enter into this controversy. St. Antoninus expressly teaches in his \*Summa\* that it is a very praiseworthy thing, in order to obtain the indulgence, to confess venial sins, provided the penitent has no mortal sins. But even this was not sufficient for our purpose, since the point is not about what is useful, but about what is necessary. The crux of the matter boils down to the question debated among theologians: if Confession is prescribed for the attainment of the Jubilee, is it also prescribed for those who have no mortal sins, but only venial ones? In Santarelli, one can find a compilation of authors from both the affirmative and negative sides. Those who profess to hold the affirmative opinion maintain that it is the surer one. Cardinal de Lugo and Leandro take the negative side, on the grounds that when one speaks of Confession, one always means the Confession of mortal sins. However, there is no lack of authority to require the confession of venial sins, as Cardinal di Lauria and Clericato very well point out, substantiating this with the text of Pope Clement V in the Clementine, which begins “Ne in agro, de statu Monachorum,” in which he imposes on monks the obligation to go to confession at least once a month, even though one could reasonably assume that a large number of them would have only venial sins. It is also certain that, for the sake of obtaining the Jubilee, works are prescribed that, by their very nature, would be purely advisory and supererogatory—such as, for example, fasting on certain days not prescribed by ecclesiastical precept, or devoutly visiting the basilicas, as is the case in the present Jubilee; Confession of venial sins may also be prescribed for those who have no mortal sins, even though, setting aside these circumstances and speaking in the abstract, there would be no obligation to confess venial sins. We have done so, and in No. XLVI of the latest Constitution we have declared that, in order to gain the Jubilee, even those who have only venial sins are obliged to go to Confession. “The Pope could, if he wished, expressly require that anyone wishing to obtain the Indulgence must confess venial sins, just as he requires other supererogatory works; yet this would not make venial sins a necessary subject of confession; rather, since venial sins are otherwise a suitable subject for confession, he might also require them, if he so wished, for the acquisition of the Indulgence.” These are the words of Benzonio.

78\. In short, the question (which arises among theologians as to whether, when Confession is required to obtain an indulgence, one who has only venial sins is obliged to go to Confession) applies when Confession is required as a disposition to the state of grace necessary to gain the Jubilee, but not when Confession is prescribed—as We have in fact done—as a work required to obtain the Indulgence, as Pasqualigo, Viva, and Costantini very rightly observe in the works cited.

79\. Following the difficulty already resolved, another arises concerning one who, having already made Confession and visited the Basilicas several times while in a state of grace, falls into a new mortal sin. There is a dispute among the Doctors as to what such a person must do to obtain the Jubilee: whether he must repeat the visits already made, or whether it is sufficient to make an act of contrition before the final visit, or whether he is obliged to confess the sin committed. Some have held that an act of contrition is sufficient, since the Jubilee Bull prescribes only a single Confession (which, in this case, is assumed to have already been fulfilled) and the state of grace necessary to obtain the Indulgence is acquired through a valid act of contrition. This is the view of Cardinal de Lugo and Leandro, who cite other authors of the same opinion. However, others—and especially Suárez, Filliuc, Quarti, and Costantini—consider that in the Bull, Confession refers to the Indulgence, and that consequently it requires the confession of mortal sins committed up to the moment the fruit of the Indulgence is obtained; it is reasonably argued that whoever has confessed and begun visiting the churches, if they fall into mortal sin before the final visit, must confess again: an act of contrition alone is not sufficient to obtain the Jubilee. The same obligation to repeat Confession also applies to anyone who recalls a mortal sin omitted through innocent forgetfulness in a Confession already made. We have adhered to this more well-founded ruling in No. XLVII, absolving the sinner from the obligation to repeat the visits already made to the basilicas, and leaving him subject to the other obligation of having to go to confession before making the final visit to said basilicas. It has been said that we have relieved him of the burden of repeating visits already made, since the question of this obligation arises only when one accepts the view that requires the state of grace at the time the prescribed works are performed; but not when the requirement of the state of grace is limited to the final work, through which the indulgence is obtained, as Benzonio astutely observes in relation to our discussion.

80\. During this Jubilee Year, as mentioned above and as established in the Bull \*Peregrinantes\*, we have included Holy Communion among the prescribed works. Since some, due to their age and natural capacity, are permitted to receive Confession but not Communion, we anticipated that a discussion would arise as to whether the aforementioned individuals are capable of obtaining the Jubilee. This question has already been addressed by the authors in all other indulgences, in which Holy Communion is prescribed as a required work. In this controversy, some have argued that those in question, not being capable of receiving Holy Communion, remain unable to obtain the Jubilee; since physical incapacity, or any other legitimate impediment, does not benefit those who cannot, or are prevented from, performing the prescribed work necessary to obtain the Indulgence. This is the argument put forward by Lavorio, without him or others of the same party being supported by the confessor’s authority to substitute the prescribed works for those who are prevented from performing them. In fact, this faculty does not include the substitution of Holy Communion, because it is restricted to those who are accidentally impeded (such as nuns, prisoners, and the sick, who cannot visit the basilicas, or the poor who cannot give alms, when this is counted among the prescribed works) and cannot be extended to those who, not by accident but by reason, are incapable of doing what is prescribed in the Jubilee Bull. This is the opinion of Passerino, Cardinal de Lugo, and Vanranst. There have been those who, acting as voluntary interpreters of the minds of the Supreme Pontiffs, have maintained that the prescribed work of receiving Communion does not apply to those who, due to their age, are unable to receive Communion, since this is a condition that is de jure impossible for them; or that it must be assumed the Pope grants confessors the faculty to substitute Communion with another pious work for the aforementioned individuals, even though he has not expressly stated so. The names of these individuals can be found in Passerino’s work. But We, to resolve this dilemma, in No. XLVIII have granted confessors the authority to substitute the prescribed act of receiving Communion with some other pious work for children who have not yet been admitted to First Communion and who, in the judgment of the parish priest or confessor, are not capable of being admitted to said First Communion during the Holy Year.

81\. The Constitution \*Convocatis\* includes, under the chapter on declarations, a few other minor provisions intended to resolve disputes that arose in the past. The first, in No. XLIX, states that it is not necessary, in order to obtain the Indulgence by visiting the Basilicas, to enter or exit through the Holy Door; since this is not stipulated in any Constitution, although entering and exiting in this manner should be considered a supererogatory act; a point that was also rightly noted by Passerino, Quarti, and Pignatello.

82\. The second case concerns situations in which, during the Holy Year, an indulgence is granted—as is sometimes customary—to reduce the prescribed number of visits to the churches. The question was raised as to whether, if someone had already visited the Basilicas, they could count those visits toward the number of visits specified in the Indult. It was also asked whether, having completed the number of visits specified in the Indult before it was issued, a person could avail themselves of the Indult without making any further visits. All these difficulties have been resolved in No. L.: anyone who visited the Basilicas at least once before the Indult was published may count those visits toward the number specified in the Indult; those who, prior to the Indult, had already completed the number of visits specified therein may benefit from the Indult, provided they make another visit to the four Basilicas on the same day; this point was also mentioned by the aforementioned Passerino, Quarti, and Pignatello.

83\. The third is as follows. Our Constitution \*Peregrinantes\* establishes that visits to the Basilicas must be devout, and that to obtain the Indulgence, one must pray to the Lord God for the exaltation of His Church, the eradication of heresies, harmony among Catholic Princes, the health and peace of the Christian people; but it does not specify—nor has it been specified in the other Constitutions of our Predecessors in which Holy Years were proclaimed—whether the prayer must be spoken aloud or whether a silent prayer suffices; a matter that has at other times given rise to controversy. Viva believed that mental prayer was sufficient; Costantini said it was probable that mental prayer would suffice, but that vocal prayer was more certain; Quarti held that either one would suffice; Cardinal de Lugo and Amort held that it is safer to add vocal prayer to mental prayer; Vanranst maintained that mental prayer alone is not sufficient, nor is vocal prayer alone if it is “notably brief.” Viva and the others mentioned also speak out against excessive brevity in prayer, because although a brief prayer, offered with great fervor, may satisfy the intent of the Bull \*Peregrinantes\*, nevertheless, the brevity of the prayer usually stems from a lack of devotion or affection for the spiritual exercises, or from negligence and tedium. And We, without in any way departing from these considerations, have declared in no. LI that pious vocal prayer is sufficient to obtain the Indulgence; those who pray mentally are to be commended, provided, however, that they also add some vocal prayer.

Part IX

(On the Repeated Purchase of the Jubilee, and Other Statements Regarding the Privileges Granted for the Current Jubilee)

84\. There is a long-standing question as to whether, during the Holy Year, if someone has devoutly visited the Basilicas thirty times, as prescribed to obtain the Indulgence—having also gone to confession and received Communion, and therefore being able to assume that they have obtained the fruit of the Jubilee—can obtain it again by visiting the prescribed number of basilicas once more, and by going to confession and receiving Communion again. This controversy was raised by Giovanni d’Anania in his short treatise on the Holy Year and was later summarized by others. Filliuc is said to have been of the opinion that, by repeating the prescribed works, one could obtain the Jubilee anew, but he later abandoned this view, believing that the opposite had been decided by the Congregation of the Council in the year 1620.

Vanranst maintains that the Jubilee cannot be acquired a second time, even if the prescribed works are fulfilled. Navarro, considering that there is nothing in the Bulls of the Holy Year that precludes a new acquisition of the Jubilee, supports the view in favor of those who repeat the prescribed works; Benzonio agrees with Navarro, and this can be said to be the prevailing view, as can be seen in Viva, Costantini, and Quarti. Costantini also reports having met with Pope Urban VIII and states that the declaration by the Congregation of the Council cited by Filliuc is not authentic.

We deemed it appropriate to state in No. LII our opinion on this controversy, which has been raised on other occasions, and having considered that this is not a two-week Jubilee, but a Jubilee lasting an entire year; it does not involve prescribed works that can be performed multiple times in a single day, as is the case when a plenary indulgence is granted to those who visit a particular church on specific days (in which case, even if the church is visited multiple times in a single day, only one indulgence may be gained per day, in accordance with the Decree of the Congregation of Indulgences expressly approved by the blessed memory of Innocent XI and legally promulgated on March 7, 1678, which reads as follows: “Semel dumtaxat in die plenariam Indulgentiam in certos dies Ecclesiam visitantibus concessam, vel aliud pium opus peragentibus, lucrifieri”), but this concerns numerous visits to basilicas located at a distance from one another, which cannot be made except over many days apart; we had no difficulty in declaring that the Sacred Indulgence could be obtained multiple times during the Holy Year by repeating the prescribed works. It has been stated that the Indulgence can be acquired anew by repeating the prescribed works. But since during the Jubilee other favors and graces are added to the Indulgence, we have declared in the same Constitution, and in the same paragraph, that whoever has already participated in it—when they first received the Jubilee—cannot participate again, if, after first obtaining the Jubilee, they have again incurred censures, or committed reserved offenses, or require new commutations of vows or dispensations.

85\. When the aforementioned Constitution \*Convocatis\* addressed the privileges granted to the minor penitentiaries of the basilicas, it made explicit mention in No. VII of the faculty to absolve from manifest heresy, while restricting this faculty within certain limits set forth in the aforementioned paragraph. But since, in the faculties granted to the confessors appointed by the Cardinal Vicar—especially for the Holy Year—a broad faculty to absolve from censures reserved in the “Bulla Coenae” was granted in No. XXXVI, and since some have claimed that from this broad authority one could also infer the authority to absolve from the extrinsic heresy mentioned in the said Bull “in Coena Domini,” we have deemed it appropriate to declare—as we do in No. LIII, that this is not sufficient; for such a faculty must be expressly named, nor can it ever be presumed to have been granted by virtue of any broadly worded provision; as was established by our Predecessor Alexander VII in a Decree he issued during a Congregation of the Holy Office held in his presence: “Our Most Holy Lord Pope Alexander VII, on March 23, 1656, adhering to the declarations previously made by his Predecessors, in order to remove every cause for doubt, and so that there might never be any hesitation regarding this matter in the future, since the crime of heresy, graver than all others, is worthy of special note, he decreed that the faculty to absolve from heresy shall not be deemed to be included in Jubilees or other similar concessions, unless the faculty to absolve from heresy is expressly granted.” We have dealt extensively with the controversy that existed prior to the Decree, and with the controversy entirely resolved by the Decree, in our Notification published while we were residing in Bologna, which is the fourth in the Italian and Latin editions.

86\. It has been stated above that those who possess the faculties granted during the Holy Year may avail themselves of them only with regard to those penitents who sincerely wish to obtain the Jubilee and who come to confession with a genuine intention to perform the works prescribed for obtaining it. A question remained regarding one who, having had the good will to do what was required, subsequently repents and fails to fulfill the obligations: whether such a person falls back under the censures from which he was absolved, and whether the modifications of vows and dispensations already obtained are thereby considered to have ceased. However, since absolution from censures was not granted—nor is it granted “cum reincidentia” in the case at hand—but is granted absolutely, in the same Constitution \*Convocatis\* no. LIV, we have declared that the aforementioned person is not bound by the censures from which he was acquitted, nor is he deprived of the grace of the commutations and dispensations obtained. Thus, after citing the most ancient authors, Giribaldi and Viva discuss the matter. And although the first of these authors exempts from mortal sin one who, having changed his mind, fails to do what remains to be done to attain the Jubilee, the opinion of the second—who considers him guilty of mortal sin, citing Suárez, Vásquez, and Filliuc, on the grounds of a serious contravention of the mind and intention of the one who granted the faculties to the confessors—having granted them as a means to attain the Jubilee—and since the penitent has obligated himself to perform the remaining prescribed works, at the very moment he received absolution from the censures and accepted the favors and graces granted on the occasion of the Jubilee; as has also been written above.

Part X

(Regarding the faculties of the other confessors in Rome who are not among the Penitentiaries, nor among those specifically appointed by the Cardinal Vicar for the Holy Year)

87\. In addition to the ordinary Penitentiaries of the Basilicas, those appointed to assist them on the occasion of the Holy Year, and the Confessors also appointed for the Holy Year by the Cardinal Vicar, there are also other ordinary confessors in Rome, as mentioned in the Bull \*Convocatis\*, so that they may be able to assist in attending to the penitents who will come to Rome for the Holy Year.

88\. These ordinary clergy fall into two categories: some are Regulars appointed by their Superiors, and Regular Prelates authorized to hear the confessions of their Regular subjects; other Regulars have, in addition, the approval of the Cardinal Vicar to hear the confessions of Seculars. To the latter, in the Constitution \*Convocatis\*, no. XLI, all the faculties granted to confessors appointed by the Cardinal Vicar of Rome for the Holy Year have been conferred, with respect to their regular penitent colleagues; furthermore, they have been granted the authority to dispense the aforementioned from any irregularity they may have incurred due to the violation of censures, provided such violation is concealed; and this applies throughout the Holy Year; provided, however, that the aforementioned Regulars approved by the Cardinal Vicar to hear the confessions of seculars, may not avail themselves of the said faculty to absolve and grant dispensations, except in regard to their own Regulars, who, in case of need, may turn to them, but not to the others simply appointed by their Superiors to hear the confessions of Regulars.

89\. Then there are the ordinary confessors of the secular clergy, who hear the confessions of laypeople with the usual approval and license from the Cardinal Vicar, but who have not been specifically appointed by him for the Holy Year. Nothing is added to or taken away from these, provided they possess some legitimate authority to absolve and dispense. The same applies to members of religious orders who, with the approval of the Cardinal Vicar, hear the confessions of laypeople; it is not Our intention to either add to or take away from them, with regard to lay penitents, any other legitimate authority they may possess by other titles. Our suspensive constitution, “Cum Nos nuper,” applies only to confessors who are outside Rome, whose faculties are suspended during the Holy Year, whether they were granted on account of, or in connection with, the Indulgences, or for any other distinct reason not pertaining to the Indulgences, as has been established elsewhere in this same letter; the aforementioned Constitution “Cum Nos nuper” does not include the confessors of the city of Rome, which needs a good number of workers to cultivate the Lord’s vineyard during the Holy Year.

90\. The point is that these confessors must undergo a thorough examination regarding the powers they claim to possess; for these must derive from legitimate, unrevoked privileges that are currently in use, in accordance with the Decree of the late Pope Clement XI dated May 3, 1711, issued for the Confessors of Rome and published by the late Cardinal Petra in the Roman edition.

91\. And this, O beloved Sons, is what we have deemed necessary to set forth for you, so that, as a result of our efforts—having summarized the resolution of the major controversies that arose on other occasions and were settled by our Constitutions issued for the Holy Year— you may be able—as we earnestly urge you—to fulfill your ministry faithfully. To this end, we believe our other Encyclical Letter of June 26 of this year, concerning preparations for the Holy Year, may also contribute; we likewise recommend that you read and reflect upon it. Nor should you be at all surprised if, in this present Letter of ours, we have drawn chiefly upon modern authors, not having imitated the head of the household, who draws from his own treasure “nova et vetera,” since—as the controversies have been raised mostly by modern authors, and by those who have written on the Holy Year—it seemed more appropriate to us to draw upon them in particular.

In the meantime, as we embrace you with all the warmth of a father’s heart, we impart our Apostolic Blessing to you.

Given in Rome, at Santa Maria Maggiore, on December 3, 1749, the tenth year of Our Pontificate.
