**La Santa Sede**

ENCYCLICAL MAGNO CUM ANIMI OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Primate, the Archbishops, and the Bishops of the Kingdom of Poland. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brother, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. It is with great sorrow in Our heart that We have learned that in quite a few of your dioceses, controversies and painful disagreements have arisen concerning private oratories. You have taken every care and shown every diligence to eliminate such abuses, but you have been disappointed at not having achieved the desired result; indeed, you have incurred the indignation of many, thereby drawing upon yourselves blame and the accusation of having exceeded the bounds of severity. You desire that this Holy See provide you with clear guidelines regarding the use of private oratories, so that, once the abuses have been removed, your provisions, upheld by papal authority, will not only be immune from any criticism by ill-wishers but will also gain ever greater weight in honor and esteem. We could very easily have fulfilled your request by recommending books to you—both those that treat the doctrine on this matter in accordance with the decrees of the Roman Pontiffs, our predecessors, and those whose authors, though dealing with private oratories, make no mention of the Decrees of the Holy See or ignore them because they are contrary to their theses. However, since we have great and special affection for you, through this Encyclical Letter of ours, as briefly as possible, we will set forth for you the judgments of our Predecessors and our own on this subject. In truth, in such rulings, apart from the obedience due to them by virtue of the authority from which they spring, as from a source, nothing is more important than the subject matter of which they deal and which we shall now address. Any discussion of this kind, however, since it concerns Apostolic Privileges (the interpretation of which, under private law, is reserved to the Supreme Pontiffs), must be guided by the intention of the one who granted the privilege, as the one who, more than anyone else, understands its meaning. Therefore, any regulations you issue and establish in accordance with the norms contained in this Letter must be regarded, as far as their implementation is concerned, as a Decree of the Supreme Pontiffs. For this reason, the title and authority of Apostolic Delegates are to be considered conferred upon Your Excellencies, and We, to the extent necessary, hereby grant and confer them upon you for this purpose. 1. In truth, nothing has been brought to our attention regarding the chapels you have in your Episcopal Palaces for the celebration of Mass by yourselves or by others, or for the performance of any other sacred function proper to your office and dignity. Addressing this matter would be very easy for Us were it not irrelevant to our present case. We shall therefore consider two ancient examples of chapels that bishops had in their episcopal sees, distinct and separate from the public churches, and in which they celebrated the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass. The first example is that of the chapel of Saint Cassius, Bishop of Narni, of whom Saint Gregory (Homily 37 \*Super Evangelia\*) recounts that, although he was gravely ill, “he celebrated Mass in the oratory of his bishop’s palace” to satisfy both his own devotion and that of those who came to visit him. The other example is that of Saint John the Almsgiver, Bishop of Alexandria. As recorded in his Acts, written by Leontius, when rebuking those who left before Mass was over when he went to celebrate it in the public church, he was in the habit of saying: “I go to the holy church for your sake; for my own sake, I could celebrate Mass in the bishop’s palace.” This example can be found in Tomassino (De veteri ac nova Ecclesiae disciplina, part 1, book 2, chapter 93, no. 6). Other examples are collected in the work entitled \*De Oratoriis privatis\* (in chapter 6). (This work was recently published in Rome by Giovanni Battista Gattico, Canon of the Lateran.) These examples provide solid evidence of the bishops’ right to have their own chapels in the episcopal palaces. 2. The Sacred Council of Trent did not in any way derogate from this right; only in Session 22, \*De observandis et evitandis in celebratione Missae\*, due to numerous and undignified incidents that repeatedly occurred, did it prescribe that bishops should not permit priests—whether secular or religious—to celebrate Mass “in private homes” or outside public churches or oratories designated for divine worship. This provision does not apply to chapels located within the bishops’ palaces, which can never be considered private homes. This has been declared on several occasions by the Congregation of Our Venerable Brothers, the Cardinals of the Holy Roman Church and Interpreters of the Council of Trent itself, in certain Resolutions of that same Council, and they have supported this declaration with valid reasons, which We have cited in our treatise \*De Sacrificio Missae\* (sess. 2, § 45 et seq.), Latin edition, Padua. Indeed, when, before having ascended to the highest Dignity, We signed certain works, We considered it Our chief pride not to deviate in any respect from the rulings of the Tribunal of the Roman Curia, inasmuch as they derive their authority above all from papal declarations. 3. The only thing that could be said about this privilege was that, since it was limited to the chapels located within the bishops’ residences, it certainly could not benefit the bishops when they were staying outside their own residences, or when they were conducting a pastoral visit, or when they were traveling. In such cases, if they wished to celebrate Mass or attend it, they were compelled to go to public churches or to ask the local Ordinaries for permission to have Mass celebrated or to celebrate it themselves in the residence where they happened to be staying. 4. Indeed, our predecessor of venerable memory, Pope Boniface VIII, in his Decretal \*Quoniam Episcopi, de privilegiis, issued in Setto, considering it unjust that bishops should fail to celebrate Mass daily without a reasonable cause or fail to attend it, granted them permission to use a portable altar even outside their own diocese: “By this Constitution we grant them the right to have a portable altar and to celebrate or have Mass celebrated on it wherever they are permitted to celebrate without violating an interdict, or to attend Mass”. The adverb “anywhere” is worthy of note, as it undoubtedly includes places outside the diocese as well. 5. The Resolutions of the aforementioned Congregation of the Council deserve particular attention. In response to the question posed to it—namely, whether a bishop who is outside his own diocese is required to seek permission from the local bishop in order to use a portable altar—the Congregation replied that he is not required to do so. Otherwise, the privilege granted by Boniface VIII would have been rendered null and void. In fact, at the time of that Pontiff, an ancient law was in force—which, as will be discussed later, was abolished by the Council of Trent—according to which the authority to celebrate Mass in private homes was granted by the bishops. Likewise, when another question was posed to the Congregation as to whether, namely, whether the privilege granted to the bishops by Boniface VIII had been in any way prejudiced by the Council of Trent or by the Decree of Pope Paul V—which will also be discussed later—the Congregation replied that no value had been taken away from that privilege. When, moreover, both the Council and the aforementioned Decree stripped the bishops of the authority to grant permission to others to celebrate Mass in private homes, the rights pertaining to the bishops themselves—which are proper to their dignity and office—were by no means taken away from them. The cited Resolutions are reproduced by Us in our treatise \*De Sacrificio Missae\* (section 2, paragraph 42, Latin edition, Padua), as well as in the Commentaries by the late Cardinal Petra on the Apostolic Constitutions (vol. 4, Super Constitutione 2 of Urban V, no. 15 et seq.), and by Gattico in his recent treatise \*De Oratoriis domesticis, De usu Altaris portatilis\* (chap. 12, no. 1 et seq.). 6. It is most certainly true that nothing causes greater harm to privileges than their abuse. This has been sufficiently and truthfully demonstrated even in the case of the privilege granted to bishops regarding the portable altar, which remained intact after the Council of Trent and the Pauline Decree. Certain indications revealed that some bishops were abusing this privilege; within or outside their dioceses, they would go to the homes of laypeople and celebrate Mass there or allow multiple Masses to be celebrated there—not out of any urgent necessity, but solely to flaunt their privilege or to satisfy the requests of the laity. When news of this problem reached the Congregation of the Council, it did not fail to curb such excesses and entrusted to the archbishops the task of correcting theabuse should it occur within their jurisdictions. The Resolutions are recorded in the Codes—or, rather, in the Registers—of that Congregation (lib. 48, Decretorum, fol. 471). But since, despite everything, the scourge was spreading, our predecessor of blessed memory, Pope Clement XI, on December 15, 1703, issued the following Decree, in which, after setting forth the problem that “Bishops in other dioceses, away from their own residences, have altars erected in the private homes of laypeople and there, through one or more of their chaplains, offer the life-giving Host of Christ,” he proceeds to the remedy: “To eliminate such abuses, Our Most Holy Lord expressly notifies the bishops and their superiors, even if they have been elevated to the rank of cardinal, that neither under the pretext of a privilege incorporated into the law, nor under any other pretext, is it in any way lawful—outside one’s own residence, in the homes of laypeople, even within one’s own diocese and much less in that of others, even with the consent of the diocesan bishop—to erect an altar and there celebrate the most holy Sacrifice of the Mass or have it celebrated.” 7. This great Pontiff had but one purpose in mind: to eliminate abuses, never to take anything away from the proper use of the privilege. Certainly, the words, rendered in such a broadly restrictive and limiting sense, could give rise to the claim that it is not lawful for bishops to use a portable altar when visiting their diocese, or when traveling, or for any other reason find themselves away from their residence and stay in the homes of laypeople. When this possibility was raised in the Congregations held under Innocent III, the immediate successor of Clement himself, and in which We—not yet having ascended to the highest office—served as Secretary, it was deemed appropriate that the Declaration, set forth below, be made official. It was subsequently included in the letter by Innocent III himself, which begins “Apostolici Ministerii,” and which the Pontiff himself composed to establish a sound organization of ecclesiastical discipline in the Kingdoms of Spain. That same letter was confirmed “in forma specifica” by his successor, Benedict XIII, who wished to include it in the Appendix of the Roman Council so that it might serve as a rule and norm for all places. The same Declaration was included among the Decrees, Title XV, Chapter III, of that same Council, to which We, too, were invited to interpret the Sacred Canons. We gladly reproduce here as well the words of the Declaration: “We declare (this refers to the Decree of Clement XI) that it is not lawful for Bishops to erect an altar outside their own residence, in the homes of laypeople, and to celebrate the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass there or have it celebrated. This prohibition is not to be understood as applying to homes—even those of laypeople—in which the bishops themselves are occasionally received as guests during a visit or while traveling. Nor does it apply when, in cases permitted by law through a special license from the Apostolic See, they are absent from their own residence and stay in the homes of others as their temporary domicile. In such cases, it shall be lawful for them to erect an altar for the aforementioned celebration just as they would in the home of their ordinary residence.” 8. We have deemed it most appropriate to set forth in our Encyclical Letter these points that particularly concern your brotherhoods: what has been granted to you regarding the chapels in your episcopal palaces, and what you have been permitted to do in the homes of others and outside your diocese. We have also indicated to you the reasons for these concessions and privileges, as well as the abuses thereof, and we have added prohibitions against such abuses. This is so that you may understand the difference between the chapels in your episcopal palaces—which are intended to provide for your spiritual consolation and comfort as well as for the decorum of your dignity— and the private oratories in the homes of the laity, both within and outside your diocese, in which—for your convenience and consolation—you are permitted to set up a portable altar and to celebrate Mass or have it celebrated. And also so that, when you must, by chance, watch over abuses in private oratories in the homes of the laity, you may not be subject to the criticism that, while you correct the faults of others in their homes, you tolerate excesses in your own home and in your own conduct. Therefore, before proceeding to address the matter of private oratories in the homes of laypeople, let us make a few preliminary remarks. 9. The first is that the practice of private oratories in homes dates back to ancient times. It is well known that the Apostles celebrated the sacred Mysteries in private homes and that this custom was preserved during the persecutions, as Cristiano Lupo rightly observes “in suis notis ad canones Trullanos.” The second point is that even in the centuries that followed, after the persecutions, there were private oratories in the homes of laypeople. In the Gallican Sacramentary (Volume 1, Musaei Italici, printed and published by Mabillon), there is a “collect” to be recited during the Mass celebrated “in the home of anyone.” There is no shortage of other evidence supporting this assertion, which we have diligently sought to gather in our treatise \*De Sacrificio Missae\* (sect. 1, § 10). A third premise is that, on several occasions, consideration was given to abolishing private oratories in the homes of laypeople—or, more precisely, the right to celebrate Mass there. We will say nothing of Justinian’s Novella 58, which by law prohibits the celebration of Mass in private oratories and permits only prayer. Nor will we discuss the Novella of Leo the Wise, who lifted the prohibition, asserting that private oratories, in which Masses were celebrated, had by then multiplied to such an extent that they were found not only among the nobility but also among people of modest means. As for the documents closer to our own times, it will suffice to have cited those that We have included in the treatise \*De Sacrificio Missae\*, in the passages mentioned. 10. Fourth Premise: Although the practice of private oratories in the homes of laypeople, where Mass was celebrated, has existed almost since time immemorial, the permission of the bishops was nevertheless always required; they were, for the most part, very willing to grant it. “We decree that Masses be celebrated not just anywhere, but in places consecrated by the bishop, or where he permits.” These are the words of the \*Can. Missarum\*, dist. 1. This leniency, which provoked—albeit in vain—the prohibition of private oratories, remained in force in the Eastern Church, especially because there was only one altar in Greek churches: so that, once a Mass had been celebrated there, another could not be celebrated on the same day. Balsamone, in his \*Commentariis ad Canones Trullanos\*, asserts that, without any other formula, the Bishop was deemed to have granted the priest permission to celebrate whenever he celebrated on altar cloths consecrated by the Bishop. 11. The final point we wish to make is this: after various discussions on this subject at the Sacred Council of Trent (this Council was received by the Kingdom of Poland with great honor and acclaim thanks to Cardinal Osio, Nuncio Commendatario, at the grand assembly held before King Sigismund Augustus, as can be seen in the history of the Council itself written by Cardinal Pallavicino (Book 24, Chapter 13, Session 22) in the Decree \*De observandis et evitandis in celebratione Missae\*, it was thus established and ordained: “Let them (meaning the bishops) not permit any secular or regular priest to celebrate this holy Sacrifice in private homes, nor ever outside a church or oratory dedicated solely to divine worship, which must be designated and inspected by the Ordinaries themselves.” The abolition of any privilege, exemption, and custom was added: “Notwithstanding privileges, exemptions, titles, and customs of any kind”. It followed from this that bishops no longer have the authority to grant permission for the use of private oratories in the homes of laypeople to celebrate Mass. Such a license to celebrate Mass in private oratories, if granted by them, would conflict with the precept imposed on them by the Council not to permit this. For this reason, the aforementioned right was reserved to the Holy See, since the circumstances of the times and the widespread existence of private oratories in the homes of the laity did not permit their complete abolition. And this has always been the intent of the Conciliar Text as transmitted by the Congregation of the Council, the sole interpreter of the Council itself. Our predecessor of blessed memory, Pope Paul V, also approved this in the Encyclical Letter sent to all the bishops. This Encyclical can be found both in the works of various authors and printed in our treatise \*De Sacrificio Missae\* (sect. 2, § 42). In it, any other interpretation of the Council’s words is condemned, and much is said about irreverence toward the Sacrifice of the Mass. The bishops’ excessive readiness to grant licenses without any limitation or caution contributed significantly to fostering this irreverence. Finally, the Letter concludes as follows: “The authority to grant such licenses has been removed from all by decree of the Council itself and is reserved solely to the Roman Pontiff.” 12. Since the right to grant permission for oratories in the private homes of the laity was reserved to the Apostolic See, it is difficult to say how much care and diligence have been exercised in its proper application. The authentic documents are found in the Archives of the Congregation of the Council, where we once served as Secretary for many years before ascending to the highest office. The provisions established by law, as evident from the wording of the letters in the form of briefs, can be summarized as follows: the Oratory must be built with walls separating it from all other rooms intended for domestic use and must be inspected either by the Bishop or by another person to whom the Bishop has delegated his authority, to verify that it is decorous and suitable, and whether anything necessary is lacking; there must be a Bishop who grants permission to celebrate Mass, and such permission is valid at the Bishop’s discretion; no more than one Mass may be celebrated per day, and that Mass be celebrated by a secular or religious priest, provided that the secular priest is approved by the Bishop and the religious priest has the permission of his religious superior; Mass shall not be celebrated on the solemnities of Easter, Pentecost, the Nativity of Our Lord Jesus Christ, or on other more solemn days, including the feasts of the Epiphany, the Ascension of the Lord, the Annunciation and Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary, the feasts of Saints Peter and Paul, the Apostles, and the patron saint of the local church; the persons whose presence is necessary for the celebration of Mass in the private oratory must be designated, as well as other persons who, while Mass is being celebrated for the aforementioned individuals, may attend and fulfill the ecclesiastical precept; and finally, everything must be done without prejudice to parish rights. 13. These are the norms contained in the ordinary Brevi of private oratories. It also happens that extraordinary Breves are issued, such as when, for example, for just cause, permission is granted to someone to have a second Mass celebrated, or for the Mass to be celebrated slightly earlier or later than the time limit set by the Rubrics or on excluded days, and other matters of this kind. When, from time to time, disputes arose concerning both the ordinary Breves of private oratories and the extraordinary Breves—and regarding them in general—the Apostolic See never failed on such occasions to address them with appropriate provisions. 14. In the ordinary Brevi, permission is generally granted to a married couple to have Mass celebrated in their private oratory, and it is specified that Mass may be celebrated if both spouses are present, as well as their children, blood relatives, and relatives living with them in the same household. The question has never arisen as to whether, when the husband or wife attends Mass, the blood relatives and relatives living in the same household who also attend Mass fulfill the ecclesiastical precept on feast days. This has always been considered certain and consistent with the letter of the Brief. The real difficulty has always been in determining whether, when neither spouse is present, one of the blood relatives or other relatives living in the same household may arrange for Mass to be celebrated in the private oratory; and whether the other blood relatives and relatives living in the same household, by attending it on a holy day, fulfill the ecclesiastical precept of attending Mass. On this point, the opinions of the authors were, as usual, divided. When this difficulty was raised and discussed in the Congregation of the Council on December 3, 1740, in the case titled Marsicen. Oratori i, it was decided that Mass could not be celebrated in a private oratory unless those who had received the indult were present—that is to say, the husband or wife to whom the Brief had been addressed, as can also be seen in the Resolutions of the Council (Volume 9 of 1740, p. 89 ff.). 15. When the report on this Decree was then presented to Us, We confirmed it on January 7, 1741. We further established that in the Breves granting permission to certain specific individuals to have a Mass celebrated in a private oratory—and stating that such a Mass is valid for their children, blood relatives, and kin—the following clause should be added: “We decree that the aforementioned children, blood relatives, and kin may, provided you are present, attend only one Mass and must never dare to have it celebrated.” And so that it might be easily recognized which persons hold the indult—and without whom the Mass cannot be celebrated— nor would those attending fulfill the precept, we added that the persons holding the indulgence are to be understood as those named on the title page or in the title of the Brief addressed to them. 16. Sometimes a specific person is named in the body of the Brief; in such cases, if that person is present, permission is granted to celebrate Mass, and the other relatives—whether by blood or by marriage—who attend it fulfill the precept, even if that person is not named at all on the title page of the Brief. In this case, without prejudice to the rule that Mass may be celebrated in a private oratory provided that one of the persons holding the indulgence and named on the title page or in the title of the Brief is present, we affirm that Mass may be celebrated even if none of the persons named on the title page or in the title of the Brief are present, provided that the person is present, to whom, in the body of the Breve, the authority is expressly granted by name to have Mass celebrated in the private oratory when she is present. 17. An example will clarify this point. A husband and wife are granted the authority to have Mass celebrated in the private oratory, and the Brief is addressed to them. The husband’s mother is alive, but she is not mentioned at all on the title page or in the title of the Brief. However, if the body of the Brief states that the mother herself may also have Mass celebrated, this is sufficient; this is sufficient for Mass to be celebrated in the oratory by the mother’s mere presence alone, even if she is not named on the title page or in the title of the Breve. The points made so far are indicated in Breves of this type; but we will ensure that in the future they are expressed more appropriately and without any ambiguity. 18. In the customary, that is, ordinary, Breves, solemn days are excluded, as has been said, including the day of the Nativity of Christ the Lord, on which every priest celebrates three Masses. In this case, those who hold a Brief for a private oratory are granted a special Brief, which permits them, in the event of illness, to attend Mass even on the excluded days. Since the Briefs refer to only one Mass, the question arose as to whether, that is, whether a priest celebrating in a private oratory could celebrate three Masses on Christmas Day. On this issue, when We were Secretary of the Council, We wrote a special treatise and published it on January 13, 1725. Well, the matter was resolved by the Congregation, which ruled that the priest could celebrate the three Masses, as can be seen in the \*Treasure of Resolutions\* (vol. 3, p. 109 ff., and p. 116). 19. It now remains to discuss the sacred services that may be performed in oratories located in private homes, where it is permitted to celebrate not more than one Mass, unless someone has been granted the authority to have a second Mass celebrated. As for the Sacrament of Baptism, it was already established at the Council of Vienna, under Pope Clement V, that Baptism could not be conferred in any place other than churches, where the fonts for this sacred ablution are found, unless there were a case of necessity or when it concerned the children of kings or princes, as can be seen in the \*Clementina unica de Baptismo et eius effectu\*. 20. As for the Sacrament of Penance, however, the Roman Ritual, in its section on the priest hearing confessions, already stipulates: “He is to hear confessions in church and not in private homes unless for a reasonable cause; in such a case, however, he should endeavor to do so in a decent and open place.” The Ritual was confirmed by Pope Paul V. And the great restorer of ecclesiastical discipline, St. Charles Borromeo, both in his Instruction on the Sacrament of Penance and in his Admonitions to Confessors, as well as at his first Council of Milan, did not fail to emphasize the aforementioned rule, as can be seen in the Acts of the Church of Milan, published in that city (Part 1, p. 11, part 4, p. 520, p. 761, and p. 773). On page 775, he further recommends that the Superiors of the Regulars ensure that this rule is scrupulously observed. The aforementioned Regular confessors invoked the authority to hear the confessions of the faithful in any place, basing their claim on the fact that Pope Clement X’s bull \*Superna\* did not impose any geographical restrictions. The Pope himself, however, declared (in the aforementioned Constitution) that “the Regulars have not been granted any authority to hear sacramental confessions in private homes; and therefore it is by no means lawful for Regulars of any Order, etc., to administer the Sacrament of Penance in private homes except in the cases provided for by Canon Law.” The Decree, published under the signature of Bishop Fagnano, was printed in various places, as can be seen in the Appendix to the Synod of Foligno, which was celebrated by the Bishop of that city, Giosafat Battistelli, of blessed memory, in 1722. 21. With regard to the Easter Communion that every Catholic is required to receive during the Easter season, as stipulated in the Decree of the Lateran Council \*Omnisutriusque sexus, de poenitentiis, et remissionibus\* and in the Decree of the Council of Trent (session 13, chap. 8, can. 10), it is well known that this precept must be fulfilled in the parish church or in another church with the permission of one’s own bishop or parish priest, in accordance with the various customs of the dioceses. Subsequently, our predecessor of blessed memory, Pope Paul IV, granted the Friars Minor the privilege of distributing the Holy Eucharist to all the faithful in their churches, except, however, on Easter Sunday. This privilege was extended to all religious orders by our other predecessor of blessed memory, Pope Pius V, “per communicationem,” as it is said. 22. But since, according to a previous constitution of Eugene IV, the Easter season—during which the precept of Communion must be fulfilled—runs from Palm Sunday to Low Sunday, a doubt arose as to whether it was lawful for the Regulars to distribute the Eucharist to the faithful in their churches within the prescribed period. The doubt was resolved by stating that on Easter Sunday, Communion could not be distributed to anyone, not even to those who had fulfilled the Easter precept in their own parish church during Holy Week; and that the Regulars could indeed administer Communion to the faithful in their churches on the other days of the Easter season, but on the condition that the faithful receiving Communion were aware that they were not thereby exempt from the obligation to receive the Easter Communion in their own parish church. The most important decrees of the Congregation of the Council are found in “quibusdam causis, namely those of Senon, Burdega, and Mechlin,” and which We have cited in our treatise \*De Synodo Dioecesana\* (Book 7, Chapter 42, No. 3). 23. Apart from Easter Communion, the following words are found in the Sacred Council of Trent (session 22, chapter 6, \*De Sacrificio Missae\*): “The most holy Synod desires that the faithful who attend individual Masses receive Communion not only spiritually but also by receiving the sacramental Eucharist, so that they may derive a more abundant fruit from this most holy Sacrifice.” From these words, some deduced the certain and clear conclusion that in private oratories, when there is the faculty to celebrate Mass there, the Eucharist may also be distributed to those attending Mass, without the need for a special indulgence. On this point, in our \*Institut. (no. 34, par. 3), We have addressed various issues, including those We published in Italian while residing in Bologna as Archbishop of that Church, and which were later translated into Latin and printed. In them, we set forth the opinion stated above, but we added another according to which the bishop’s permission is required so that those who have a private oratory in their home may receive Communion while attending Mass there. This opinion seemed to us consistent both with the proper order of things and with the custom or practice of Rome. Therefore, we decreed that those attending Mass in a private oratory could not receive Communion, whether celebrated by a secular or a religious priest, without first obtaining permission from Us or from our Vicar General. 24. Nor do We currently have any intention or reason to depart from this rule. When, some years ago, the famous controversy arose in Italy regarding the distribution of Communion to those who request it while attending Mass, We cited the words of the Council of Trent; we praised the zeal of those who receive Communion during Mass; we also urged the Pastors of the Church not to deprive those who hunger for it of the Eucharistic food; we also acknowledged that there may be circumstances in which, due to time or place, the prudence of the Bishops might suggest theit would be advisable not to distribute the Holy Eucharist even to those present at Mass, especially since current discipline allows full freedom to receive it in other places and at other times. Nevertheless, we ordered that due obedience must be given to the precept of one’s superior. Anyone who refused thereby clearly demonstrated that he had a spirit ill-disposed and unprepared to receive the Sacrament of the Altar, as can be seen in our Letter \*Certiores effecti\* (in our \*Bollario\*, vol. 1, no. 64). 25. These, Venerable Brothers, are the matters we have deemed appropriate to set forth for you in this Encyclical. From this you can well understand how, by reading and consulting the Apostolic Letters on the concessions granted to the Oratorians, the Decrees of our Predecessors, and the Resolutions of these Congregations—which We have now confirmed—there is no longer any room for disputes or controversies. But We imagine you will reply: “All well and good. The fact remains, however, that the decrees issued are not being observed at all.” Please do not take offense if We reply that such non-compliance stems from a superficial reading and reflection on the Briefs or from ignorance of the papal decrees and resolutions. We bear no blame for this, especially since—despite our advanced age and the burden of extremely difficult responsibilities—we have not refused to make known everything there is to know on this subject. 26. If, in your synods and in the edicts you publish for the good governance of your dioceses, you take care to instill observance of the papal decrees and resolutions mentioned above, a twofold benefit will result: to keep their memory alive among you and to dispel ignorance among others—or to render such ignorance inexcusable if it is gross and willful. Such conduct and practice were (and still are) followed by our bishops in Italy, who either included in their synods the collection of papal decrees issued regarding private oratories, or appended them in full to the appendices of those same synods, as can be seen in the Synod held in Rimini by Cardinal De Vio, of blessed memory, in 1724, and in the one convened by Cardinal Pignatelli, of blessed memory, Archbishop of Naples, in 1726, and in the one held by our venerable Brother Cardinal Annibale Albano, then Bishop of Sabina, in 1736, and in many others. 27. We are not among those who are convinced that all the misfortunes and scandals occurring in our time did not occur in times past. We are certain that what happens today also happened in other times. But so as not to stray too far from the subject, we will say that if today the laws concerning private oratories are violated by priests for the protection of the secular princes on whom they rely, and to whom they serve as chaplains, this also happened in the 9th century. In fact, Saint Agobard, then Archbishop of Lyon, in his treatise \*De Privilegio et jure Sacerdoti I\*, chapter 11, laments the very same things. However, you must not let this cause you to lose your priestly steadfastness and courage. Poland is a pious and religious nation. If any abuse were to occur in private oratories—whether committed or introduced into a prince’s household—bring it to his attention, presenting reasons and demonstrating that such abuses result in the loss of privilege. It is to be hoped that in this way he will withdraw his protection from the disobedient chaplain. And if this approach were to yield no results, you always have at your disposal the spiritual weapons you can use against the chaplain. Had this course of action been taken as soon as the problem came to light, we can say—to everyone’s relief—that matters would not have reached the point they are at now, as evidenced by the appeals made to Us, which have prompted Us to write this Encyclical Letter. 28. We anticipate that you may say that the problems stem from the Privileged and the Exempt, that is, from the Regulars, regarding whom several insinuations have been made in the aforementioned appeal submitted to this Holy See. And We reply that there are neither privileges nor exemptions that can prevent the eradication of abuses. 29. The Regulars undoubtedly have the privilege of the portable altar and of celebrating Mass on it wherever they may be, without any permission from the Bishop, as can be seen in the Decretal of Honorius III (chap. In his, de privilegiis). Because of this privilege, they were able to celebrate Mass even in the homes of laypeople, whether on a portable altar or a fixed altar, even if the layperson did not have the privilege of a private oratory. This was because the celebrant’s privilege remained in force even though the place of celebration was not itself privileged. The Sacred Council of Trent (Session 22, Decree \*De observandis et evitandis in celebratione Missae\*) had ordered the bishops not to permit Masses to be celebrated in private homes “neither by secular clergy nor by regulars of any order” and had granted them the authority to take action against those who refused to comply. It had also abolished all privileges, exemptions, and customs to the contrary, of whatever kind they might be: “notwithstanding privileges, exemptions, appeals, and customs of any kind.” It necessarily follows from this that the Sacred Council of Trent repealed both the previous privileges regarding the portable altar and the right to celebrate Mass in private homes without the bishop’s permission, and that, furthermore, the Bishop, as Delegate of the Apostolic See, may take action against the disobedient even if they are exempt, and that, finally, there is no privilege or exemption that opposes or prevents the removal of abuses. 30. The observation put forward by some—namely, that the Council did not derogate from the privilege in question because it was included “in corpore Juris”—is without merit. The Sacred Council of Trent, in fact, was in the habit of derogating even from those privileges that were included “in corpore Juris” without making express mention of them, but simply by establishing something to the contrary through the application of the general derogation to conflicting privileges, as the renowned Bishop Fagnano amply demonstrates (in the chapter “Nonnulli,” no. 42 et seq., title “De Rescriptis”). 31. If by any chance someone were to object that the Apostolic See has often granted—and even now, after the Council of Trent, continues to grant—the use of a portable altar, it must be known to Your Fraternities that this privilege is granted in those places where there are no churches or, if there are, the power of the heretics is such that Catholics cannot gather to hear Mass without grave danger. In short, to put it another way, this addresses a necessity—a situation entirely different, as you can clearly see, from the case at hand. 32. Read the privilege that Our Predecessor Gregory XIII granted in 1580 to the Friars of the Order of Preachers in the Province of Poland, and which was included in the Bullarium of that Religious Order (vol. 7, p. 192): “In certain cities, villages, and localities of the Province of Poland, the power and impiety of the heretics are so great that they oppress Catholics with impunity, and it is not lawful for Catholics to attend Mass safely in churches, of which there is a scarcity in those parts. We, being favorable to your requests on this matter, grant you—currently the Provincial Superior of the Polish Province of the Order of Preachers—the authority to grant the aforementioned Professors the license and permission to use portable altars with due reverence and honor, upon which, in the homes of nobles and the inhabitants of cities, villages, and localities of the said Province where churches are lacking and where the power and impiety of heretics is such that they commit violence with impunity, they may celebrate Mass in suitable and decorous places, but only in cases of necessity, so as to be free from blame: this We grant by Apostolic Authority in accordance with the present Letter and by special grace.” 33. Please also read the aforementioned Decree of Clement X, which we are including here for your convenience, although we know that when it was drafted, in addition to being published in Rome, it was sent to the Ordinaries both within and outside Italy. From this Decree—which, were it not for its existence, We ourselves would view with suspicion what We are called upon to uphold with Our arguments and considerations (and it would be easy to do so, for us, who have at our disposal everywhere the rulings of the most distinguished authors among legal experts)—from this Decree, therefore, you will be able to clearly ascertain whether the privileges of the portable altar granted to the Regulars still exist or not, and whether the Council of Trent has derogated from them. You will know whether the Regulars are permitted to celebrate Mass in any private oratory when a Mass has already been celebrated there, and no special indulgence has been granted for a second Mass. You will know whether it is lawful to celebrate Mass in the aforementioned private oratories before dawn or in the afternoon, and whether they may celebrate it on days excluded by the Indult. Finally, you will learn whether, despite the exemption, you have the authority to take action against offenders. Although there is no need whatsoever, We now confirm the Decree and entrust to your Fraternities the task of ensuring its proper observance, since it is not praiseworthy to appeal to the Apostolic See to report these problems unless the remedies prescribed by the Apostolic See itself against such problems have first been implemented. 34. In the appeal submitted to Us, mention is also made of another problem, namely, that Regulars perform exorcisms without a license. But it is not stated whether it has been established by you, or in your Synods, or in your Edicts, that no priest—whether secular or regular— dare to perform exorcisms either in his own church or in that of others, whether inside or outside the monastery, unless he has first been approved by you and has first obtained a license from you. This is what must prevail and what the bishops must guarantee, as can be seen in \*Clericato\* (De Sacramento Ordinis, decis. 19, n. 42), which cites the Episcopal Synods. If, however, after you have taken appropriate measures, as far as it depends on you, regarding this matter and that of private oratories, and your orders are violated and the penalties you have imposed and inflicted are disregarded, we will undoubtedly fulfill our duty by exercising all our authority in your favor. For what matters most to us is that the rights of the Bishops, who are our Brothers, be safeguarded. Meanwhile, with great affection, we impart the Apostolic Blessing to Your Fraternities and to the peoples entrusted to your care. Given at Castel Gandolfo, on June 2, 1751, the eleventh year of Our Pontificate. DECREE OF POPE CLEMENT XI OF BLESSED MEMORY ON THE CELEBRATION OF THE MASS IN PRIVATE ORATORIES ISSUED ON DECEMBER 15, 1703 Some bishops and many members of religious orders, under the pretext of privileges, believe that what is in fact prohibited is lawful for them. Bishops, in fact, even in the dioceses of others and outside their own residences, have altars erected in the homes of laypeople, where one or more of their chaplains offer the life-giving Host of Christ. The members of religious orders, moreover, in certain oratories that the Apostolic See customarily grants from time to time for legitimate reasons to princes or other nobles, dare to celebrate one or more Masses than they have been permitted, or without the presence of the persons for whom the concession was granted, or outside the established hours—in the afternoon or even on those days when, by diocesan constitutions or even by decrees of the Sacred Congregation of the Council, it is forbidden to celebrate—or even on days excluded from celebration by Apostolic Indults. Nor do they hesitate to use theportable altar in defiance of the sacred sanctions and with irreverence toward the holy Sacrifice. Therefore, in order to eliminate these abuses and to renew reverence for the awe-inspiring Mystery, Our Most Holy Lord, with the consent of the Cardinals of the Holy Roman Church, interpreters of the Council of Trent, and in accordance with the declarations previously issued on this subject, hereby declares: to the Bishops and to the Prelates superior to them, even if endowed with the dignity of a cardinal, it is not lawful—neither under the pretext of a privilege included in the body of canon law nor on any other grounds—to erect an altar and celebrate the Most Holy Sacrifice of the Mass thereon, or to have it celebrated outside their own residence, in the homes of laypeople even within their own diocese, and much less so in the dioceses of others, even if they present the consent of the diocesan bishop. Likewise, in private oratories granted by the Holy See, it is not lawful for members of religious orders, institutes, or congregations—including the Society of Jesus, or even of any Military Order, including that of St. John of Jerusalem, or any other priest—even if a bishop—to celebrate Mass on the days of Easter, Pentecost, and the Nativity of the Lord, as well as on the other most solemn feasts of the year or on the days excluded by the Indult. On other days, however, no priest—even if a bishop—is permitted to celebrate Mass in the aforementioned oratories if the single Mass permitted by the Indult has already been celebrated there. Anyone intending to celebrate Mass is required to investigate and ascertain the exact details regarding all of this. Furthermore, that single Mass may not be celebrated even on the permitted afternoons. In all cases, it must be emphasized and declared that all those attending such Masses do not fulfill the Church’s precept. As for the portable altar, in accordance with the aforementioned declarations, the Decree establishes that the licenses or privileges granted to certain members of religious orders in the chapter \*In his, de privil.\*, and granted by certain Popes to other members of religious orders to use the said portable altar and to celebrate Mass thereon without a license from the local Ordinaries in the places where they reside, have all been revoked by the Council of Trent itself. Therefore, the said members of religious orders are prohibited from using those altars, and, in accordance with the terms of this Decree, the Bishops and other Ordinaries of the respective localities, acting as Delegates of the Apostolic See, are ordered to take action against all transgressors—even if they are Regulars—with the penalties prescribed by the same sacred Council in the aforementioned Decree, sess. 22, sole chapter, up to censures “latae sententiae.” This Decree also grants them the authority to proceed as if said authority had been specially granted by the Holy See. Thus, His Holiness declares and commands that this be observed.
