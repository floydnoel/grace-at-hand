## Summary

*Sicut Dudum* (1435) is a papal bull issued by Pope Eugene IV condemning the enslavement of indigenous Canary Islanders by certain Christians. It orders the restoration of their liberty under pain of excommunication, and prohibits future enslavement of both baptized natives and those freely seeking Baptism.

## Historical Context

The bull was written in the context of 15th-century Atlantic expansion, shortly after the Canary Islands' discovery and initial evangelization. Some Christians had begun raiding the islands, capturing and selling the native Guanches into slavery, often under the guise of evangelization or by breaking promises of safe conduct. This occurred during a period when the Church was wrestling with questions of just war, slavery, and the rights of non-European peoples—decades before Columbus's voyages would thrust these issues onto a global stage.

## Key Themes

- **The right of non-Christians to liberty**: The natives had "imitated natural law alone" and committed no offense warranting enslavement.
- **Condemnation of fraudulent evangelization**: Using the promise of Baptism as a trap to enslave people is explicitly denounced.
- **Restitutive justice**: Slaveholders must free captives and restore goods within fifteen days, no payment demanded.
- **Ecclesiastical penalty**: Automatic excommunication (reserved to the deathbed) for those who disobey or who enslave islanders in the future.
- **Pastoral authority**: The Pope invokes his duty to "rebuke each sinner about his sin" and to provide for suffering souls.

## Significance

*Sicut Dudum* is one of the earliest papal condemnations of slavery in the modern period and establishes a moral principle: conversion to Christianity cannot be coerced, and those seeking Baptism must not be enslaved. It anticipates later papal interventions on slavery and indigenous rights, including Paul III's *Sublimis Deus* (1537), and forms part of the Church's developing social teaching on human dignity and the integrity of evangelization.
