# Spesse Volte · English editorial summary

## Summary

Against the Italian government's systematic suppression of Catholic institutions — including charitable societies, newspapers, rural banks, shelters, and catechism programs — in the years following the unification of Italy and the loss of the Papal States. The Pope defends these associations as peaceful, lawful, and essential to the moral and material welfare of the Italian people, and denounces their dissolution as arbitrary, sectarian, and destructive to both religion and civil society.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was written twenty-eight years after the Italian army seized Rome (1870), ending the Pope's temporal sovereignty and sparking the "Roman Question." In the decades that followed, successive Italian governments confiscated Church property, suppressed religious orders, imposed military service on clergy, and systematically secularised public life. In response, Leo XIII had encouraged Catholics to organise socially and charitably — forming rural banks, bakeries, cooperatives, and catechism centres — to counter the spread of socialism, anarchism, and irreligion. By 1898, these associations had grown into a widespread network. That same year, popular riots (the *Fatti di Maggio*) broke out across Italy over rising bread prices and harsh government policies. The government, fearing Catholic power, used the unrest as a pretext to dissolve hundreds of Catholic organisations, shut down Catholic newspapers, and ban congresses — claiming, falsely, that Catholics had fomented the violence. *Spesse Volte* is Leo XIII's response.

## Key Themes

- **The Church's right to associate**: Catholics possess the same civil right as all other citizens to form peaceful associations for piety, charity, and mutual aid — a right violated by the government's arbitrary decrees.
- **Religion as the foundation of society**: True social order depends on religious faith; the suppression of Catholic institutions weakens public morality, encourages socialism and anarchy, and leads to civil strife.
- **The injustice and hypocrisy of the government**: The authorities suppressed Catholic works without evidence, trial, or due process, while granting freedom to masonic and anti-clerical groups — exposing their claim to "liberty" as a sham.
- **Non-revolutionary but non-cooperative**: Italian Catholics are loyal subjects who obey lawful authority and reject conspiracy; but they cannot actively support a political order that aims at the destruction of the Papacy.
- **Providential confidence**: Despite persecution, the Church has divine promises that guarantee her survival; adversity only strengthens her.
- **The Roman Question**: The Pope's lack of temporal independence (imprisonment in the Vatican) is the root cause of the Church's vulnerability in Italy; a just resolution is essential for religious freedom.

## Significance

*Spesse Volte* is one of Leo XIII's most forceful statements on the Roman Question and the persecution of the Church in post-unification Italy. It articulates a principle that would shape Catholic social action for decades: that Catholics may and must organise independently of the state to serve the common good, but that they cannot be expected to endorse a regime that attacks their faith. The encyclical foreshadows the later development of Catholic Action, and its critique of state overreach into civil society resonates with the Church's social teaching on subsidiarity. Though the immediate political situation it addressed was resolved only with the Lateran Treaty of 1929, the letter remains a landmark in the Church's defence of associative freedom against secularist coercion.
