ENCYCLICAL ACCEPIMUS PRAESTANTIUM OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Venerable Brothers, Archbishops, Bishops, and Ordinaries of the Papal States. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. We have learned from the testimonies of distinguished men that, for some time now, a custom has been introduced in certain cities within ecclesiastical jurisdiction that fosters devotion and promotes religious sentiment, but which runs counter to the decrees of the Church and the custom of the majority. We ourselves have observed this in some churches of this city of Rome, and likewise in others that are subject to the suburban bishops. It would seem that We were failing in Our duty if We did not bring this to the attention of those whom We have appointed as ministers for the spiritual guidance of this city, and others who care for souls by governing suburban bishoprics or the episcopal see of another city within the Papal Jurisdiction. On the altars that have been erected by Christian communities, and on which the sacred rite is traditionally celebrated before a larger panel depicting—either painted or carved—the image of the saint in whose name and memory the altar has been consecrated, another smaller panel has begun to be added, on which the portrait of another saint is either depicted in color or engraved on bronze, wood, or marble; consequently, the Cross is removed and must be placed between the candlesticks, next to the Rubrics. Indeed, so that it may be clear to all that the sacred rite cannot be performed unless the Cross bearing the image of the Most Holy Crucified One is placed above the Altar, a very small crucifix is placed above the minor table, so that it is not readily visible to the celebrating priest, nor can it be distinguished from anything else unless he looks closely, or unless the image of the Crucified One has in fact been displayed above the altar. In truth, we do not disapprove of the fact that the same altar is dedicated to the name and memory of several saints, or the fact that one or even more saints are painted on a larger panel, or finally the fact that another smaller panel is placed lower down, or in any other manner, where one or more saints are displayed for the veneration of the faithful. Under no circumstances can we permit the Mass to be celebrated at these altars, which lack the image of the Crucified Christ, or where the image is placed in an inappropriate manner before the celebrating priest, or is so small as to be almost invisible to the priest himself and to the congregation present; for this is, in fact, contrary to the laws and institutions of the Church, which are codified in the canons and other ecclesiastical norms; it is also most contrary to sacred antiquity and to the custom of the Eastern Churches. Therefore, it is absolutely certain that the laws of the Church are violated if even a small image of the Crucified Christ is placed in front of a smaller altar table, or in front of the statue of the saint placed upon it for veneration by the faithful. In the Rubrics of the Missal, under the heading “On the Preparation of the Altar and Its Vestments,” the following words are found: “The Cross is to be placed in the center of the altar, and at least two candlesticks with lit candles on either side of it.” In the Roman Pontifical, under “On the Consecration of the Elect to the Episcopate,” when a homily is delivered from the altar—where the bishop-elect awaits the sacred rite until the Offertory— after which the Mass continues at another altar, where the consecrating Bishop performs the sacred rite, the following should be noted in that Pontifical: “In the smaller chapel designated for the Elect, which must be separated from the larger one, an altar with a cross and two candlesticks shall be added.” Furthermore, the Ceremonial of Bishops, after referring to Book I, Chapter 12, regarding the candlesticks and candles, adds: “In the center of these shall be placed a Cross of the same metal and workmanship, very tall, so that the base of the Cross is equal in height to the neighboring candlesticks and the entire Cross itself rises above the candlesticks with the image of the Most Holy Crucified One facing the front of the Altar.” We ourselves have spoken of this ancient custom of placing the Cross on the Altar during the celebration of the sacred rite in Our writings \*On the Sacrifice of the Mass\*, which We composed in Italian; We added much more when these same writings, translated into Latin, were published a second time. To this we also add that, especially because of the antiquity of this custom, the greatest devotion has always been dedicated to the Cross, which is placed at the center of the altar when Mass is celebrated; even the most hostile reformers have been very reluctant to remove it from the center when they consecrate the Eucharist; Gretser bears witness to this in his \*Treatise on the Cross\*, in Chapter 13, Volume I of that edition, which was eventually printed. In truth, if we observe the customs of the Eastern Churches, the Greeks have established the practice of placing, on both sides of the main door of the sanctuary, images of Christ the Lord and the Blessed Virgin, and above the altar, the Cross together with the book of the Holy Gospels. In the Coptic-Arabic liturgy—which, derived from the Vatican Codices, was printed in 1736 at the Urban College of the Propaganda Fide— on p. 33, the celebrating priest is instructed to impart the blessing to the people with the Cross, then to kiss it and hand it to a deacon so that he may place it above the Altar. Finally, the Syrian rite of the Maronites establishes precisely the same provisions that we have indicated above and that are prescribed in the Rubrics of the Roman Missal. In fact, Patriarch Stephen writes as follows: “This custom developed in all the Churches, so that the symbol of the life-giving Cross is placed above the Altar” (Book 2, Treatise 2, 4). The National Synod, which was convened in the year 1736 on Mount Lebanon and which We ourselves ratified with the Apostolic Letter On the Most Holy Mass, also stipulates as follows: “The Cross is to be placed in the center of the Altar; on either side of it, at least two candlesticks with lit candles, one on each side” (§ 2, chap. 13). The authors who have explained the sacred rites engage in some discussion: first of all, whether it is sufficient to place the bare Cross on the Altar, or whether it is appropriate to add the image of the Crucifix at the same time. In truth, the Rubrics of the Missal mention only the Cross. However, since the Ceremonial of the Bishops mentions both the Cross and the Crucifix at the same time, and since this is in accordance with a common custom of the Church, no scholar will therefore doubt that this view should be accepted. This is the view of Gavantus and Claude La Croix, who, in his work \*On the Mass\*, admits that, by virtue of common traditional practice, the sacred rite cannot take place without the image of the Crucified Christ hanging on the Cross, unless prevented by necessity, as was the case at the beginning of the Church’s history, when the faithful feared they would greatly provoke the wrath of the pagans if they displayed the Crucified Christ for public adoration (Book 6, § 2, n. 318). Nevertheless, they openly displayed the Cross adorned in many different ways and affixed to the Cross, either engraved or painted, the Lamb, whose image has always been associated with the beloved Savior, so that at least in this way they might show the image of the Crucified One; this is still observed in some churches of this city and is also attested to by Magrio in his \*Ierolessico\*, under the entry \*Crux\*. Secondly, we examine whether the Crucified Christ hanging on the Cross should be placed on the altar, even if our Crucified Savior appears painted or carved on a panel larger than the altar. Gavantus certainly agrees; in truth, others consider it scarcely necessary, provided, however, that the Crucifix—whether painted or carved on a larger panel—is in the foreground before the other figures depicted there. This is the opinion of Pasqualigus (cf. \*The Sacrifice of the Mass\*, question 716), Quarto (cf. Rubrics of the Missal, § 1, tit. 20, dub. 10), and Giribaldo (cf. On the Sacraments and the Sacrifice of the Mass, chap. 9, dub. 2, n. 20). The Congregation of Sacred Rites likewise conformed to this view, as is evident from the response it made public in the year 1663 and which Meratus examines carefully (cf. The Preparation of the Altar, no. 6; Index of Decrees, same volume, § 2, no. 400). Thirdly, there is debate as to whether the Crucifix should be placed on the Altar (when Mass is celebrated there) where the Tabernacle is located, in which the Pyx containing the Sacred Hosts is kept; all the more so if a small cross bearing the image of the Crucified Savior is always placed in front of that same Tabernacle. In fact, the Ceremonial of the Bishops states: “It is not improper but most fitting that Masses not be celebrated on the Altar where the Most Holy Sacrament is present, a custom which we believe has been observed since ancient times.” In the old Ceremonial, dating from the pontificate of Clement VIII, it was written: “A custom that we see has been observed since ancient times.” Nevertheless, this seems entirely unacceptable to some, because, as they themselves argue, in ancient churches either a single altar was built, or the Divine Eucharist was not kept there, or no Mass was celebrated there. On the contrary, those who are familiar with and have studied this custom in depth believe that it was practiced in ancient times when the Holy Eucharist was kept in a chapel within the church, or in an elegant cabinet affixed to the wall, as we still see today in the Basilica of the Holy Cross in Jerusalem. Mabillon shares this view (cf. \*Museo Italico\*, vol. I, p. 89, and vol. II, p. 139). But what currently poses a difficulty and resolves nothing in the examination of the aforementioned controversy is the fact that, according to current practice, the Holy Eucharist is kept in the Tabernacle placed above an altar in the church on which, without any specific rule, Mass is celebrated, after a careful examination of the text of the Ceremonial, since it has been recognized that this in no way conflicts with the aforementioned celebration, as Christianus Lupus notes in Volume XI of the most recent edition of the treatise \*On Sacred Processions\*, p. 356. Therefore, since we ourselves examine this controversy elsewhere in the aforementioned writings on \*The Sacrifice of the Mass\*, § 4, section 1, 18 we have appreciated the opinion of those who hold that the Crucifix must be placed between the candlesticks and that a small cross affixed to the Tabernacle is not sufficient to ensure that the Rubric is not violated; as we will explain below. The same rule was established by the Congregation of Sacred Rites in the year 1663, as can be read in the Index of Decrees, presented by Meratus, where the following words are found: “A small cross bearing the image of the Crucified Christ placed above the Tabernacle, in which (on the Altar) the Most Holy Sacrament is kept, is not sufficient during the celebration of Mass: another cross must be placed between the candlesticks” (§ 2, Volume I, no. 400). Fourth, there is discussion as to whether the Crucifix should be placed between the candlesticks when the sacred rite concludes at that Altar, where the Divine Eucharist is not enclosed in the Tabernacle but is exposed for the public veneration of the faithful. On May 14, 1707, the Congregation of Sacred Rites held that this is indispensable. In fact, the question had been posed: “Whether, above the altar on which the Most Holy Sacrament has been exposed, the Cross should be placed according to custom.” The response at that time was as follows: “One must never fail to place the Cross bearing the image of the Crucified One.” We also recall that when We ourselves (then serving as Coadjutor to the Promoter of the Faith) participated in that same Congregation, the votes on that decision were varied and divergent; prudence was required to prevent that decision from being adopted. The issue was raised again at the beginning of Our Pontificate on September 2, 1741, in the same Congregation of Sacred Rites, which, after examining the matter scrupulously, decreed that every church should continue to observe in the future the custom it had practiced up to that point. In truth, regarding the removal of the Cross from the Altar while Mass is being celebrated there, and the Eucharist is exposed there for public veneration, it matters little that some consider it inappropriate for the image of Christ to be in the place where Christ Himself, in the form of bread, is truly present and is seen by all under the same species of bread. This undoubtedly occurs every time the priest, performing the sacred rite at any altar, consecrates the Eucharist; and the Church has never held that it should be decreed that, once the consecration is complete, the Cross be removed from the altar. Similarly, for those who maintain that the Cross must necessarily be on the altar where the Sacrament has been exposed for public veneration, if Mass is celebrated there: it is of no avail to argue that the public exposition of the Eucharist signifies the triumph and glory of the Savior, whereas the Mass commemorates his death; the Church, both during the Mass—in which the Eucharist is consecrated—and at the conclusion of the prayers, which are recited in the presence of the Eucharist now consecrated and openly exposed for the veneration of the faithful, equally uses the same Collect composed by St. Thomas Aquinas: “God, who has left us the memory of your Passion within the wondrous Sacrament, grant, we pray, that we may venerate the sacred mysteries of your Body and Blood, so that we may always feel within us the fruit of your redemption (that is, of your Passion and death).” Given this situation, and since interpreters and writers on liturgical rites have, for the reasons mentioned, expressed differing opinions, the Decrees that were prudently and wisely issued by the aforementioned Congregation for Sacred Rites cannot be highly enough recommended, precisely so that every Church or Diocese may maintain its own custom; so that nothing may be changed in that Diocese where the Cross is customarily placed on the Altar while Mass is being celebrated, even if the Holy Eucharist is publicly exposed; nor should a new practice be encouraged in that diocese where a custom contrary to it has long been established. We have carefully set forth all these matters, certainly not to compose a comprehensive treatise on the subject, but so that through them it might be known and observed how alien to the sacred rites is what many have done lightly and without reflection, namely, celebrating the sacred rite at altars on which the Cross does not rise above the candlesticks, but only a small crucifix is affixed to the panel of some saint—whether painted or sculpted—that was customarily added to the main altar panel. For if, in accordance with the Rubrics of the Missal, the Cross is to be placed between the candlesticks; if, in accordance with the Ceremonial of the Bishops, the Cross bearing the image of the Crucified One is to rise above the candlesticks; if, in accordance with the teaching of the Congregation for Sacred Rites, a small crucifix affixed to the tabernacle is not deemed sufficient when Mass is celebrated at the altar, where the pyx containing the Sacred Hosts is enclosed in the tabernacle; if the present discussion does not concern an altar on which the image of the Crucified Savior is prominently displayed to the faithful, nor an altar on which the Holy Eucharist is exposed for public adoration, there can be no one who does not see that the practice under discussion—recently introduced and carried out by certain individuals with particular aggressiveness—must be absolutely disapproved of for the reasons stated, all the more so when the small crucifix affixed to the panel of that saint placed above the altar certainly does not fulfill the purpose established by the Church when it decrees that the Cross must be placed between the candlesticks. On this subject, His Eminence Cardinal Bona wrote as follows: “The sight of the Cross brings to the mind of the celebrating priest the Passion of Christ, a Passion of which this Sacrifice is the true image and real representation, expressing in a bloodless manner the bloody death of our Savior; although presented in a different way, it is the same Sacrifice that was offered on the Cross.” These final words set forth the orthodox doctrine expressed by the Council of Trent (Rerum liturgicarum, Book I, Chapter 25, No. 8). Therefore, all of you, Venerable Brothers, to whom we address these apostolic writings, prevent with all your heart the custom we have explained above from taking root in your dioceses. If this custom has already taken root, using first gentleness and then severity if necessary, root it out completely. This concerns the Sacrifice of the Mass; therefore none of you is unaware that the Council of Trent conferred upon bishops and the ordinaries of various places the authority by which, even as delegates of the Apostolic See, they may correct the bad customs that are contrary to the rite of the celebration of the Mass; as is clearly inferred from the decree of the 22nd session, “On Things to Be Observed and Avoided in the Celebration of the Mass.” Nor is it unknown to you that there is no privilege or immunity that can be granted to those who do not comply with this provision, so that they may be punished by your authority with penalties imposed by you. The Sacred Congregation, acting as the interpreter of the Council of Trent, has expressed this view on several occasions, referring to the decree of that same Council which we have just mentioned. Fagnanus attests to this: “In general, the Sacred Congregation has established that even members of religious orders are included in this decree, even though they were previously excluded; all those who had been exempted will be required to scrupulously observe, in the celebration of Masses, the norms established by the Ordinaries; they may be compelled to do so by ecclesiastical penalties and censures to be imposed by the Ordinaries themselves” (De officio Ordinarii, chap. Grave, no. 46). We, in truth, hereby reaffirm through this Letter the authority with which you have already been invested. It remains to be noted that We do not believe you should remove from the center the images of the Saints that have been placed above the aforementioned main altar table, since there will undoubtedly be envious individuals who, in order to cast a stain on your religious sentiment, will spread the rumor among the people that you are not moved by any devotion toward that Saint, whose image you are removing from the veneration of the faithful. We hereby prescribe the following: under no circumstances are you to allow the sacred rite to be celebrated at altars where the Crucifix does not rise between the candlesticks, so that the priest celebrating and the people attending the Sacrifice may easily gaze upon the Crucifix itself, which cannot happen if the faithful are shown only a small cross affixed to a smaller board. If this corruption has not spread significantly throughout the diocese, it can easily be eradicated if the superiors of the churches privately inform the religious and secular clergy of this matter. But if it has spread widely throughout the diocese, then you will necessarily have to issue an edict in accordance with this Letter of Ours. We will refrain from writing more to you: We hold you in high esteem for your prudence and doctrine. Know with certainty that Our support, whenever necessary, will never fail you if you ask for it to bring this provision to a successful conclusion. In the meantime, We most lovingly impart the Apostolic Blessing to Your Fraternities and to the peoples entrusted to your care. Given at Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on July 16, 1746, the sixth year of Our Pontificate.
