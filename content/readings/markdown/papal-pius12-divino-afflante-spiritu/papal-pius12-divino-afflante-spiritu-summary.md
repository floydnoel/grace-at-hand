## Summary

*Divino Afflante Spiritu* ("Inspired by the Divine Spirit") is a 1943 encyclical by Pope Pius XII that established the authoritative Catholic approach to modern biblical scholarship. It reaffirms the inspiration and inerrancy of Scripture while encouraging Catholic exegetes to use historical-critical methods, textual criticism, archaeology, and the study of original languages (Hebrew and Greek) to uncover the literal sense of the sacred texts — provided they remain faithful to the Church's teaching authority and patristic tradition. The document is widely regarded as the *Magna Carta* of modern Catholic biblical studies.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was issued on the 50th anniversary of Pope Leo XIII's *Providentissimus Deus* (1893), which had opened the door to critical biblical study while defending Scripture against rationalist attacks. In the intervening decades, archaeological discoveries in Palestine, the study of ancient Near Eastern texts, and the development of textual criticism had transformed biblical scholarship. Pius XII wrote during World War II (1943), a time of profound global upheaval, and frames the return to Scripture as a source of consolation, moral renewal, and peace. The document also responds to anxieties within the Church that modern critical methods threatened traditional doctrines of inspiration and inerrancy.

## Key Themes

- **Inspiration and inerrancy** — The sacred writers are "living and reasonable instruments" of the Holy Spirit; inspiration extends to all parts of Scripture and is incompatible with error.
- **Legitimacy of critical methods** — Textual criticism, archaeology, philology, and the study of ancient Near Eastern literary forms are not threats but essential tools for recovering the literal sense.
- **Primacy of the literal sense** — The interpreter's primary task is to discern what the inspired author intended; spiritual interpretation is valid only where clearly intended by God.
- **Original languages over the Vulgate alone** — The Council of Trent's endorsement of the Vulgate was juridical rather than critical, and recourse to the Hebrew and Greek originals is now "almost demanded."
- **Freedom within tradition** — Few passages have been definitively interpreted by the Church; in the remainder, Catholic scholars enjoy "true liberty of the children of God" to explore and debate.
- **Pastoral purpose of Scripture** — Study is ordered toward preaching, spiritual nourishment, and the salvation of souls, not mere academic curiosity.
- **Rejection of both narrow traditionalism and arbitrary allegorizing** — Exegetes must avoid timidity toward everything new *and* fanciful spiritualizing that ignores the literal text.

## Significance

*Divino Afflante Spiritu* is the single most important modern papal document on Scripture. It gave Catholic scholars formal permission to engage seriously with historical-critical methods at a time when Protestant biblical criticism had long since embraced them, while Benedictine and Jesuit scholars at the Pontifical Biblical Institute had already been preparing the ground. Its endorsement of textual criticism paved the way for critical editions of the Vulgate (the *Nova Vulgata*) and the Greek New Testament. The encyclical's ideas directly shaped the approach of the Second Vatican Council's *Dei Verbum* (1965), which deepened and extended its principles. By reconciling rigorous scholarship with Catholic doctrine, Pius XII effectively ended the "anti-modernist" chill on biblical studies in the Church and inaugurated a flourishing of Catholic exegesis that continues to this day.
