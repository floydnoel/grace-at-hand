## Summary

*Libentissime Quidem* (1745) is an encyclical of Pope Benedict XIV reinforcing the Church's discipline on Lenten fasting. It mandates a single daily meal for all who are bound to fast, strictly forbids mixing meat and fish at the same meal, and sets rigorous conditions for granting dispensations — requiring verified urgent necessity, written attestation from physicians, and prior consultation with the Apostolic See. The letter also replies at length to objections raised by the Archbishop of Compostela and other theologians who had questioned earlier apostolic letters on the same subject.

## Historical Context

Benedict XIV reigned from 1740 to 1758, an era in which the Church was implementing the disciplinary reforms of the Council of Trent (1545–1563). Widespread laxity had crept into Lenten observance: many bishops routinely dispensed entire cities or dioceses from abstinence, and "sophists" (the pope's word) had produced a tangle of conflicting theological opinions that eroded the fast. A severe epidemic of chest inflammation (likely influenza or pneumonia) swept Europe in 1730, 1733, and 1740, creating pressure for wholesale dispensations. The document also reflects the global reach of Catholic discipline — it discusses a request from missionaries on the island of San Domenico (Hispaniola) in the French Americas.

## Key Themes

- **Primacy of Lenten discipline** — the fast is an ancient institution and a non-negotiable part of Catholic life, rooted in mortification of the flesh.
- **Single-meal rule** — even those permitted to eat meat during a fast may eat only one meal per day, with no second table or evening collation beyond what fasting custom allows.
- **No mixing of foods** — meat and fish may never be served at the same meal, even when a dispensation from abstinence has been granted.
- **Strict conditions for dispensations** — only genuine and urgent necessity (verified in writing by reputable physicians) justifies exempting an individual, let alone a whole community.
- **Episcopal accountability** — bishops are told not to request dispensations lightly; the pope warns of the "shame of rejection" and reminds them that habitual leniency "opens a gateway for everyone to transgress the laws."
- **Pastoral realism** — poverty, high food prices, and military occupation are examined and rejected as general grounds for dispensation, but a genuinely contagious epidemic may warrant a community-wide exception.
- **Papal vs. episcopal authority** — Benedict XIV insists that matters of discipline are decided by the Supreme Pontiff, while praising the Archbishop of Compostela for deferring to Rome.

## Significance

*Libentissime Quidem* is a landmark document in the history of Catholic fasting discipline. It represents Benedict XIV's broader project — often called a "golden age" of canon law scholarship — to centralize authority, curb pastoral laxity, and standardize practice across the universal Church. The encyclical shows the papacy functioning as a practical tribunal for resolving concrete disciplinary controversies, down to the level of what foods may be eaten and when. Its rulings on fasting remained the baseline for Catholic practice until the post-conciliar reforms of Pope Paul VI (*Paenitemini*, 1966). The letter also offers a vivid window into 18th-century pastoral life — the interplay of doctors, bishops, theologians, missionaries, and ordinary faithful navigating sickness, scarcity, and salvation.
