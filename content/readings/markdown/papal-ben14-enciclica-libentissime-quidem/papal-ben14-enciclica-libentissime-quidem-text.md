# Libentissime Quidem

**La Santa Sede**

ENCYCLICAL LIBENTISSIME QUIDEM OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Venerable Brothers, Patriarchs, Primates, Metropolitans, Archbishops, and Bishops who are in peace and communion with the Apostolic See. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. Truly, it is with the greatest joy that We seize every opportunity that presents itself to Us to urge you to devote assiduous care to the restoration of ecclesiastical discipline. For we are aware that the dignity of the Supreme Pontificate—to which we, though unworthy, have been elevated—is accompanied by a duty of care toward the other Churches, entrusted to us who are well aware of the limitations of our own strength. Therefore, the subject of this Letter concerns exclusively the fast prescribed for the faithful during the season of Lent, in accordance with an ancient custom and the law of the Church. We consider it unnecessary to deliver a lengthy discourse on the institution of Lent or to cite all the Acts of the Fathers and Councils on this matter—which would not be difficult for Us but would rather be inappropriate and in no way befitting you, since we consider you eminent in doctrinal merit and in your knowledge of the Canons and Sacred History. Therefore, we were prompted to write this Letter by those stern words of the Council of Trent, which, in Session 25 on the Reformation, prescribed that bishops devote all their efforts to ensuring that the faithful entrusted to their care apply the prescriptions of the Sacred Canons with great devotion... “So that they may be obedient to all the rules, and in particular to those that lead to the mortification of the flesh, such as the choice of foods and fasting.” As soon as we were appointed to lead the Church of Bologna (to which we were transferred from the See of Ancona by concession of Clement XII, of blessed memory), we issued a Mandate that is included in the first book, fifteenth chapter, of Our Instructions for Men of the Church: in it we outlined several impediments, on the basis of which we were of the opinion that those who, for just cause and by prior intervention of a legitimate authority, did not observe Lenten abstinence should not be exempted from the rules of fasting; these individuals, we repeat, must not be exempted from fasting, since it is not permissible for them to set the table twice a day rather than once, with the addition of an evening meal that was customarily granted to those who fast. Finally, upon our accession to this Papal See, we have once again examined the entire matter and have also entrusted distinguished individuals with the task of examining it with the utmost diligence and subsequently presenting their opinions to Us. Having completed these steps, we decided to put an end to this controversy and decreed that in the future, no particular or general permission to consume meat at the table during a fast or Lent shall be granted to any city or village, unless there is an agreement to observe the fast or to have a single meal. It should also be noted that it is by no means permissible to serve both meat and fish at the same meal. Indeed, having resolved matters that were all too complex using this criterion, we have decided to prescribe a single daily meal even for those who were regularly permitted to eat meat, when a fast is proclaimed; \[we have decided\] to eradicate that perverse custom, already all too widespread among many, of serving meat with fish at the table, after they have been dispensed from the obligation of Lenten abstinence, especially because eating fish can seriously compromise their health. These provisions are clearly set forth in Our Apostolic Letters, which We sent in the form of a Brief to all the bishops of the Catholic world in the year 1741; the first Letter begins with \*Non ambigimus\*; the other with \*In suprema\*. We have certainly never granted anyone permission to eat meat during Lent or any other period of fasting, except under the condition stated and set forth in writing that We have mentioned above. Likewise, We felt great joy when We received in response numerous letters and your decrees, which are entirely in accordance with Our Apostolic Letters, inasmuch as through them you seek to urge the people to devoutly observe the prescribed discipline. Meanwhile, following the publication of the very Apostolic Letters We mentioned earlier, several quibbles have been raised by some, as We, for the rest, had suspected and easily foreseen. Numerous questions were submitted to Us, which in Our judgment are of little significance, since they are either refuted or resolved by the words of the Law itself or by a thorough examination. Nevertheless, We entrusted the Sacred Tribunal of the Penitentiary—which is well-versed and highly experienced in such matters—with the task of commenting on these questions, and it provided a full and convincing answer to each one, whenever necessary. The Archbishop of Compostela, the chief inquisitor in the Kingdoms of Spain, who recently passed away, sent Us a long letter in which he enumerated several difficulties that conflicted with Our Apostolic Letters, regarding how to put them into practice, on the grounds that quite a few theologians had arrived at differing opinions and judgments in resolving certain issues to which Our Apostolic Letters provided the basis and occasion; therefore, he sought Our judgment on them. Indeed, the Archbishop’s letter took Us by surprise, as We were already overwhelmed by very serious and difficult circumstances. However, as We reviewed the issues set forth in the letter, it seemed to Us that it was not appropriate to respond to them, as if, once resolved, there would remain no obstacle or impediment to Our Apostolic Letters, but rather that they would not serve the purpose desired by the quibbling objectors. Moreover, the Archbishop’s letter was replete with many obscure expressions and arguments; therefore, although many urged Us not to give any reply to the Archbishop, We nevertheless granted him a response, discussing the most significant assertions in his letter and summarizing all the issues. First, We stated that Our delayed response was due to Our busy schedule; We praised his zeal and also warmly approved of the fact that, being uncertain and doubtful, he had turned to this Holy See to seek counsel. We then did not fail to address the issues raised, while reminding him of the teaching of the Supreme Pontiffs, to whom it belongs to enact laws, to decide matters of discipline, and to pay no heed to those who raise grounds for doubt capable of disrupting and subverting the institutions. No sooner had the letter been sent than the Archbishop passed away; therefore, Our responses contained in the letter were neither received nor made known to those who had raised the issues. Subsequently, they nevertheless presented to Us quite a few of the same questions raised by the Spanish bishops, so We referred to the same responses We had previously given to the Archbishop of Compostela. Furthermore, the most distinguished Italian theologians did not fail to comment admirably on Our Apostolic Letters of the year 1741: this was also the case with the response We gave to the Archbishop of Compostela on July 8, 1744, which they included as a complete preface in their books. But since these books may not yet have reached you, or because, distracted by other commitments, you have not been able to peruse them, we are also enclosing with this Encyclical Letter the one we wrote (as has been repeatedly stated) to the Archbishop of Compostela, so that you may know what discipline you must observe in your dioceses and so that you may not be deterred by any difficulties that might be raised by sophists. “To the Venerable Brother, the Archbishop of Compostela. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brother, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. If Your Brotherhood will reflect with Us on how many grave and severe concerns weigh upon Us—and, especially in these difficult circumstances, how deeply Our concern for all the Churches, imposed upon Our frailty, engages Us— you will certainly not be surprised that the reply to that letter—in which you asked that certain disputes arising in Spain be resolved with apostolic authority, concerning Our two letters to all the bishops, dated May 30 and August 22 of the year 1741, in which We sought to rein in, within just limits, the marked laxity regarding ecclesiastical fasting. Rather, you will be led to reflect on Our singular love for the most devout Spanish Church, which is why We—though burdened by an immense volume of problems—place Our authority at your disposal to clear the path to salvation of every doubt. Although, in publishing the aforementioned Constitutions, We had no other purpose than to curb the arbitrariness of a few theologians who, placing too much confidence in their own ingenuity, take pleasure in outlandish opinions; although it was neither idleness nor arrogance on Our part to establish what could be deduced through careful examination from the rules of fasting proposed by Us; nevertheless, with singular paternal charity, We embrace the Spaniards, so tenaciously devoted to the Roman See, and in this regard We have taken your supplications into consideration and willingly turned Our mind to alleviating your anxiety; therefore, We have decided to settle the issues you have highlighted Ourselves. They are therefore set forth in the following terms: I) Are those rules—which, in Our aforementioned Letters in the form of a Brief, prescribe a single meal and prohibit the mixing of foods—to be rejected, even by a categorical precept? We reply: Those who grant permission to eat meat during prohibited times are gravely at fault for granting such permission unless they attach these two conditions: namely, to consume only one meal per day and to keep foods separate. On the other hand, those who make use of such permissions are obliged, by strict provision, to fulfill both conditions. II) May those who have been granted permission to eat meat consume, as part of an evening snack, the portion of meat permitted to those who are fasting? We respond: It is not permissible; rather, it is obligatory to consume the same food and drink as those who fast with a righteous and scrupulous conscience. III) Are those who are permitted to eat meat during fasting and must consume a single meal required to observe the time prescribed for those who are fasting? We declare: They must observe it. IV) What are the permitted foods that cannot be combined with prohibited foods? We answer: Meat itself is a permitted food for those who have been granted permission to eat meat, whereas fish is a prohibited food to such an extent that the two foods cannot be consumed together. However, those who are permitted to eat only eggs and dairy products are not prohibited from eating fish. V) Does the precept against mixing these two types of food also apply to Sundays during Lent? We affirm: It does apply. VI) Does this rule also apply to those who, according to the Crusade Bull, are permitted to eat eggs and dairy products? We reply: In Our aforementioned Apostolic Letters, nothing has been decided regarding the comprehensive Letter of the Crusade. Therefore, those who adhere to it should ponder its spirit with the utmost care and act in accordance with its provisions. They should also be careful not to consider themselves exempt, under any flimsy pretext, from the laws prescribed therein. VII) Are the two aforementioned precepts also valid outside of Lent? The answer is: Both precepts are valid outside of Lent; that is, the precept regarding the single meal, along with the other laws set forth in the second and third answers to these questions; and also the other precept prohibiting the mixing of lawful foods with forbidden ones, as specified in the fourth question. Every question that, as you write, has raised doubts in your mind arising from Our frequently cited Apostolic Letters has been thoroughly clarified for you, Venerable Brother. In this instance, We commended your decision to consult the Supreme Roman See, so that the Pastors may, with a free and unwavering hand, proceed in instructing the flock in sound doctrine; in the decision you made, we recognized the Spanish devotion that holds that there is no certainty in itself unless it comes from the Chair of Saint Peter—a devotion through which Spain has earned such glorious and splendid renown and the fruit of uncorrupted faith. On the other hand, you must avoid (and this applies above all to you, who hold the distinguished office of Archbishop and Inquisitor) any room for debate or doubt when examining the Constitutions of the Apostolic See, due to an excessive haste to conclude the matter. Furthermore, you must devote assiduous effort to resolving among yourselves, once and for all, those doubts that easily arise in discussion—given the diversity of minds—so that hearts do not waver in endless debates, especially since the Apostolic Constitutions themselves clearly reveal the purpose they aim at and the end toward which everything that seems to raise doubt can readily be directed. If those who raised several doubts on this matter had realized this, they would have untangled the knot to their own advantage. It was evident, in fact, that we had no other intention than to rein in the vivid imaginations of certain theologians who, going far beyond the bounds of the sacred fast and partly forgetting a divinely ordained institution intended to mortify the body, were appeasing this fatal adversary of the spirit. If some were to learn immediately what needs to be defined in the proposed questions, it would cause no trouble for you, nor for Us, who are pressed on all sides by urgent commitments. They would not take away the time that we nevertheless willingly devote to showing you the right path, out of the singular and solicitous love we bear for your people, in order to clarify how to encourage your progress in matters of this nature. It remains, Venerable Brother, for Us to affectionately impart to you the Apostolic Blessing, and We do so wholeheartedly, as a pledge of Our special benevolence. Given on July 8, 1744.” This being the case, it seems to Us that We have sufficiently fulfilled Our duty, so that those who are permitted to eat meat—when fasting is prescribed for the faithful during Lent or outside of Lent— do not exceed the limit of a single meal, nor allow themselves to serve both meat and fish at the table, nor devise sophisms to obscure the Law with darkness. There remain a few matters that still require Our intervention. For many years now, We have observed that not only have many people in every Italian city been dispensed from Lenten abstinence by your decision, but that, moreover, We are persistently asked to exempt the entire citizenry and the entire diocese from abstinence. Consequently, in certain communities and dioceses, all the faithful—for many years now and without any distinction—have been consuming meat during Lent. This is a matter of the greatest concern to Us, all the more so because We have learned from reliable witnesses that in certain northern regions the consumption of milk and eggs is freely permitted throughout the entire Lenten season, since for several years this permission was requested and granted by the Apostolic See; and therefore those people became convinced that it was not necessary to appeal again to the Pope to obtain such permission, but that the previous concessions obtained year after year would suffice, so that the use of milk and eggs during Lent would be confirmed. Furthermore, we believe that the following words of the Council of Trent must be given the utmost consideration: “Just as it is sometimes beneficial for the public good to relax the strictness of the law, so that the common good may be more fully served when conditions of necessity arise; so too does the frequent violation of the law—and being lenient toward petitioners, whether by example or through a deliberate choice of persons and matters—amount to opening a gateway for everyone to transgress the laws” (De Reformatione, sess. 25, chap. 18). In Our Apostolic Letters, which We sent to all Catholic bishops in the year 1741, We sought to close the door to this corruption with the method and words that We recall here once again: “The proper paternal zeal of each of you and the obligation of charity require that you make known to all and proclaim that no one, without just cause and without the counsel of both physicians, neither to the multitude, nor to the people, nor to the citizens, nor to people of undoubted integrity (except in cases of most grave and urgent necessity and in the cases prescribed by the Sacred Statutes of the Canons, with due reverence toward this Holy Apostolic See), be granted a dispensation from Lenten fasting whenever it is necessary; nor is it to be usurped with resolute audacity, nor may it be requested of the Church with pride and arrogance, as we have learned is customary elsewhere.” We have therefore followed this rule when We declared the multitude, the people, or an entire citizenry exempt from Lenten abstinence. First of all, the local bishop had to request this dispensation from Us and attest that it was a matter of grave and urgent necessity. Thereafter, the consumption of meat was prohibited if it seemed to Us that the consumption of eggs and dairy products was sufficient. Only then was permission granted to eat meat—when this was deemed absolutely necessary based on the Bishop’s testimony, or when the mere provision of eggs and dairy products proved an insufficient remedy for the need. Whenever We subsequently abolished abstinence by Our own decision, We nevertheless prescribed specific days on which total abstinence was to be observed—naturally on Ash Wednesday, during the Four Seasons, on the vigils to be observed according to the precept, and on the weekdays preceding Palm Sunday. At times we also included the entire week of Ash Wednesday; at other times we prescribed abstinence even on weekdays and Saturdays, although the authority we had granted permitted only the consumption of milk and eggs. Finally, we always added the rule that the law of fasting—which prescribes only one meal a day and prohibits serving meat and fish together at the table—must not be violated in any way. While we rigorously examined the grounds for this decision of ours, we were not entirely at ease, for we were concerned and doubtful that the reason put forward by a local bishop—which we considered sufficient to grant a dispensation from Lenten abstinence—might not, in fact, constitute a true and urgent necessity. In fact, we have always kept in mind the intention not to do anything contrary to reason, nothing rash or ill-advised. Likewise, the words that Saint Bernard addressed to Pope Eugene are deeply engraved in our hearts: “Do what you can; but the question is whether you ought to act and in what manner” (De consideratione, Book 3, Chapter 4). Nevertheless, we placed our trust in those who had earned it—namely, the local bishops. For in no other way, in fact, can one be attentive to the individual Churches and dioceses unless one has confidence in the bishops who administer them. But in the future, so that this matter may be addressed in the most prudent manner, do not find it burdensome to give your attention to the arguments we have presented to you. Do not overlook the fact that our predecessor, Innocent III (chap. \*Consilium de observatione ieiuniorum\*), included a genuine and manifest illness among the just causes for dispensation from abstinence from meat during times of fasting: “Regarding those who fall ill during Lent or other recurring fasts, and request permission to eat meat, we reply that since necessity is not subject to the law, you can and must grant the request of the sick when necessity demands it, so as to spare them greater danger.” In the same way, long before Innocent III, the Fathers of the Eighth Council of Toledo, held in the year 653, ruled: “Anyone who, out of unavoidable necessity and due to exhaustion resulting from evident frailty, or even due to incapacity resulting from old age, dares to eat meat during the days of Lent, will not only be guilty on Resurrection Sunday but also excluded from Holy Communion on that day... Those, however, whom either advanced age, extreme frailty, or compelling necessity may affect, must not dare to violate these prohibitions before obtaining permission from a priest” (Council of Toledo, can. 9). There is no one who does not understand that such reasons are sufficient for some to be deemed exempt from the rules of fasting and abstinence, but not for the people or an entire citizenry to obtain the same privilege. For who, indeed, could possibly believe that all the citizens of a single city or diocese are afflicted at the same time by a most serious illness, or that they are all in the same position of contracting a dangerous disease? Unless we are referring to those diseases that arise from inclement weather or infection—which we will discuss shortly—who could ever believe that the entire population of a diocese could be weakened by general languor or exhausted by extreme old age? In truth, the fact that fish or eggs happen to be sold at a high price should not be considered sufficient grounds to exempt a community or a people from Lenten fasting and to permit them to eat meat. In fact, cities are inhabited by both poor and rich citizens; among them, some earn their living through labor and sweat, while others, on the other hand, have an abundance of goods and riches. Therefore, if one must pay a high price to buy fish, this benefits the poor citizens and not the rich; this is the argument put forward by certain theologians of not overly strict discipline, whose opinion we corrected in the third book, ninth chapter, which we had printed in Bologna. Furthermore, the argument that cities or dioceses where armies are encamped should be exempted from Lenten abstinence must be considered entirely futile, since the soldiers stationed in those places do not observe the rules of abstinence at all. This circumstance should spur the citizens to practice fasting with devotion and according to the rite, precisely so that the soldiers, struck by such virtue of temperance, may follow their example. Only one argument can be put forward in favor of this just cause: the garrisons cause such a shortage of vegetables and oil for the citizens—even though they do not observe Lenten fasting—that the prices of those products rise sharply. Therefore, the rules we have just outlined must be applied: fish and eggs should be compared with the highest prices. Consequently, exceptional illnesses, however frequent, should not be cited as grounds for exemption from abstinence, unless such common illnesses are widespread among all citizens due to some form of atmospheric pollution; and finally, the prices of vegetables, oil, fish, and eggs must not be considered so significant that the entire citizenry or the diocese demands to be exempted from the canonical Lenten fast and from temperance. If, then, you ask Us for well-founded and legitimate reasons to obtain such permission, We shall examine two in particular: the first derives from canon law, while the other has been drawn from experience itself, the teacher of truth. Innocent III, whom we cited above (in the same chapter, \*Consilium de observatione ieiuniorum\*) replied with these words to the Archbishop of Braga, who asked what penance should be imposed on those who, during Lent, had not abstained from meat due to extreme poverty and a shortage of food—as a result of which many were dying of hunger: “If you then ask what penance should be assigned to those who are forced to eat meat on Lenten days, while times of famine loom and therefore a large part of the population perishes from lack of food, we reply that in such a contingency we hold that such people are not to be punished. For them, however, offer prayers to God, and do not (to a certain extent) attribute any guilt to them; for it is characteristic of sound minds to fear guilt where guilt is found to a lesser degree.” If a bishop of our day, inspired by the example of Antistite Bracarense, were to seek the same opinion from the Apostolic See, he would be regarded as inept and naive. But in that most blessed century for the Church, the precept of Lent was held in high regard, as is right. Therefore, those who disobeyed it entirely were investigated with great severity, even if a just cause intervened to make them appear exempt from that precept; nor was any consideration given to the interests of princes, even though their safety was deemed linked to the welfare of the state, as We demonstrated at length in the fifteenth chapter of the first book of Our Institutions. But setting aside the many other issues on this subject that We could bring up, first of all, we have inferred from the response of Innocent III that an entire citizenry may be dispensed from Lenten fasting when there are insufficient supplies of those goods without which the precept of Lent cannot be observed. Therefore, if in some place it is truly impossible to obtain either oil or fish, then it is lawful to permit the inhabitants of that place to consume milk and eggs. If even these foods are truly in short supply, then even the healthy are granted permission to eat meat, provided always that the fast is observed. The island of San Domenico in South America, in matters of temporal governance, is subject to our most beloved son in Christ, Louis, the Most Christian King of France, and no bishop has authority over him. The Prefect of the Fathers of the Society of Jesus, in the year 1742, asked Us whether, based on a concession granted to him by the Apostolic See to dispense with the consumption of meat, eggs, and dairy products during times of fasting and Lent, it could be inferred that he, too, was granted the authority to exempt not only individual persons but also a multitude of men and all the inhabitants of his district, whenever he judged, before God, that such a measure would be beneficial. Having received the reply that he had been granted the authority to grant exemptions to individual persons, but not to a large number of people or to all the inhabitants of his district, he invoked that concession. As reasons, he cited the poisonous nature of the fish; the small number of fishermen who, fishing far from the island, delivered fish that were foul-smelling or spoiled due to excessive heat; the poverty of that population, which could not afford the oil needed for Lenten foods; and finally, the scarcity of oil and legumes, which in that region easily spoil or are devoured by worms. Therefore, after thoroughly discussing the matter in Our presence at the General Congregation of the Holy Roman and Universal Inquisition on April 12, 1742, and taking into account that this was a very remote region from which it was impossible to submit an appeal to the Apostolic See every year; being sympathetic to the needs of the sick faithful on the islands under French jurisdiction in the Americas, we granted authority to the Prefects of the Missions in those places, in cases of true and unavoidable necessity and only from one year to the next, provided that the aforementioned state of necessity persisted (and in no other case or in any other manner), to allow the consumption and distribution among those same faithful peoples entrusted to their governance of the surplus of eggs, dairy products, and even meat during Lent, after having prescribed, at the very moment of that distribution, the observance of fasting with a single meal, and after having laid the burden of conscience upon the aforementioned Prefects should they fail to exercise the granted authority as prescribed. But now let us turn to experience. Fifteen years ago, throughout most of Europe, a disease causing chest inflammation raged to such an extent that this deadly plague spread from one province to another. This occurred in the years 1730, 1733, and 1740. Although this disease, which afflicted people of all ages and social classes, caused only moderate inflammation in the chest, it nevertheless inflicted extreme harm on the elderly, who were naturally already weakened by age; in fact, it was followed by acute and fatal fevers. The same disease also endangered even the youngest, as soon as they were afflicted with chest pain. Finally, the disease affected the chests to such an extent that very serious complications subsequently arose. Medical writers provide excellent testimony that on other occasions such a disease, causing chest inflammation with great danger, brought the patients to the brink of death. Therefore, if medical professors—summoned and seriously admonished to answer truthfully—unanimously admit (as We ourselves observed while still residing in Bologna) that the disease is of the nature described above, such that no age group can be free from it or remain unscathed; and they affirm that the same contagion threatens even those citizens considered safe and healthy; and that, furthermore, one contributes greatly to warding off or avoiding this plague if both the sick and the healthy abstain from fish, oil, and likewise from milk and eggs; if, I say, this is indeed the case, then beyond any doubt the plague must be judged a sufficient reason (based on experience and, having always been accepted, to be accepted in the future as well) to exempt the People—that is, the entire citizenry—from the precept against eating meat. No difficulty should prevent you from consulting the most distinguished physicians. You must also ensure that, whatever their opinion may be, they do not fail to put it in writing: if, in the end, this condition is not met, no permission whatsoever shall be granted to the citizenry or the diocese to eat meat or dairy products during Lent; this is absolutely necessary. It also happens that physicians, with incredible carelessness, are in the habit of deeming individual citizens exempt from the Lenten precept (and therefore you must sternly admonish them, lest they bring their souls to ruin through such leniency). When, then, it was proposed to introduce this measure for the benefit of all citizens, We ourselves observed how \[the doctors\] showed themselves to be distrustful, anxious, and concerned. Finally, if they care to review the medical treatises published in Italy and abroad by renowned authors, they will easily learn that these authors strongly recommend abstinence and frugality; they will learn to distinguish between different diseases; to demonstrate with sound reasoning that, in treating certain illnesses, it is by no means prescribed that broth and meat should replace fish, oil, and vegetables. They will also learn that it is beneficial for the sick to consume oil or milk, provided they abstain from meat as prescribed by fasting. Finally, they should investigate with the utmost diligence and write accurately about chest ailments (which generally affect everyone) so that those who have so far remained immune to the common plague may not be struck by the same illness. We felt it necessary to bring these matters to your attention. It remains only to urge you not to ask too readily for permission, for the entire community or diocese, to consume meat and dairy products during Lent. Indeed, it is necessary to finally put an end to the custom by which, for many years now in certain places, such permissions have been routinely granted. From experience, you will find that Lenten abstinence causes no harm to those who are frail or weak, and that there are very few among the sick or the dead who have perished for this reason. We ourselves have followed this customary rule in Rome and in Bologna (whose see still belongs to Us) during the season of Lent. If you, too, observe this rule and do not readily ask Us for permission to dispense an entire city or diocese from Lenten abstinence—or (if necessary, judge this for yourselves) do not submit a request for dispensation to Us unless the aforementioned conditions are met— then not only will the fruits We have already indicated come to fruition, but you will also avoid the shame of rejection and, moreover, you will compensate Us for the pain We feel when We do not grant any of your requests. Finally, with great love, We embrace you all and impart the Apostolic Blessing to you and your peoples. Given at the Residence of Castel Gandolfo, on June 10, 1745, the fifth year of Our Pontificate.
