## Summary

This encyclical, *Deiparae Virginis Mariae* ("Of the God-bearing Virgin Mary"), is a consultative letter Pope Pius XII sent to every bishop in the world asking whether the bodily Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary into heaven should be defined as a dogma of the Catholic faith. It details the groundswell of petitions from clergy, religious, and laity over the preceding century (1849–1940) requesting this definition, and calls on the bishops to survey their clergy and people and report back.

## Historical Context

Written in 1946 and issued quietly ("in forma del tutto reservata") to all bishops, the letter arrived just a year after the end of World War II. The Church was emerging from a period of immense global upheaval, and Pius XII was positioning Marian devotion as a source of hope and unity. He followed the precedent of Pius IX, who consulted the bishops before defining the Immaculate Conception in 1854. The bishops' overwhelmingly affirmative responses would lead to the formal definition of the Assumption in the apostolic constitution *Munificentissimus Deus* in 1950.

## Key Themes

- **Sensus fidelium** — The widespread, persistent devotion of the Christian people is treated as a genuine witness to the faith, prompting the hierarchy to consider formal definition.
- **Consultative governance** — Pius XII models a collegial approach, seeking input from the whole episcopate before exercising his teaching authority.
- **Marian devotion as pastoral consolation** — The encyclical frames Marian doctrine as a source of strength and hope for the faithful, especially relevant in the post-war period.
- **Continuity with precedent** — The pope explicitly follows the method of Pius IX before the definition of the Immaculate Conception, establishing a pattern for consulting the bishops on Marian dogmas.

## Significance

*Deiparae Virginis Mariae* is the immediate procedural precursor to the dogmatic definition of the Assumption (1950), one of only two ex cathedra definitions since Vatican I. It demonstrates how the Church's ordinary magisterium can survey the faith of the whole Church before solemnly defining a doctrine. Historically, it also marks one of the last major Marian dogmas and remains a model for episcopal consultation in preparation for infallible teaching.
