## Summary

*In Dominico Agro* (1761) is a papal encyclical by Clement XIII mandating that priests return to the *Roman Catechism* (the *Catechism of the Council of Trent*) as the exclusive, normative textbook for instructing the faithful. The pope warns that novelty-seeking theologians and the proliferation of unofficial catechisms have fractured doctrinal unity, sown confusion among the simple faithful, and created partisan rivalries in the Church. He orders a corrected reprint of the original Latin edition and a new vernacular edition, commanding bishops to enforce its use and to appoint only humble, peaceable teachers to the office of catechesis.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was issued on 14 June 1761, the third year of Clement XIII's pontificate, a period when the Catholic Church faced mounting pressure from Enlightenment rationalism, Jansenist rigorism, and febronianist calls for conciliar supremacy. The *Roman Catechism*—commissioned by the Council of Trent (1545–1563) and published in 1566 under St. Pius V—had been the Church's standard for a century, but by the mid-18th century, a flood of locally produced catechisms had eroded its authority. Clement's predecessor Benedict XIV had attempted some liturgical consolidation, but the problem of catechetical fragmentation persisted, exacerbated by learned disputes that filtered down to unsettle ordinary believers. The document reflects a pope responding to what he saw as a crisis of unity in teaching, at a time when the Church's institutional and doctrinal authority was broadly contested across Europe.

## Key Themes

- **Doctrinal unity through a single, approved text.** The *Roman Catechism* is presented as the bulwark against novelty; only teaching marked by universality, antiquity, and harmony should be proposed to the faithful.
- **Protecting simple believers from dangerous speculation.** The faithful should not be exposed to "peculiar ideas" of scholars, even Catholic ones, but only what is "necessary or useful for salvation." The pope draws on the Mosaic image of boundaries set around the holy mountain.
- **The danger of "contentions" among clergy.** Rival schools of thought (paraphrasing 1 Cor 1.12: "I am of Apollo, I of Cephas, I of Paul") are described as "fatal to God's greater glory"; they destroy the fruit of Christian discipline.
- **Episcopal vigilance and teacher qualifications.** Bishops are to enforce catechetical uniformity and appoint teachers who possess not merely theological knowledge but also humility, charity, and a peaceable spirit. "The salt of wisdom" must be accompanied by peace.
- **The illusion of novelty.** The love of novelty is presented as a diabolical temptation, and the proliferation of new catechisms as an act of "insolent" pride that breaks the bond of unity.

## Significance

*In Dominico Agro* represents one of the strongest papal mandates for catechetical centralization before the modern era. It reaffirms the Tridentine vision of a single, universally binding catechism and anticipates the later emphasis on uniformity found in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1992). The encyclical's insistence that the shepherds keep the flock from "trackless places" and novel opinions captures the Church's defensive posture in the late Baroque period. Its practical effect was to reinforce the *Roman Catechism* as the standard in seminaries and parishes for another century, until the First Vatican Council and the crisis of the Papal States shifted the Church's concerns. The document remains a significant witness to the perennial tension between theological exploration and pastoral prudence.
