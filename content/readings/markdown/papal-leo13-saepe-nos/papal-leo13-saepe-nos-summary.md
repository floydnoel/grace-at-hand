## Summary

*Saepe Nos* is a papal letter from Pope Leo XIII to the Irish bishops, condemning the tactics of "boycotting" and the "Plan of Campaign" used in the Irish Land War as morally unlawful. The Pope defends the authority of the Sacred Congregation's decree, rebukes those who resist it, and reaffirms that even a just cause does not justify unjust means.

## Historical Context

Written in June 1888, during the Irish Land War (1879–1882 and after), a period of intense agrarian agitation by tenant farmers against landlordism. The "Plan of Campaign" (where tenants collectively withheld rent) and "boycotting" (social and economic ostracism of landlords or agents) were the chief non-violent but coercive tactics. Leo XIII had already sent a legate to Ireland to investigate, and the Holy See had issued a decree through Propaganda Fide ruling these tactics unlawful, sparking backlash from nationalist and clerical figures who accused Rome of siding with the British establishment.

## Key Themes

- **Moral limits on political struggle** — even a just cause cannot justify intrinsically wrongful means like coercion, injustice, or violation of natural right.
- **Ecclesiastical authority** — the Church has the rightful office to judge the morality of human actions, and its decrees demand obedience from the faithful.
- **Pastoral solicitude** — the Pope defends his careful inquiry into Irish affairs and insists the decree arises from love for the Irish people, not indifference.
- **Lawful means for lawful ends** — Catholics must pursue agrarian reform through justice, charity, and public order, avoiding violence, secret societies, and the violation of contracts.
- **Unity and obedience** — the bishops are called to unite in conveying the decree and to correct those who stir up excitement against it.

## Significance

*Saepe Nos* is a key document in the Church's social teaching on the limits of protest and economic coercion. It set a firm precedent that the Church could and would intervene in socio-political movements to define moral boundaries, even at the cost of popular opposition. It also strained relations between the Irish hierarchy and nationalist movements for a time, though it did not diminish Leo XIII's broader support for Irish self-government and relief from landlord oppression.
