## Summary

*Benedictus Deus* (1750) is a papal bull in which Benedict XIV solemnly closes the Jubilee Year of 1750 and extends its plenary indulgence to all the faithful throughout the world. After giving thanks for the unprecedented crowds of pilgrims who came to Rome to visit the four major basilicas, the Pope grants a plenary indulgence to any repentant Catholic who confesses, receives Communion, and visits their diocesan cathedral plus three other churches on fifteen separate days within a six-month window. The bull provides detailed pastoral accommodations for sailors, prisoners, the sick, cloistered religious, children, and others unable to complete the prescribed visits, and grants confessors extensive faculties to absolve reserved sins and censures and to commute vows (with specific exceptions).

## Historical Context

1750 was a Jubilee (Holy Year) in the Catholic Church — a tradition dating to 1300 in which pilgrims visiting Rome could obtain a plenary indulgence. Benedict XIV had opened the Jubilee the previous year, and *Benedictus Deus* was issued on Christmas Day 1750 to extend its spiritual benefits beyond Rome to Catholics everywhere who could not make the journey. The mid-18th century saw the Church navigating the aftermath of the Council of Trent (1545–1563), consolidating papal authority, and refining the discipline of indulgences against Reformation critiques. Benedict XIV was a notably learned and reform-minded pope who codified much of Catholic canon law and wrote extensively on liturgy, beatification, and the sacraments. The bull reflects both the baroque piety of the era — with its emphasis on pilgrimage, relics, and the Roman basilicas — and the practical pastoral care characteristic of Benedict's pontificate.

## Key Themes

- **Plenary indulgence & the Jubilee tradition** — The bull is fundamentally an exercise of papal power to open the "treasury of merits," granting full remission of temporal punishment for sin to those who complete the prescribed spiritual works.
- **Universal accessibility** — Benedict goes to great lengths to ensure the indulgence is not limited to Romans: he extends the grace period to six months, accommodates travelers, sailors, the sick, prisoners, hermits, nuns, and children, and allows local ordinaries to substitute alternative pious works.
- **Papal authority & the power of the keys** — The Pope grounds his action in the "supreme power to bind and loose" granted to Peter, and the document bristles with jurisdictional language — reserving absolution from certain censures, specifying exceptions (e.g., the *Sacramentum Poenitentiae* limitation on absolving an accomplice in sins against the Sixth Commandment), and requiring episcopal obedience.
- **Sacramental discipline** — Confession and Communion are non-negotiable prerequisites; the bull reaffirms Tridentine norms on the sacrament of penance and the role of approved confessors.
- **Ecclesial unity & communion with Rome** — The repeated emphasis on obedience to the Apostolic See, on bishops acting in harmony with the Roman Church, and on the basilicas as symbols of the universal Church underscores the centrality of Rome in Catholic identity.
- **Harmony among Christian princes** — The prayers required of the faithful explicitly include petitions for peace among Catholic rulers, and the bull appeals to emperors and kings to support the Jubilee in their domains, linking spiritual renewal to civil order.

## Significance

*Benedictus Deus* is a classic example of the 18th-century papal Jubilee bull, demonstrating how the Church adapted the ancient Roman pilgrimage indulgence for a globalized Catholicism. Its detailed pastoral provisions — especially the delegation of faculties to local bishops and confessors — reflect Benedict XIV's characteristically juridical and pastoral style and anticipate later codifications of indulgence discipline (notably Paul VI's *Indulgentiarum Doctrina*, 1967). The bull also stands as a monument to the high-water mark of Baroque Catholicism, when the penitential pilgrimage to Rome was still the paradigmatic act of popular piety, before the Enlightenment, secularization, and the upheavals of the French Revolution reshaped the Church's relationship with the world.
