## Summary

*Humanae Vitae* (Latin: "Of Human Life") is an encyclical letter issued by Pope Paul VI on July 25, 1968, reaffirming the Catholic Church's traditional teaching on marriage, conjugal love, and the regulation of birth. It rejects artificial contraception as intrinsically immoral and insists that every marital act must remain open to the transmission of life.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was written in the aftermath of the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), a period of rapid social change, the sexual revolution, and the advent of the oral contraceptive pill (approved in 1960). Pope John XXIII had established a commission to study birth regulation, and Paul VI expanded it. The commission's majority reportedly favored allowing contraception, but Paul VI, after prayerful reflection, instead reaffirmed the traditional prohibition — a decision that shocked many inside and outside the Church.

## Key Themes

- **Inseparable connection between the unitive and procreative meanings of the marital act** — every conjugal act must remain open to life (section 12).
- **Responsible parenthood** is defined not by number of children but by conformity to God's design, with couples free to space births using natural rhythms but never artificial means.
- **Rejection of contraception, sterilization, and abortion** as intrinsically evil, regardless of intention or circumstances (section 14).
- **Legitimacy of natural family planning** — using infertile periods to avoid conception is morally licit, while artificial contraception is not (section 16).
- **Consequences of contraception** — feared to lead to marital infidelity, loss of respect for women, and government coercion in reproductive matters (section 17).
- **Appeal to virtue and grace** — the teaching, though difficult, is made possible through prayer, the sacraments, and self-mastery (sections 20–25).

## Significance

*Humanae Vitae* is one of the most controversial papal documents in modern history. It was met with widespread dissent, including public statements from bishops' conferences and theologians who argued for a more permissive approach. Yet Paul VI's successor, John Paul II, strongly reaffirmed its teaching in his "theology of the body" catechesis. The encyclical has become a touchstone for Catholic orthodoxy on sexual ethics, symbolizing both the Church's counter-cultural stance and the ongoing division between those who accept and those who reject its teaching.
