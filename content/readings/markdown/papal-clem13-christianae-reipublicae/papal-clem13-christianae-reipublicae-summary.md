## Summary

*Christianae Reipublicae* (1766) is Pope Clement XIII's forceful condemnation of the surge in anti-Christian books and writings that were spreading across Europe. The Pope warns that such works poison faith, corrupt morals, attack Church doctrine, and undermine the papacy itself. He calls on bishops to be vigilant, to identify and suppress harmful literature, and to rally Catholic civil authorities in defense of the faith.

## Historical Context

The encyclical appeared during the height of the Enlightenment, when French *philosophes* (Voltaire, Diderot, d'Alembert) and their allies were publishing works that challenged revelation, divine providence, the immortality of the soul, and the authority of the Church. The *Encyclopédie* and other clandestine texts were circulating widely. Clement XIII — a staunch defender of Jesuit and traditional Catholic orthodoxy — saw this as an existential crisis for Christian civilisation. The document also reflects the Church's anxiety in the years leading up to the Suppression of the Jesuits (1773) and the wider assault on ecclesiastical power in Bourbon courts.

## Key Themes

- **The danger of bad books** — writings that spread heresy, immorality, and irreligion are a plague that infects the home, the bedroom, and the mind, doing more lasting harm than evil company.
- **Rationalism over Revelation** — Clement attacks those who subject the mysteries of faith to human reason, depriving faith of its merit by demanding proof.
- **Materialism and mortal soul** — he condemns those who deny the immortality of the soul, treat matter as eternal, or exclude spirit from human nature.
- **Corruption of morals** — indecent writing and example lead the unwary into license, sin, and loss of faith.
- **Attack on the Papacy** — anti-Christian authors target the See of Peter directly, hoping that shattering the head will make it easier to tear apart the members (the Church).
- **Episcopal duty** — bishops must guard the deposit of faith, warn the faithful, and work with Catholic princes to suppress impious literature.

## Significance

The encyclical is one of Clement XIII's strongest statements on the Church's place in an increasingly hostile intellectual environment. It encapsulates the pre-Revolutionary Catholic response to Enlightenment rationalism and serves as a landmark in the Church's teaching on the dangers of unchecked publication — a theme later taken up by Gregory XVI in *Mirari Vos* (1832) and by the First Vatican Council. While largely forgotten outside historical circles, it illustrates the deepening fault line between Catholic orthodoxy and modernity that would culminate in the French Revolution barely two decades later.
