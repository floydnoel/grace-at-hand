## Summary

This papal bull, issued by Pope Alexander IV on 22 September 1255, grants graduates of the University of Salamanca the right to teach (*ius ubique docendi*) at any other general studium in Christendom without having to undergo a fresh examination — except at the universities of Paris and Bologna, which are explicitly excluded. It responds to a petition from the university and the King of Castile and León, who had complained that Salamanca graduates were being forced to re-sit exams every time they wished to teach elsewhere.

## Historical Context

The bull belongs to the great age of university foundation in the thirteenth century. Salamanca had been established as a *studium generale* by Alfonso IX of León around 1218 and later confirmed by Ferdinand III of Castile. By the 1250s it was competing with the older, established universities of Paris and Bologna for recognition and prestige. Graduates who had been rigorously examined at Salamanca found that other universities did not automatically recognise their qualifications, forcing them to repeat the examination process — a costly and demeaning obstacle. Pope Alexander IV, whose pontificate (1254–1261) was marked by the ongoing conflict between the papacy and the Hohenstaufen emperor and by efforts to consolidate ecclesiastical authority, here exercises the papal prerogative to regulate higher learning across Latin Christendom.

## Key Themes

- **Papal authority over universities** — The Pope asserts his right to determine the universal validity of academic degrees, treating the *studium generale* as an institution subject to the Holy See.
- **The *ius ubique docendi*** — The right to teach anywhere is the defining privilege of a *studium generale*; this bull effectively confirms Salamanca in that rank, albeit with a carve-out for Paris and Bologna.
- **Fairness to scholars** — The document recognises the burden of repeated examinations and seeks to protect students from harassment and expense, framing this as a pastoral concern for their quiet and freedom to study.
- **Selective precedence of Paris and Bologna** — The exemption of Europe's two oldest universities reflects their customary primacy; their teaching licenses retained superior standing that not even a papal privilege for Salamanca could override.

## Significance

*Dignum Arbitramur et Congruum* is one of the foundational documents of the University of Salamanca's corporate privileges. By granting Salamanca graduates the *ius ubique docendi* (except at Paris and Bologna), Alexander IV placed the Spanish university among the small circle of recognised *studia generalia* whose degrees carried trans-European authority. The bull also illustrates the medieval papacy's role as the ultimate guarantor of academic standards and mobility — a function that helped shape the international character of European university education. Its exclusion of Paris and Bologna highlights the gravitational pull of those two institutions, which retained unique prestige long after other universities had received similar privileges.
