## Summary

*Fides et Ratio* ("Faith and Reason") is an encyclical letter by Pope John Paul II, issued on 14 September 1998, on the relationship between faith and reason. The central metaphor — faith and reason as "two wings" by which the human spirit rises to the contemplation of truth — frames the document's argument that these two modes of knowing are not opposed but mutually supporting. The Pope argues that philosophy, when properly exercised, serves the search for ultimate truth, and that faith both purifies and elevates reason, while reason in turn gives intelligibility and credibility to faith.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was written near the close of the second millennium, a period John Paul II saw as marked by a "crisis of meaning" — the fragmentation of knowledge, the rise of nihilism and relativism, and the marginalisation of philosophy in favour of narrowly technical or scientific modes of thought. It follows in the tradition of Leo XIII's *Æterni Patris* (1879), which had revived Thomistic philosophy, and builds on the First Vatican Council's teaching on faith and reason as well as the Second Vatican Council's engagement with the modern world. The Pope was particularly concerned by the growing separation between faith and philosophical reason since the late medieval period, which he traces through rationalism, idealism, atheistic humanism, positivism, and ultimately nihilism — all of which he saw as undermining the human capacity to know objective truth.

## Key Themes

- **Faith and reason are complementary, not opposed**: Both originate from God and cannot contradict each other; the unity of truth is a fundamental premise.
- **Philosophy has a necessary role in theology**: Theology requires philosophy to articulate the *intellectus fidei* — the understanding of faith — especially in dogmatic, moral, and fundamental theology.
- **The dignity and autonomy of philosophy**: The Church does not canonise any particular philosophy, but insists philosophy must remain open to the truth and to the data of Revelation.
- **Recovery of metaphysics**: Philosophy must recover its sapiential (wisdom-seeking) dimension and its capacity for genuine metaphysical enquiry, transcending the merely empirical.
- **Critique of modern errors**: The encyclical identifies and rejects eclecticism, historicism, scientism, pragmatism, and nihilism as currents that undermine reason's orientation to truth.
- **Inculturation and universality**: Christian faith encounters different cultures without abandoning its Greco-Latin philosophical heritage; the Gospel's truth is universal and must not be relativised.
- **Saint Thomas Aquinas as model**: Thomas is presented as the supreme example of the harmony between faith and reason, with his philosophy of being offering a dynamic, open, and realist framework.
- **The diakonia of truth**: The Church has a duty to serve the truth, including by exercising discernment over philosophical opinions that threaten revealed truth.

## Significance

*Fides et Ratio* is the only papal encyclical devoted entirely to the relationship between philosophy and theology. It reaffirms the Catholic intellectual tradition's confidence in reason's ability to know truth, including truths about God, while insisting that reason is wounded by sin and needs the light of faith. The encyclical has been influential in Catholic higher education, especially in the formation of clergy and theologians, and remains a key reference point for debates about the role of philosophy in theology, the critique of relativism, and the Church's engagement with modern and postmodern thought.
