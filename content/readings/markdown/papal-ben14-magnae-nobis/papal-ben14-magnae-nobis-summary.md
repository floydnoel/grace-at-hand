## Summary

*Magnae Nobis* (1748) is an apostolic letter by Pope Benedict XIV addressed to the Polish episcopate, defending the Holy See against the false rumor that it had been granting matrimonial dispensations permitting Catholics to marry heretics without prior abjuration. Benedict XIV clarifies the Church's constant rule: such dispensations are never granted unless the heretical party first abjures heresy, and any apparent counterexamples stem from executors who failed to read the conditions attached to the letters.

## Historical Context

The mid-18th century was a period of significant religious division in Europe. Poland, though a staunchly Catholic kingdom, had substantial Protestant and Orthodox populations living alongside Catholics — a situation that inevitably produced mixed-faith marriage petitions. Rumors surfaced that Rome had been issuing dispensations without requiring heretics to abjure, which Benedict XIV saw as a calumny against the Apostolic See. He wrote to correct this, reaffirming the discipline of his predecessors (Urban VIII, Clement XI, Innocent X) and remanding responsibility to local ordinaries to scrutinize the letters they received.

## Key Themes

- **The Church's perpetual condemnation of Catholic-heretic marriages** — Benedict traces an unbroken line from earlier popes, citing Urban VIII (1624) and Clement XI (1706–7), showing that the rule was not novel.
- **Abjuration of heresy as a non-negotiable prerequisite** — No dispensation is valid unless the heretical party has formally renounced their error; this was enforced by the Holy Office and by oath.
- **The executor's duty to examine circumstances** — Dispensation letters often included conditional clauses (e.g., "provided the petitioners are truly of the orthodox faith") and required the local executor to verify that no hidden impediment (like heresy) existed.
- **Presumption of Catholic faith at the Roman curia** — When a petition came from a mixed region, officials presumed both parties were Catholic and inserted faith-based clauses; if the executor ignored these, the fault was his, not Rome's.
- **Distinction between dispensation and declaration** — In the case of a girl under marriageable age, the document argues that when "craft supplies for age," the matter may be a declaratory judgment (within a bishop's competence) rather than a true dispensation, but the executor must still confirm the minor is not marrying a heretic.

## Significance

*Magnae Nobis* is a relatively concise but theologically firm reaffirmation of the Church's discipline on mixed marriages in the period before the First Vatican Council. It illustrates the early modern papal emphasis on the protection of Catholic spouses and the religious education of children, a position that would remain largely unchanged until the 1917 Code of Canon Law and later developments in ecumenism. It also serves as a case study in administrative accountability: Benedict XIV carefully deflects blame from the Roman curia and places it on local executors who failed to read the conditions in their dispensation letters, reflecting a broader concern with bureaucratic rigor in the 18th-century Church.
