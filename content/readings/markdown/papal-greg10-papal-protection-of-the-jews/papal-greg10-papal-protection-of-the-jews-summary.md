## Summary

This document, a papal bull issued by Pope Gregory X in 1272, reaffirms the Church's traditional protection of Jews in Christendom. It prohibits forced baptism, violence against Jews, disruption of their festivals, and desecration of their cemeteries. It also addresses the blood libel — the false accusation that Jews murder Christian children for ritual purposes — declaring it baseless and ordering the release of Jews imprisoned on such charges.

## Historical Context

The bull was issued from Orvieto in the first year of Gregory X's pontificate (1271–1276). It largely repeats protections first articulated by Pope Gregory I in 598 and reaffirmed by earlier popes including Calixtus, Eugene, Alexander, Clement, Innocent, and Honorius. The mid-thirteenth century saw rising antisemitic violence across Europe, including blood libel accusations (e.g., Lincoln 1255), the Fourth Lateran Council's forced badge requirements (1215), and increasing pressure on Jewish communities. Gregory X wrote in direct response to Jewish appeals for protection.

## Key Themes

- **No forced baptism:** Baptism must be voluntary; unwilling converts "are not believed to possess the Christian faith."
- **Physical protection:** Christians may not seize, wound, torture, kill, or otherwise violence Jews without judicial authority.
- **Festival freedom:** Jews may not be disturbed during their celebrations.
- **Blood libel rebuttal:** The pope explicitly rejects ritual murder accusations, noting Jewish law forbids consuming blood, and observes that Christians sometimes hide their own children to extort money from Jews.
- **Equal testimony:** Jews can only be convicted on testimony that includes Jewish witnesses — an early form of the right to be judged by one's peers.
- **Cemetery protection:** Grave robbery for ransom is forbidden under threat of excommunication.

## Significance

The bull represents the high-water mark of medieval papal protection of Jews, codifying principles that stretched back to Gregory I. Its explicit rejection of the blood libel was unusual for its time and demonstrated a willingness to contradict popular prejudice with theological and legal reasoning. Though these protections were often ignored in practice and later popes retreated from them, the document remains an important witness to the Church's traditional teaching that Jews must not be coerced into conversion and deserve legal protection from violence.
