## Summary

*Aeterna Dei Sapientia* is an encyclical issued by Pope John XXIII in 1961 to commemorate the fifteenth centenary of the death of St. Leo the Great, Pope and Doctor of the Church. The document honors Leo's life, governance, and theological contributions while using his example to exhort the Church toward unity on the eve of the Second Vatican Council.

## Historical Context

Written in November 1961, the encyclical appeared just one year before the opening of the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), which John XXIII had unexpectedly announced in 1959. The world was deep in the Cold War, and the Church faced internal tensions over ecumenism, liturgical reform, and its relationship with the modern world. John XXIII invokes St. Leo’s fifth-century defense of Church unity — against heresies (Eutychianism, Nestorianism), imperial overreach, and schism with the East — as a model for the impending council's work.

## Key Themes

- **Papal primacy and the Chair of Peter** — Leo is presented as the embodiment of the Petrine office, the focal point of visible Catholic unity, and the successor to Peter's authority to teach and govern.
- **Unity of the Church** — The encyclical repeatedly insists that Christ founded one Church, that this unity is visible and hierarchical, and that all Christians are called to gather under the one Shepherd.
- **Christological orthodoxy** — Leo’s *Tome to Flavian* and his role at Chalcedon (451) are celebrated for defining the two natures of Christ against monophysite error.
- **The bishop of Rome as the guarantor of faith** — The document emphasizes that St. Leo exercised infallible teaching authority and that this same authority resides in his successors.
- **Ecumenical longing** — John XXIII expresses hope that separated Christians will recognize the marks of the true Church and return to unity with Rome, a theme that anticipates the council’s ecumenical outreach.
- **The priesthood of the faithful** — A quieter theme: through baptism, all Christians are made kings and priests; confirmed and nourished by the Eucharist, they are transformed into Christ.

## Significance

The encyclical is both a tribute to a major patristic figure and a programmatic document for the Second Vatican Council. It reaffirms the doctrine of papal primacy and the necessity of visible unity under the Roman pontiff at a moment when the Church was preparing to engage the modern world and other Christian communities. While Leo's emphases on papal authority and intransigent orthodoxy reflect a pre-conciliar sensibility, the document also captures John XXIII's characteristic pastoral optimism — the hope that the council would rekindle unity, peace, and the Church's missionary zeal.
