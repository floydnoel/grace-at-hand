## Summary

This encyclical, addressed to the hierarchy and clergy of the Province of Albania, reaffirms a decree from the 1703 Provincial Council (held under Pope Clement XI) that forbids giving Turkish or Muslim names to Christian children or adults at Baptism, and prohibits Christians from accepting or tolerating being called by such names — even when done to gain tax exemptions, trading advantages, or to avoid penalties. Pope Benedict XIV calls the practice a "shameful concealment of the Christian Faith, akin to infidelity," and warns that neglecting this discipline brings dishonor upon the Church and harm to souls.

## Historical Context

Written in the mid-18th century (1744, the fourth year of Benedict XIV's pontificate), the encyclical addresses the situation of Catholics living under Ottoman rule in Albania and neighboring regions. The Ottoman Empire's *millet* system imposed higher taxes and legal restrictions on non-Muslims, creating strong incentives for Christians to appear Muslim — whether by adopting Muslim names, using them in daily life, or even having them formally given at Baptism. The earlier 1703 council and Benedict's own prior encyclical *Inter omnigenas* (also 1744, for the Kingdom of Serbia) had already condemned this practice. The present letter was prompted by reports that the abuse persisted, indicating ongoing pastoral failure and pressure on Christian identity in a Muslim-majority state.

## Key Themes

- **Christian identity and public witness** — Adopting Muslim names, even for practical gain, is treated as a grave sin because it conceals one's faith and creates public scandal.
- **Pastoral vigilance** — Bishops, parish priests, and missionaries are commanded to enforce the ban strictly; negligence would bring dishonor on the entire province.
- **The integrity of the sacrament of Baptism** — The naming of the newly baptized is not a trivial matter; giving a non-Christian name undermines the sacrament's meaning.
- **Moral integrity over temporal advantage** — No exemption from taxes, no facilitation of trade, and no avoidance of penalties can justify even the appearance of apostasy.
- **Continuity of discipline** — Benedict XIV explicitly confirms and extends the decree of his predecessor Clement XI and his own prior letter, reinforcing that this is settled Catholic teaching, not a new imposition.

## Significance

*Quod Provinciale* is a concrete example of 18th-century Catholic discipline in contexts of religious persecution or significant non-Christian cultural pressure. It illustrates the Church's insistence that the faith must be professed openly, even when doing so comes at a material cost. The encyclical also reflects the practical challenges of the *propaganda fide* mission territories under Ottoman rule, where the boundary between accommodation and apostasy was tested daily. In Catholic moral theology, the document is frequently cited in discussions of religious indifferentism, the duty of external profession of faith, and the limits of dissimulation in hostile environments.
