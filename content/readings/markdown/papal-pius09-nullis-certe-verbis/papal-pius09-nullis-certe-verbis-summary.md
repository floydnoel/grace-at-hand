## Summary

*Nullis Certe Verbis* is an encyclical issued by Pope Pius IX on January 19, 1860, defending the temporal sovereignty of the Papal States against revolutionary uprisings and responding to a letter from Emperor Napoleon III of France, who had urged the Pope to voluntarily cede the rebellious provinces of Emilia. The Pope refuses this counsel, arguing he cannot surrender territories that belong not to his person but to the universal Catholic Church, and that to do so would violate solemn oaths, encourage further rebellion, and undermine legitimate civil authority everywhere.

## Historical Context

Italy was in the throes of the *Risorgimento* — the nationalist movement for unification. By 1860, revolutionary forces had seized the papal provinces of Emilia (including Bologna and Ravenna), and the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia was consolidating control over much of the peninsula. Napoleon III, who had initially been sympathetic to the papacy, shifted toward accommodation with the nationalists after the 1859 war with Austria. His published letter to Pius IX urged the Pope to accept the loss of the rebellious provinces as a settled fact. Pius IX, who had already condemned these developments in the 1859 encyclical *Qui Nuper*, here forcefully restates the Church's claim.

## Key Themes

- **Temporal sovereignty as necessary for spiritual liberty**: The civil power of the Roman Pontiff is divinely ordained so that the Pope, "never subject to any power, might exercise in full liberty" his apostolic ministry.
- **Inviolability of Church patrimony**: The Papal States are not a dynastic possession but the "patrimony of blessed Peter," belonging to all Catholics; the Pope cannot alienate them.
- **Rebellion does not confer legitimacy**: The fact that revolts have occurred does not make them just; civil sovereignty cannot be rightfully diminished by force.
- **Providence and martyrdom**: Pius IX frames his refusal as following the footsteps of his predecessors, willing to suffer or even lay down his life rather than "desert the cause of God, the Church, and justice."
- **Appeal to the universal Church**: The encyclical rallies bishops and the faithful worldwide to defend the temporal dominion, describing the response from hundreds of thousands of Catholics as a source of consolation.

## Significance

The encyclical represents the definitive hardening of the papacy's position against Italian unification and the loss of the Papal States — a stance that would culminate in the 1870 capture of Rome and the Pope's self-imposed confinement as the "Prisoner of the Vatican." It illustrates the 19th-century Church's deep conviction that temporal sovereignty was essential to papal independence, a doctrine that would only be abandoned (in practice) with the 1929 Lateran Treaty and (theologically) re-considered at Vatican II. It also exemplifies Pius IX's increasingly confrontational style, which shaped Catholic identity for generations.
