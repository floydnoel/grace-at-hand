## Summary

*Evangelium Vitae* ("The Gospel of Life") is an encyclical by Pope John Paul II, issued on 25 March 1995, affirming the Catholic Church's teaching on the sacredness and inviolability of human life. It confronts what the Pope calls a "culture of death" — including abortion, euthanasia, contraception, capital punishment, and other attacks on human dignity — and calls for the building of a "culture of life" rooted in the Gospel, natural law, and the inherent dignity of every human person from conception to natural death.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was written in the years following the fall of the Soviet Union and the rise of new bioethical challenges. John Paul II had convened an Extraordinary Consistory of Cardinals in April 1991 specifically to address threats to human life. He then wrote to every bishop in the world asking for input, making this one of the most collaborative encyclicals in history. It was published in the same period as *Veritatis Splendor* (1993) and *Centesimus Annus* (1991), forming part of a trilogy on the moral foundations of modern society. The 1994 International Year of the Family also directly influenced its themes. The document responds to the rapid expansion of abortion legislation, legalised euthanasia in some jurisdictions, and the growing influence of international bodies promoting population control and reproductive "rights."

## Key Themes

- **The sacredness and inviolability of human life** — life comes from God and belongs to God alone; direct killing of an innocent person is always gravely immoral
- **The "culture of death" vs. the "culture of life"** — a framework diagnosing modern societies' willingness to legalise abortion, euthanasia, and other practices that eliminate the weak for reasons of efficiency or convenience
- **Abortion as an "unspeakable crime"** — the encyclical declares with papal authority that direct abortion is always a grave moral disorder, and reaffirms the automatic excommunication for those who procure it
- **Euthanasia and assisted suicide** — clearly distinguished from the refusal of extraordinary or disproportionate treatment; euthanasia is condemned as a "false mercy" and a violation of God's sovereignty over life and death
- **The relationship between civil law and moral law** — unjust laws (those permitting abortion or euthanasia) lack binding force; citizens have a grave duty of conscientious objection
- **Freedom, truth, and conscience** — individualistic freedom detached from objective truth leads to the strong dominating the weak; authentic freedom is ordered toward the good and toward self-giving love
- **The family as the "sanctuary of life"** — the domestic church is called to proclaim, celebrate, and serve the Gospel of life
- **The role of women** — the encyclical calls for a "new feminism" that rejects male-dominated models and affirms women's unique genius, especially their capacity to receive and nurture life
- **The death penalty** — John Paul II teaches that cases requiring capital punishment are "very rare, if not practically non-existent" in modern society, laying groundwork for later development of this doctrine
- **A positive vision** — the encyclical is not merely prohibitive; it calls for prayer, fasting, education, healthcare, adoption, volunteer work, and political engagement to build a civilisation of love and life

## Significance

*Evangelium Vitae* is one of the most important papal documents on moral theology of the 20th century. It is the first encyclical devoted entirely to the defence of human life and remains the definitive Catholic statement on bioethics and respect for life. Cited extensively in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, it shaped the Church's engagement with political and legal questions around abortion and euthanasia for decades. Its teaching on conscience, civil disobedience, and the limits of democratic majoritarianism has influenced Catholic social thought profoundly. The encyclical's call for a "new feminism" and its nuanced treatment of the death penalty — which materially advanced Church teaching toward virtual abolitionism — are among its most lasting contributions.
