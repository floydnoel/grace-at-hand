## Summary

*Quemadmodum Preces* (1743) is an encyclical of Pope Benedict XIV asserting the Church's exclusive authority to prescribe public prayers, particularly those recited during Mass. The immediate occasion was a secular magistrate issuing decrees ordering public prayers for princes — decrees that a higher secular court later annulled on procedural grounds, but which the Pope considered invalid from the start because no civil authority may command liturgical prayer. Benedict instructs bishops to cooperate willingly with legitimate requests from civil rulers for prayers, but to resist firmly — following the example of St. Osio of Córdoba — any secular attempt to dictate the form, content, or occasion of public worship.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was issued on March 23, 1743, the third year of Benedict XIV's pontificate (1740–1758), an era when Church–state relations across Europe were increasingly contested by regalist and Gallican currents that sought to expand royal control over ecclesiastical affairs. The specific trigger was a local magistrate's decree mandating public prayers for the ruling prince — an act overturned by a superior secular court not on principle but on procedural grounds. The Pope saw this as a dangerous encroachment and used the occasion to reaffirm the Tridentine principle that only the Church may regulate the liturgy, referencing earlier cases where secular authorities had even attempted to order *Te Deum* thanksgivings after military victories.

## Key Themes

- **Exclusive ecclesiastical authority over liturgy** — Only the Church may prescribe the wording and occasions of public prayer, especially the Mass; no secular power may "decide and establish by law" what prayers are offered.
- **The axiom *lex orandi, lex credendi*** — Quoting St. Celestine, Benedict links approved prayer formulas to the integrity of the faith itself.
- **Continuity with patristic tradition** — The encyclical cites Paul (1 Tim 2:1–2), Dionysius of Alexandria, Tertullian, Cyprian, Origen, and Athenagoras to show that prayer for rulers was always practiced, but always governed by the Church.
- **The precedent of St. Osio of Córdoba** — Osio's rebuke to Emperor Constantius ("God entrusted the Empire to you; to us He entrusted all that pertains to the Church") becomes the template for episcopal resistance to civil overreach.
- **Pastoral pragmatism with firm red lines** — Bishops should gladly accommodate requests for prayers for princes, but must refuse any attempt to impose novel or unauthorized forms, and refer intractable cases to Rome.

## Significance

*Quemadmodum Preces* is a minor but sharp assertion of libertas Ecclesiae — the freedom of the Church from state control over worship — in the mid-18th century, decades before the revolutionary upheavals that would radically redefine Church–state relations across Europe. It anticipates the stronger affirmations of liturgical authority in *Quod Primum* (St. Pius V), *Quo Primum* (which Benedict XIV himself cited), and later the First Vatican Council's teaching on the Church's legislative power. The encyclical remains a reference point for the principle that no civil authority, however well-intentioned, may prescribe the Church's public prayer.
