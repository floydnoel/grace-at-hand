**La Santa Sede**

ENCYCLICAL QUEMADMODUM PRECES OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Venerable Brothers, Patriarchs, Primates, Archbishops, and Bishops. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. Just as it is most right to offer prayers to God on behalf of princes, so it is fitting that the wording of these prayers conform to that which the Church has adopted; especially if such prayers are to be recited during the celebration of Mass. Furthermore, it is the sole responsibility of ecclesiastical authority to establish and prescribe these prayers, since no secular power is permitted to decide and establish by law that public prayers be offered, whether to give thanks to God for some benefit received or to implore His help in a time of grave difficulty. 1. As you well know, Saint Paul, in his First \[Letter\] to Timothy, chapter 2, expresses himself as follows: “I urge, I implore, I call upon—above all—that public prayers, supplications, intercessions, and acts of generosity be offered on behalf of all people, on behalf of rulers and all those in positions of authority” (1 Tim 2:1–2). If it is permissible at this point to indicate the practice that the early Church followed in its prayers and supplications to commend rulers to God, this has been made quite clear by the letter of Saint Dionysius, Bishop of Alexandria, to Governor Emilianus; Tertullian in his work \*Ad Scapulam\* and in the \*Apologeticus\*; Saint Cyprian in his \*Epistle to Demetrian\*; Origen in his \*Reply to Celsus\*; and Athenagoras in his embassy to the emperors on behalf of the Christians. 2. Indeed, in many respects they agree on the manner of professing their faith and of praying. Similarly, in the famous additions to the letter of Saint Celestine to the Bishops of Gaul, chapter 11, we read: “Let us also turn our attention to the norms governing priestly prayers... so that the law of prayer may confirm the law of faith.” It follows from this that the formulas prescribed by the Church must be used in public prayers, especially when it comes to prayers that are to be recited during the Mass, as has been stated. Thus, the Sacred Council of Trent also cautioned against including in the celebration of Masses prayers “that are not those approved by the Church and that have not been accepted through assiduous and praiseworthy custom.” For this reason, the Roman Missal contains pious and devout prayers for almost every circumstance, appropriately drawn from the ancient and venerable sacred texts. 3. In truth, We do not believe We are going too far; on the contrary, We believe We are certainly remaining within the proper limits of Our Authority when We maintain that the power to regulate ecclesiastical and spiritual matters belongs solely to ecclesiastical authority and not to secular authority. That great Osio, Bishop of Córdoba, in a letter concerning those who led a solitary life at Saint Athanasius, wrote the following to Emperor Constantius regarding ecclesiastical freedom: “Do not meddle in ecclesiastical matters nor lay down laws for us in this matter, but rather learn from us. God entrusted the Empire to you; to us He entrusted all that pertains to the Church... Avoid making yourself guilty of a grave crime by claiming for yourself what belongs to the Church. It is written: ‘Render to Caesar what is Caesar’s; to God what is God’s.’” 4. Turning now to more recent times and to the event that gave rise to this letter of Ours, We affirm that the secular court itself had repealed and annulled the decrees of a certain magistrate by which public prayers for the Princes had been ordered: it had made it known that such decrees were devoid of all authority and legal force. Not many years ago, the Sacred Congregation of the Council, with the consent of Our Predecessors, publicly revoked and rendered inoperative an edict of secular authority by which the latter, following a victory won by the Prince, had ordered a Te Deum laudamus in thanksgiving, even though it had assured that it did not intend to violate ecclesiastical law, whereas, without a doubt, that very assurance was in fact contradicted. 5. Therefore, so that matters may be handled strictly with fairness and righteousness, We admonish and exhort you—both you personally and others through your intercession—to pray earnestly to God for the safety and happiness of your Princes, just as We do every day for the Catholic Princes. Accept with a serene and joyful spirit whatever the secular authorities ask of you regarding the recitation of public prayers for them, and ensure that the Church’s liturgy is used in these prayers and that no new or unusual prayers are recited during Mass. 6. If, on the other hand (though We find it hard to believe), some secular authority, by virtue of some custom or practice (which in reality must be defined as an abuse), presumes not to recognize Your authority at all, but through an arbitrary act claims the right to order public prayers and even dares to impose a penalty on those who protest, then You, too, will speak as Osio spoke to the Emperor. Use arguments that may be unknown to those who are in error. Explain to them that this is not the way to pray to God and to fulfill one’s vows; explain that they must take refuge in you, for although you were chosen from among men, you are nevertheless, for the benefit of all men, placed among those who belong to God, as the Apostle says to the Hebrews; explain that apart from you, no one can undertake such a work or assume this honor, but only one who is called by God, like Aaron. 7. If, however, your words are not believed, and you do not deem it appropriate to proceed against them in accordance with the dictates of ecclesiastical discipline, We strictly enjoin you to inform Us of these matters as soon as possible, including by submitting the relevant documentation to Us: We are, in fact, ready to carry out all those actions that Our illustrious Predecessors were accustomed to undertake in similar circumstances. For We do not wish, once called before the supreme tribunal of God, to be accused of having neglected the rights of the Holy See. In the meantime, We embrace you with paternal love and impart Our Apostolic Blessing to you. Given at Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on March 23, 1743, in the third year of Our Pontificate.
