## Summary

*Quum Diuturnum* (1898) is Pope Leo XIII's apostolic letter convening the first Plenary Council of the Latin American bishops in Rome. The Pope expresses his longstanding desire to strengthen the Church in Latin America, recounts that the bishops chose Rome over an American venue as a sign of their devotion to the Holy See, and announces that the Sacred Congregation will convoke the assembly the following year.

## Historical Context

Written at the close of the 19th century, the letter follows the 1892 fourth centenary of Columbus's voyage and reflects Leo XIII's strategic focus on unifying the Church in the rapidly developing New World. Latin American nations were consolidating after their independence wars, and the Pope saw a bishops' council as the best means to preserve uniform discipline, promote Catholic morals, and counter secularizing pressures across the culturally related but politically fragmented region.

## Key Themes

- **Episcopal unity and collegiality** — Leo XIII calls the bishops together for consultation under papal authority, emphasizing shared wisdom and coordinated discipline.
- **Affection for the Apostolic See** — The bishops' preference to meet in Rome is praised as a sign of their love for the Holy See, reinforcing the bond between the Latin American Church and the papacy.
- **Pastoral solicitude** — The Pope frames the council as a vehicle for fostering morals worthy of the Catholic profession and for advancing the Church's public prosperity.
- **Provision for Church discipline** — The primary aim is to preserve unity of discipline among peoples united by race and faith.

## Significance

*Quum Diuturnum* set the stage for the landmark 1899 Plenary Council of Latin America (Concilio Plenario de la América Latina), the first continent-wide synod for the region. It exemplified Leo XIII's worldwide vision for the Church and his strategy of using Rome-centered councils to strengthen ecclesiastical unity, while also acknowledging the distinct identity of the Latin American Church. The document remains a touchstone for understanding the modern institutional development of Catholicism in Latin America.
