## Summary

*In Hac Tanta* is a 1919 apostolic letter by Pope Benedict XV commemorating the twelve-hundredth anniversary of St. Boniface's missionary work in Germany. It praises Boniface as the model of a bishop in union with the Holy See, celebrates the ancient bond between Germany and the papacy, and calls for a renewal of Christian faith and unity amid the postwar chaos.

## Historical Context

Written in May 1919, just months after the end of World War I, the encyclical responds to a Europe devastated by war, revolution, and social upheaval — Benedict XV refers directly to "disorder and anarchy" in Germany and neighboring countries. The twelfth centenary of Boniface's mission provided an occasion to recall a founding moment of Christian civilization in Germany and to advocate for peace, religious unity, and a return to the Church as the foundation of social order.

## Key Themes

- **Union with Rome as the source of mission** — Boniface's success came from receiving his mandate from the Apostolic See; his constant correspondence with successive popes is held up as the ideal model.
- **Boniface as "representative of the Holy Roman Church"** — his identity as a Roman missionary is central; he called himself "the German representative of the Holy Roman Church."
- **Church unity as the remedy for division** — Benedict XV links the ancient unity of Germany with Rome to the need for reconciliation after the war, urging the return of the separated and the end of schisms.
- **Papal primacy** — the letter cites Cyprian, Ambrose, and Jerome in support of the papacy as the foundation of the Church's unity and warns that denying the pope's authority leads to schism.
- **Charity transcending national boundaries** — Boniface's love extended even to enemy peoples (the Franks), modeling the universal charity needed to overcome postwar hatreds.

## Significance

*In Hac Tanta* is a wartime appeal dressed as a commemorative letter. It reinforces the ultramontane vision of papal authority in the early twentieth century, using Boniface to argue that authentic evangelization requires submission to Rome. While primarily pastoral in tone, it is also a document of Church–state relations in a moment when Germany's political future was uncertain. It remains a significant reference for Catholic teaching on missionary obedience, episcopal communion with the Holy See, and the Church's role as a peacemaker after national catastrophe.
