## Summary

*In Suprema Catholicae* (1744) is a papal brief in which Pope Benedict XIV, surveying a Europe consumed by war, natural disaster, and plague, grants a plenary indulgence to the faithful of Italy and the adjacent islands. The document opens with a lament: Christian kingdoms are at war with one another, earthquakes have convulsed the land, floods have destroyed crops, a plague has decimated populations, and Rome itself is threatened by the sword. In response, the Pope opens "the treasuries of heavenly graces," offering a full remission of sins — modeled on the Jubilee indulgence — to those who participate in a solemn procession on the feast of St. Andrew, visit designated churches, fast, confess, receive Communion, and give alms. The indulgence runs from November 30 through the third Sunday of Advent, 1744.

## Historical Context

The brief was issued on November 20, 1744, the fifth year of Benedict XIV's pontificate, during the **War of the Austrian Succession** (1740–1748). Italy was a major theatre of that conflict, with Spanish, French, and Austrian armies clashing across the peninsula. The Pope describes "all of Europe in flames" and "countless men killed by thirst and hunger," cities emptied, streets covered with corpses. He also references a recent plague outbreak, earthquakes, and floods — a cascading series of calamities that lent the document its urgent, apocalyptic tone. Benedict draws explicitly on the language of St. Gregory the Great, proclaiming "the end of a now-aging world and the approach of the day of wrath and vengeance."

## Key Themes

- **Lament over Christian warfare** — The Pope is anguished that kingdoms and peoples "honored with the name of Christians" are warring against each other.
- **Calamity as divine judgment** — Earthquakes, plague, famine, and war are presented as visible signs of God's avenging justice, calling the faithful to repentance.
- **Indulgence as a spiritual remedy** — The central act is the granting of a plenary indulgence, opened for a limited two-week window, with conditions (procession, church visits, fasting, confession, Communion, almsgiving) that mirror a Jubilee Year.
- **Pastoral flexibility** — The brief makes provision for seafarers, travelers, prisoners, the sick, and the infirm, allowing confessors to substitute conditions with other pious works.
- **Papal authority to bind and loose** — Confessors are authorized to absolve from all censures (including those reserved to the Holy See), commute vows, and grant full remission — a robust exercise of the power of the keys.

## Significance

*In Suprema Catholicae* is a characteristic document of Benedict XIV's pastoral style — erudite, emotionally vivid, and administratively meticulous. It shows an 18th-century Pope responding to war and disaster not with political intervention but with a spiritual mobilization of the faithful, channelling traditional Catholic piety (indulgences, processions, confession) as a bulwark against crisis. The brief also demonstrates the mature form of the post-Tridentine indulgence system, with its careful regulation of conditions, confessor faculties, and exceptions. While not a landmark doctrinal document, it is a revealing window into how the papacy understood its role in an age of European dynastic war.
