ENCYCLICAL CUM ILLUD SEMPER OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Venerable Brothers, Patriarchs, Primates, Archbishops, and Bishops. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. The Catholic Church has always feared the danger that the care of souls and the guardianship of the Lord’s flock might be entrusted to unworthy persons lacking priestly merit; for the entire family structure falters if what is required of the body is not found in the head. Therefore, through canonical sanctions and especially through the decrees of the Sacred Council of Trent, appropriate measures have been taken to ensure that the governance of parish churches be entrusted to those whose lives, from childhood to maturity, has been spent in ecclesiastical service in such a way that there can be no doubt regarding their promotion over others, nor regarding the favorable judgment given concerning their doctrine, morals, and constant activity. Gradually, the prejudicial opinion has taken hold among many that the Decrees of the Council of Trent prescribe not that the most worthy be elected, but only that parish churches and other benefices involving the care of souls not be entrusted to unworthy persons. Innocent XI, of blessed memory, our Predecessor, condemned this opinion as erroneous and too far removed from the true and sincere intention of the Council Fathers, and taught that one must be prudent and diligent in assigning a pastoral office. 1. Therefore, in accordance with the directives of the Holy Synod, it has become customary that, when a parish church is vacant and the Ordinary is to freely assign it, a competitive selection process be held in which the age, character, doctrine, and abilities of each candidate are examined; and the Bishop then chooses the one he deems most suitable among them. 2. Since it can sometimes happen that, whether out of self-interest, favoritism, or a less than fair judgment, the less worthy are preferred over the more worthy, therefore, to ensure that there is nothing irregular or unclear in such an election, Pius V, of blessed memory, our Predecessor, by promulgating a most valid Constitution, decreed that those unjustly rejected in the competition might appeal to the Metropolitan or the neighboring Bishop or, in some cases, to the Apostolic See to have the chosen candidate re-examined and, if entitled to do so, to claim—through a new examination of merits—the Church not legally assigned to others. And so that there would be no pretext for a frivolous appeal, the Constitution appropriately stipulated that such an appeal may be made only “in a devolutive capacity,” without suspending or in any way delaying the Bishop’s appointee from taking possession of the church. 3. These most just laws, promulgated to prevent the ignorant from being preferred over the learned, newcomers over the long-established, and the inexperienced over the experienced in a matter of such great importance, have been violated by the fraud and malice of men: the remedy has become a weapon for the exasperated. Very often, in fact, those rejected by the Ordinary, under the pretext of the Constitution, were quick to resort to the option of appeal and, on even the flimsiest of grounds, to force a new examination of those chosen by the Bishop. They forced them—after they had abandoned the care of the flock and the Church—to undertake long journeys, to shoulder the burdens of endless toil, time, and money, and to defend their case in the second and third instances, and sometimes even beyond. 4. Indeed, experience has also shown that such disputes are ultimately resolved to the grave detriment of justice. In fact, those who had submitted to the examination and, unaware of the legitimate regulations, had been rejected in the first round, subsequently—as the dispute dragged on—devoted themselves purposefully and passionately to scholarship, thereby earning preference over others; they also became deeply indignant toward the Bishop—who, while a judge of acquired aptitude, was not a judge of aptitude to be acquired—for having been unjustly rejected. 5. This gave rise to endless complaints from respectable individuals and guardians of justice. To appease them, the Interpretive Congregation of the Council of Trent took every care and diligence and entrusted Us, who served as Secretary, the task of attempting, to the best of our ability, through a treatise—later published—to thoroughly examine this matter, the source of the present evil, and to seek appropriate remedies to eliminate it. In setting forth our views on the matter, we pointed out that, above all, the practice of conducting the examination orally rather than in writing was flawed, precisely because those chosen for the care of souls by the Ordinary Collator, when summoned to a second examination before another judge, could not defend their right to legitimate collation with the certain proof they already possessed of their proven suitability. The entire matter seemed to hinge on the outcome of a new examination to be conducted before an appellate judge who was completely unaware of the facts. Consequently, to the grave detriment of justice, the \[ecclesiastical\] court had accepted the view that one could appeal to another judge without presenting any document evidencing the undeserved rejection. Such a method was too far removed from the spirit of the sacred Canons; therefore, we thought that this malpractice could be remedied first and foremost by establishing a reliable and appropriate form of examination, then by putting in writing the questions posed to the examinees and their respective answers, as well as the entire sequence of events, and finally by submitting the complete records of the entire examination to the appellate judge. 6. The rule We proposed met not only with the approval of the Congregation, which adopted it at its session on November 16, 1720, but also with the favorable judgment of Pope Clement XI, an eminent guardian and upholder of ecclesiastical discipline. And so that the Ordinaries might implement these provisions with the diligence and filial deference they deserve, they were duly set forth in our own hand in the form of a Letter on January 10, 1721, and approved with the consent and response of the Pope himself. Although their content has already been published and included in the Bullarium of the aforementioned Clement, our Predecessor, we have deemed it appropriate to reproduce it here. 7. Most Reverend Lord and Brother. In order that the governance of parish churches might be entrusted to more worthy persons, the Holy Council of Trent, in Session 24, chapter 18—established, as is well known, that a competition be announced and held, and that from among the candidates, once they had been examined and approved by the Bishop or his Vicar General and by at least three examiners, the Bishop should elect the one whom, in terms of age, character, doctrine, prudence, and other qualities necessary and appropriate for the governance of the vacant parish, be deemed more worthy and more suitable than the others. Pius V, a most holy Pontiff in name and in fact, added that, in the event that the Bishop had elected a less suitable candidate while passing over others who were more suitable, the latter could appeal against such a prejudicial election to the Metropolitan or, if the elector were the Metropolitan himself or an exempt bishop, to the nearest Ordinary as Delegate of the Apostolic See, or otherwise to the Apostolic See itself, and summon the chosen candidate to a new examination before the same appellate judge and his examiners, with the proviso, however, that the appeal must not be on a suspensive basis, but with devolutive effect, as explained more fully in its thirty-third Constitution. And it concluded that, once the elector’s unreasonable judgment had been established and revoked, the parish church should be entrusted to the most suitable candidate. When, however, neither a decree of the Council nor a papal bull succeeded in establishing a specific system or method for conducting the examination, it is difficult to say how many different forms of examination there would be—and even more in other regions—and this gave rise to complaints. In fact, in some places, since the same questions and cases were not posed to all candidates, there were always those who, whether in person or from afar, complained, claiming that they—having been rejected—had been given the most difficult questions, while the chosen candidate had received the easiest ones. Elsewhere, however, while the same questions were indeed posed, neither the questions nor the candidates’ answers were submitted in writing or by letter. Subsequently, it often happened that one of the rejected candidates, invoking the aforementioned Bull, would summon the successful candidate to a new examination before the appeals judge and his examiners. The Sacred Congregation, considering that the challenge could not be proven except through a new examination, established as early as 1603 that the summons for a new examination must be accepted, even if the challenge had not yet been proven, because evidence is required only in the subsequent judgment. In that trial, once the challenge regarding doctrine has been proven through the appellant’s new examination, it remains to be proven that he is more capable than the previously chosen candidate with regard to the other qualities required to govern the parish church, so as to be able to judge which of the two is more suitable to govern the parish Church. For in fact, the most learned is not always considered—nor must he be considered—the most suitable or the most fit for governance. The authors of the Sacred Congregation and the Tribunals praised this ruling. Only then did the practice become established in other dioceses whereby the same questions and cases were presented to all candidates (so as not to give the Chancellor an opportunity to add, remove, or alter anything on his own initiative) and whereby the candidates themselves wrote the questions and answers in their own hand. Furthermore, the Ordinaries who considered this method of examination to be the best often left it to the Congregation to determine whether—without first requesting evidence of the challenge—it was appropriate to grant the summons of the elected candidate for a new examination to those who, having been examined in this manner and then rejected, as they subsequently tended to appeal: for these candidates could easily substantiate their challenge regarding doctrine based on the records of the first examination; something that others, examined by a different method, naturally could not do except through a second or new examination. Nor were there any shortage of men distinguished for their integrity and experience in a long and commendable administration of the Churches, who pointed out that it was time to curb the license of the appellants and to moderate the already all-too-frequent summonses to a new examination, which are almost never carried out without serious harm to the Churches. In fact, when a new examination must be conducted before an appellate judge located far from the parish, the candidate chosen by the bishop, having been summoned, is forced for the entire duration to abandon the parish and leave it to a steward or a vicar—like a bride entrusted to strangers—while he, the groom, would be away not just momentarily, but for a very long time; for the dispute is usually complex, and the examinations—first concerning the excellence of doctrine, then regarding the other qualities necessary for suitability, are meticulously piled one upon another—three or four at a time—and drag on at a leisurely pace, not to say idly, before deciding to which of the candidates the parish should be awarded. To eliminate the cause of both complaints and inconvenience, the Sacred Congregation Interpreting the Council of Trent, after reviewing the entire matter from the beginning in two sessions—on October 1 and November 16, 1720—has strongly reaffirmed (as is hereby enacted through this Encyclical Letter) that all and every Bishop and other Prelates who have the right and authority to hold competitions are urged not to refuse to institute such an examination, as is already the practice in many dioceses and in Rome itself; this is also required by the Apostolic Dataria when, upon the vacancy of the See, a parish church is also vacant—the assignment of which falls to the Apostolic See—or when a parish is vacant “iuxta Decretum,” as it is said, or when, finally, a major office in a collegiate or cathedral church—to which the care of souls is attached—is vacant, a competitive examination must be held; as is well known, this must be referred to the Apostolic Dataria, as is clearly prescribed in the letters that, by order of the Most Holy One, are issued precisely by the Dataria. Therefore, when a parish church becomes vacant and must be assigned through a competition held according to the usual procedures, by decree of the Sacred Congregation, the following shall be strictly observed: First: All candidates shall be given the same questions and cases, as well as the same Gospel passage on which to compose a short sermon, to demonstrate their ability to speak before an assembly. Second: The cases and questions to be answered shall be dictated to all at the same time, and the Gospel text shall be distributed to all at the same time. Third: The same time limit shall be established for all to resolve the cases, answer the questions, and compose the short sermon. Fourth: all contestants are to be locked in the same room, from which—while they are writing (for all will be given the opportunity to write)—none of them may leave, nor may anyone else enter, until they have completed and submitted their work. Fifth: Everyone must write and sign their answers and essay in their own handwriting. Sixth: The answers must be written in Latin; the essay in the language commonly used among the people. Seventh: every answer and every speech, when presented by one of the contestants, must be signed not only by the author and the competition’s Registrar, but also by the examiners and the Ordinary or his Vicar who will be present at the competition. Once the competition has been completed according to this procedure and the parish church has been assigned to the candidate deemed most suitable and worthy, no appeal shall be admitted—whether against an inaccurate report by the examiners or against an unreasonable judgment by the Bishop—unless it is filed within ten days of the date of the assignment. If anyone then files an appeal within this time frame and requests that the records of the competition be sent to the appellate judge, either the original records of the competition—sealed and locked—or at least a certified copy prepared by the Registrar of the competition and a second notary, and certified in the presence of the Vicar or another person holding ecclesiastical office, appointed by the Ordinary, who shall also be responsible for appointing the notary assisting the Registrar. The copy shall be signed by the synodal examiners who were present at the examination. Anyone who, having been examined in accordance with the foregoing, after having lodged an appeal against the examiners’ inaccurate report or against an unjustified judgment by the Bishop, fails to prove from these documents or their certified copy anything other than the challenge regarding doctrine, will have sought in vain from the Sacred Congregation the authority to proceed with a new examination. Likewise, anyone who complains of being challenged regarding other qualifications will have sought in vain to assert their right in the appeal proceedings, unless, after having filed in a timely manner, as stated, an appeal against the Bishop’s unreasonable judgment in a timely manner, as has been stated, fails to demonstrate—based on the records of the first examination or at least through extrajudicial testimony and documents, provided they are not frivolous—the challenge regarding the other qualifications. Thus did the Sacred Congregation rule, and the Most Holy One approved. But if any Ordinary, despite everything, continues to conduct the examinations differently from what has been stated above, the Sacred Congregation will also continue, in accordance with previous custom, to grant appellants who claim to be challenged the right to a new examination without first having to prove their challenge. Meanwhile, so that the memory of this Letter may not fade, the Sacred Congregation wishes that it be permanently preserved in the Chancery of each Ordinary. As I notify all of the Sacred Congregation’s firm resolve, I invoke upon Your Excellency every blessing from heaven. Rome, today, January 10, 1721. Of Your Excellency As a Brother P.M. Cardinal Corradino, Prefect P. Lambertini, Secretary 8. We have experienced more than sufficiently how beneficial the salutary institution of the aforementioned laws has been in the proper assignment of ecclesiastical offices, in the administration of justice, in the settlement of disputes, and in keeping the clergy in their ministry, we have experienced more than sufficiently when we personally embraced with paternal love our Bride, first the Church of Ancona, and then that of Bologna. Precisely because we were strengthened by these laws, we have appointed the most worthy to lead the parishes and care for souls. And this took place, with the Lord’s blessing, with such widespread approval among the faithful that no one has ever complained that the reward of a higher office was given to a less worthy person or that the governance of a Church was unjustly entrusted to others. 9. But since we know from certain indications that this has not been the case with other bishops—indeed, there is no shortage of those who, led astray by personal interests, often presume to circumvent and refute episcopal judgment— We, anxious to fulfill Our office as We ought, deemed it necessary to add certain points to the aforementioned Letter and to explain more clearly others that are stated there only between the lines and in concise form, so that everything may proceed rightly and in an orderly manner. 10. We have therefore learned with regret that in many dioceses, although the commendable custom—which must be firmly upheld—of putting the evaluation of candidates in writing has been adopted, the examiners’ evaluations focus solely on literary ability, and no consideration is given to their opinions regarding the candidates’ age, formation, seriousness, moral character, and prudence, nor to the offices they have previously held, nor, finally, to whether they are capable of guiding their flock by word and example. Just how far this practice strays from the path indicated by the Council of Trent will become clear to anyone who reflects on the following words: “Once the examination is complete, the names of those whom they have judged to be suitable in terms of moral character, doctrine, prudence, and other qualities appropriate for governing the vacant Church shall be notified” (Council of Trent, sess. 18, chap. 24, De Reformatione). Being well aware of this, the Sacred Congregation, as the interpreter of that same Council, has declared on several occasions that examiners fail in their duty if they judge only on the basis of doctrine and do not investigate which candidates are suitable and recommendable for their good character, diligence, reverence toward the Church, and other qualities necessary, taken as a whole, to exercise the office of parish priest. 11. Once the examination is complete, as is well known to all, the examiners have only the authority to notify those whom they have judged suitable to govern the Church, for the election of the most worthy is reserved solely to the Bishop, as was decreed by the Council of Trent in these words: “Of these, the Bishop shall elect the one whom he deems most suitable among the others.” In the event that any of the clerics appeals against an inaccurate report by the examiners—whose sole concern was to verify doctrine, without at the same time conducting a thorough investigation into the other qualities necessary for the office of pastor— the order of the judgment will consequently require that even the judge to whom the appeal is made limit himself to an examination of doctrine alone; that, not without grave harm to souls and an offense against justice, one who is learned in the letters be placed in charge of the Church, even if in other respects he is less suitable and, at times, unworthy; and that, conversely, a candidate who, though lacking in doctrine, nevertheless excels in moral character, dignity, prudence, good reputation, constant service to the Church, and high esteem for virtue, be rejected. 12. Furthermore, it does not appear that much progress has been made in rooting out abuses, even when both the bishop and the examiners, with equal diligence, have made every effort to unanimously assign the Church to a person who, although inferior to others in learning and scholarship, nevertheless stands out for the combination of all his other qualities. In fact, the rejected candidate, placing too much confidence in his own learning, not infrequently first appeals against the Bishop’s unreasonable judgment; then, after referring the case to the appellate judge, devotes himself entirely to acquiring even more learning and to refuting the challenge with scholarly arguments, without ever giving any weight to the other qualities desired in an appellant. The vigilant Shepherds of the Churches deeply deplore the outcome of such an appeal and grieve in their hearts that the governance of parishes is entrusted, as has been said, to learned pastors, not to suitable pastors. 13. But even if the appellate judge were to attach (which rarely happens) sufficient importance to scholarship, and were to investigate—through a more thorough and careful examination—the character, seriousness, and prudence of the individuals, their examples of virtue, and finally their entire past lives to determine whether they are fit to shepherd the flock, the appellant presents him with so many attestations gathered for this very purpose that the judge, overturning the bishop’s judgment as unreasonable, does not hesitate to side with the appellant, supported by so many and such notable attestations of integrity. 14. Finally, it often happens—especially to bishops, who, being in high positions, are aware of their subjects’ transgressions—that they see examiners in a competitive selection process declare someone suitable in terms of knowledge and character, even though that person is deeply tainted by vice or crime, a fact unknown to everyone except the bishop. If the bishop, for just cause and without revealing the crime, selects another candidate of good character, silently passing over the guilty party, the latter immediately lodges a feigned challenge and appeals to a higher judge who is unaware of the crime; thus, through the usual expedient of appeal, one is elevated to pastoral office who is capable not of caring for the people, but of harming them; not of ensuring good governance, but of worsening it. 15. Lest, therefore, men of evil intent transform the remedy of appeal—established to defend justice—into a means of defending iniquity, it might seem to some an excellent idea to abolish all appeals and leave the task of appointing the shepherds of souls solely to the bishops, who would be accountable for their actions only to Christ the Judge. In truth, we cannot in any way approve of this thesis (which would be contrary to the spirit of the Council of Trent), which tacitly permits an “appeal in devolutive” against the inaccurate report of the examiners, as those words seem to indicate: “No devolution and no appeal, even if lodged with the Apostolic See, or with the Legates of that See, Vice-Legates, or Nuncios, or with Bishops, Metropolitans, Primates, or Patriarchs, may ever suspend or prevent the report of the aforementioned examiners from following its course.” The Piana Constitution also concurs with this decision, admitting an “appeal in devolutivo” against the Bishop’s unreasonable judgment. 16. Therefore, so that everything in this matter may proceed rightly and orderly, we have deemed it proper for Our Office, Venerable Brothers, to prescribe those norms which, through long experience, we have recognized as valid for the formation of the shepherds of souls, so that they may preside over and benefit the flock entrusted to them. I. Upon learning of a vacant parish, the Bishop shall immediately appoint a suitable Vicar in accordance with the provisions of the Council of Trent, with an appropriate allocation, at his discretion, of a portion of the parish’s revenues for its sustenance until a pastor is appointed. II. The news of the competition shall be made public through a public notice, and the competition must be held within a reasonable period of time, as determined by the Bishop. The notice shall clearly inform all parties to submit, within the established deadline, to the Episcopal Chancellor or to another person designated by the Bishop, evidence, judicial and extrajudicial attestations, and other similar documents free from fraud. Otherwise, once the established deadline has passed, such documents and others of this kind will not be accepted under any circumstances. III. When the day of the competition arrives, the merits, qualities, and qualifications (as they are called) of the individual candidates—drawn with absolute fidelity from the certificates submitted in a timely manner—shall be summarized in writing by the Chancellor. Then a copy of the summary shall be delivered not only to the Bishop or to the Vicar General acting in his stead, but individually to all the examiners invited to the competition, so that they may give their judgment both on the candidates’ knowledge and on their life, conduct, and other qualities necessary for governing the Church. IV. The examination shall be held on the day set by the Bishop, strictly observing in every respect the procedure described in the aforementioned Letter published in 1721; and the entire series of proceedings of the examination shall be recorded in detail, diligently, and in writing. Then, in order to arrive at a sufficiently certain and clear judgment, the examiners shall diligently assess the candidates’ ability to present and explain orally certain points of ecclesiastical doctrine—whether drawn from the Holy Fathers, the Sacred Council of Trent, or the Roman Catechism; and with the same diligence, they should examine the written responses to the proposed questions; they should assess each candidate’s ability to demonstrate sound judgment and correct expression in the short sermon submitted in writing, as well as how well it has been adapted to the Gospel text and the other assigned topic. The examiners should exercise equal, if not greater, discernment in assessing the other qualities necessary for the care of souls; they should investigate the integrity of character, seriousness, prudence, the devotion shown to the Church up to that point, the merit acquired in other positions, and the array of many other virtues that are closely linked to doctrine. After examining everything collectively, they shall reject the unsuitable candidates by their vote and notify the Bishop of those deemed suitable. V. Once the selection process is complete, the Bishop—or, if he is unable to do so, the Vicar General—together with the synodal examiners, numbering no fewer than three, shall hand over the form, distributed in advance and summarizing the requirements, to the Chancellor so that he may burn it or keep it in a secret place together with the records, and not show it to anyone without a mandate from the Bishop or his Vicar General. Immediately thereafter, the Ordinary, as soon as he deems it appropriate, shall elect the most worthy candidate from among those approved, and his installation \[in the Church\] shall not be delayed by any pretext of appeal or prohibition. VI. If any of the clerics should appeal against the examiners’ inaccurate report or against the Bishop’s unreasonable judgment, he shall produce the complete records of the selection process before the appellate judge. And the judge shall not render a judgment until after having reviewed them and assessed the challenge. Furthermore, in rendering the judgment and resolving the challenge, the judge shall base the decision solely on the evidence contained in the records, both regarding doctrine and other qualifications. Since there is sufficient time between the public announcement \[of the competition\] and the day of the competition to comfortably submit the necessary certificates, references, qualifications, and other relevant documents, therefore, to preclude any possibility of fraud, we decree and strictly order that said certificates, references, judicial and extrajudicial attestations, and all documents artfully obtained—and, as they say, “fished out”—after the competition, shall in no way be taken into consideration. This is notwithstanding the aforementioned Letter, published by the Sacred Congregation Interpreting the Council of Trent in 1721, from which we derogate in this regard for the purposes of what has been stated above, and which, in all other respects and in its entirety, shall remain firmly in force. VII. If, however, the Bishop, instead of assigning the church to one or another of the approved candidates, assigns it to a more suitable candidate for a reason known only to him—and which he intends to notify to the appellate judge to dispel the accusation of an unjust preference—he shall inform the judge of this in a private letter, while observing the law of inviolable secrecy. No one should attribute this practice to our own caution: it derives from the Decrees of the Council of Trent. In fact, in Session 24, Chapter 20, “De Reformatione,” it is established as follows: “Furthermore, if anyone, in the cases provided for by law, appeals, or objects to some challenge, or otherwise due to the expiration of the two-year period mentioned above, and turns to another judge, he is required to transfer to the appellate judge, at his own expense, all the proceedings that took place before the Bishop, provided, however, that he first informs the Bishop that if anything seems useful to him for the investigation of the case, he may notify the appellate judge” (Council of Trent, Session 24, Chapter 20). And although We must rightly fear that this practice—once in use—of notifying the judge to whom an appeal has been made has today fallen into disuse and disappeared from the \[ecclesiastical\] courts, nevertheless, if the Bishop (as has been said) for a reason known only to him and not to others—but one that deserves to be accepted—has assigned the Church, he shall report and make known the reason to the judge of appeal by means of a letter delivered in secret. Let the judges also know that the cases and reasons referred by the Bishop must be kept under the guarantee of inviolable secrecy; nor should the testimony of that Shepherd be taken lightly, for he is commanded by the divine word to distinguish his own sheep. Indeed, it is hard to believe that bishops would be so indifferent to their own and others’ salvation as to allow themselves to be influenced by animosity or favoritism, completely unafraid of the threat of divine judgment, and to call—in defiance of the sacred Canons— “evil for good, good for evil, darkness for light, and light for darkness.” If, moreover, the bishop harbors suspicions regarding the integrity of the judge to whom the appeal has been made, and believes he should not reveal the confidential points of the case to him, he shall notify them by means of a secret letter to the Cardinal of the Sacred Roman Congregation, the pro tempore Prefect of the Congregation of the Council, who will lack neither wisdom nor authority to induce the judge to give justice its due place. 17. Since it is also in the interest of equity to bring appeal cases to a conclusion as quickly as possible—cases that are sometimes interminable, to the great detriment and harm of the Church— therefore, when the appellate judge has rendered a judgment fully in accordance with the Bishop’s pre-election, no further opportunity for a new appeal shall be granted, but authority shall be exercised to put an end to the dispute. If, on the other hand, the appellate judge rules differently from the Ordinary, the candidate pre-selected by the Bishop who lost the case shall be permitted to appeal to another judge, while retaining possession of the parish church in the meantime. Finally, after the third judge has also rendered a judgment, so that the parties are not burdened beyond the limit of effort and expense—especially since this concerns the care of souls, for which it is harmful not to have the comfort of a permanent pastor— the one who has two rulings in his favor shall have the legitimate right to govern the church, and the defeated party shall be left with no possibility of a further appeal. 18. Truly, with these regulations—even though the right of appeal has not been abolished—we believe that ecclesiastical discipline has been sufficiently provided for. Only one thing remains: namely, that the measures proposed thus far be duly implemented, and that to this end the local Ordinaries exercise their vigilance without fail. It would be unacceptable for new complaints to be brought before our Apostolic Office every day and for those who neglect and disregard the existing laws to call for new ones to put an end to abuses. 19. Finally, since it is not uncommon for the Apostolic See to confer parish churches, dignities, canonries, and other benefices to which the care of souls is attached—either because they have remained vacant during the reserved months or because they have been reserved for the said See for some other reason— We, following in the footsteps of Our Predecessors, prescribe and order that in either case the competition be announced by the Bishop without any distinction, and without the need for any permission or license, and the Bishops are to know that this authority has been granted to them by this Letter of Ours. 20. Once the competition has concluded, if the benefices in question are “reserved solely for the duration of the months,” the Bishop shall select the most suitable candidate from among those approved and notify the Dataria without submitting the documents, unless the Dataria deems it appropriate to request them. If, on the other hand, the said benefices to which the care of souls is attached are reserved to the Holy See for a reason other than that of “apostolic months,” in this case, without changing the old practice, the Bishop shall refrain from determining who is the most worthy and shall spontaneously submit the documents of the competition to the Dataria. 21. It shall, however, be permissible for the Ordinaries, at their discretion, to notify the Datario in letters of the person they deem most suitable to govern the Church, and to inform him if there is any hidden reason—rightly omitted from the documents—that would prevent someone from obtaining a parish benefice. We ourselves, from this See, Guide and Teacher of all, will teach by a shining example how the episcopal judgment is to be esteemed and how to honor you, Venerable Brothers, who are called to share in Our concern; to you, in the meantime, We impart with great affection the Apostolic Blessing. Given in Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on December 14, 1742, the third year of Our Pontificate.
