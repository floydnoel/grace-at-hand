ENCYCLICAL QUAM GRAVE OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Patriarchs, Archbishops, and Ordinary Bishops of the various localities. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brother, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. 1. We deem it unnecessary to explain at length what a grave and heinous crime is committed by anyone who, not having been ordained to the priesthood, presumes to celebrate the sacrifice of the Mass, since the reasons why such a sacrilegious crime is rightly considered detestable and punishable by the strict application of penalties are evident to all. It will suffice here to refer to the Apostolic Constitutions of our Predecessors, which establish the most severe penalties against those guilty of the aforementioned crime; namely, those issued by the Roman Pontiffs of blessed memory, Paul IV, Sixtus V, Clement VIII, and Urban VIII; according to which it is established that anyone found to be celebrating Mass without having the priestly character must be handed over to the secular authorities for just punishment. 2. We ourselves have recalled and confirmed all these Constitutions in our Constitution, which we published in the year 1744 of the Incarnation of the Lord, the fourth year of our Pontificate, on April 20, beginning with the words: “Priest Forever,” and which was printed in Our Bullarium, Vol. I, No. 97; where, moreover, many exceptions and loopholes are abolished that the defense used to cite to spare the guilty from the punishment they deserved—namely, being handed over to the secular authorities— whenever irrefutable evidence demonstrated that the individuals in question, without ever having received priestly ordination, had celebrated Mass. 3. Although the Church has established that those who celebrate Mass without having been admitted, for the sake of justice, must be handed over to the secular courts, nevertheless, being a merciful Mother and, as everyone knows, full of goodness and mercy, she has never failed—nor does she fail even now—to demonstrate her clemency, both in determining the form of verbal defrocking, which usually precedes actual defrocking, and in carrying out the actual defrocking, and also through the use of admonitions and interventions to dissuade even the most depraved from the sacrilegious attempt to intrude upon the celebration of the Sacred Mysteries without priestly ordination and character; thus, in a sense, blocking the path that would have led them to the aforementioned punishment—that of being handed over to secular authorities. 4. The Roman Pontifical contains the formula for verbal degradation, through which the sentence entailing actual degradation is pronounced, not only against those who have been conferred the Sacred Orders—excluding the Priesthood—but also against all others who have received only the Clerical Tonsure or the Minor Orders. The only difference between the two is this: with regard to clerics who have received Minor Orders or only the Tonsure, both verbal and actual defrocking are decreed by the bishop alone, without the involvement of others; as for the others, who have been conferred the Major Orders preceding the Priesthood, the Bishop cannot proceed with verbal demotion except with the assistance and support of certain other persons, whose presence and opinion are required by Canon Law. 5. Well known is the Decretal of our Predecessor, Pope Boniface VIII, which states: “Regarding this matter, here is our response: verbal degradation or deposition from Orders or Ecclesiastical Ranks must be carried out by one’s own Bishop with the assistance of a certain number of Bishops established by the Canons, if it concerns Clerics who have been ordained to the sacred Orders; if, however, it concerns the demotion of those who have received only Minor Orders, the opinion of their own bishop is sufficient, without the presence of other bishops” (chap. Demotion of Penalties, section 6). 6. Also noteworthy are the penalties prescribed by the Canons, as indicated by the Canonical Gloss (in the cited chapter Degradation, section on canonical penalties, paragraph 6), which define the number of bishops whose assistance is required when a verbal degradation is to be pronounced against a cleric who has been ordained to the major orders. 7. Nor can one ignore what was established by the Council of Trent (Session XIII, Chapter 4, On the Reform): having noted that it would not be easy to gather the number of bishops required by the Canons every time a degradation must be carried out, the bishop may proceed even without them, provided, however, in such a situation, an equal number of abbots residing in the city or diocese and invested, by indulgence of the Apostolic See, with the miter and crosier; or, if such abbots are not available, other persons distinguished by ecclesiastical dignity, of mature age, and deeply versed in sacred law. 8. Since, according to ancient discipline, verbal degradations were carried out in Provincial Synods and in them the individual bishops cast their votes, bearing in mind that currently the bishops—or the other ecclesiastics mentioned above— take the place of the Fathers who participated in the Provincial Councils, it clearly follows that the right to vote in verbal degradations to which they are invited as assessors is transferred to them; as we have clearly demonstrated in our treatise “On the Diocesan Synod” (Book 9, Chapter 6, No. 4), recently published. 9. Verbal defrocking is followed by actual defrocking; the Roman Pontifical clearly sets forth the rules for carrying it out. And even in this, the Church does not fail to offer clear evidence of her goodness and understanding. In fact, before the defrocked person is handed over to the Minister of the Secular Court, an urgent plea is made on his behalf that neither the death penalty nor the mutilation of limbs be imposed upon him: “Your Honor—these are the words of the aforementioned Pontifical—with all possible affection we implore you, for the love of God, in the name of piety and mercy, and also in light of these prayers we address to you, not to expose this wretched man to the danger of death and mutilation.” And long before the compilation of the Roman Pontifical, the same thing is expressly stated in the chapter “We Know” of the work \*On the Meaning of Words\*, where it is said: “For him, however—referring to the defrocked priest who must be handed over to the secular court—the Church must effectively intercede with the secular judge so that, with the death penalty excluded, the sentence against him may be lenient.” See also Alteserra in the aforementioned chapter “We Know” of the work \*On the Meaning of Words\*, in which he gathers many testimonies from the Fathers that are perfectly in line with what is established in the aforementioned Decretal. 10. To confirm our assertion regarding the Church’s immense concern even for those who do not deserve it—and her efforts to prevent them from incurring the death penalty as a consequence of some crime they have committed—we affirm that, with a foresight dating back to ancient times, it has been established: anyone who transfers from his own diocese to another, if he is not well known in the latter, must carry with him a letter from his Ordinary attesting that he is a priest and, as far as is known, is not subject to any canonical impediment that would prohibit him from celebrating the Sacred Mysteries. This had already been decreed by the Holy Council of Chalcedon; in which, where it is stated that it is not lawful for a cleric or lector to transfer from his own diocese to another without first obtaining from his own bishop the letter mentioned above—which we shall call a “letter of leave”— Giacomo Cuiazio and, after him, other careful writers have noted that the word “Lector” does not fit the context and therefore must be read as “unknown”; a point we ourselves have noted in our Instruction 34 (Latin edition, par. 1). Furthermore, if one wishes to examine even the most ancient principles of this discipline, one may consult the so-called Apostolic Canons, as found in Cotelerio’s edition of the Apostolic Fathers (Volume I, Book 8); among these is Canon XIII, which states: “If a cleric or a layperson, whether suspended from communion or even in communion, travels to another city and is received without a letter of commendation, both those who received him and the one who was received shall be excommunicated; and the excommunicated person shall receive the same punishment as would be meted out to one who has lied and deceived the Church of God.” Canon XXIV concurs with this, as evidenced by the following words contained therein: “No traveling bishop, presbyter, or deacon shall be received without letters of commendation; and when such letters are presented, their contents shall be carefully examined; and they shall be received if they are found to be of proven piety; otherwise, do not even provide them with the necessities, and under no circumstances admit them to communion: many problems can arise from surreptitious behavior.” 11. Although there is debate among scholars regarding the true author of the Apostolic Canons, their authority is nonetheless great, having been compiled at least by the third century of the Church from the various councils held prior to the Council of Nicaea; a point on which researchers of ancient sacred texts now seem to agree. 12. The sanctions of the Council of Trent are in accordance with these most holy laws, just as are the encyclicals frequently sent by the Roman Congregations to the bishops of the various Churches. And this, too, must be held as certain: although the Bishop need not concern himself with religious who intend to celebrate Mass in their own churches—since responsibility for this lies with their regular superiors—nevertheless, if a secular priest wishes to celebrate Mass in a church belonging to religious, even in this case he is required to present the letter of permission obtained from his own Ordinary to the Bishop of the diocese in which he wishes to perform the sacred rite; as can also be seen in our Instruction 34 (Latin edition, par. 1). 13. This ancient and most just sanction—the implementation of which has been detailed for us by our writers expert in practical law (among whom the most diligent Monacellio deserves special mention (Legal Formulas, Part I, Title 4, Formula 8, p. 81 and Formula 6, p. 79; likewise Tit. 6, Formula 18, p. 160), was made even more effective, through far-sighted and prudent regulations, by that great reformer of ecclesiastical discipline, St. Charles Borromeo; We urge you to read and study carefully the decrees he issued on this matter, which are found in many documents: in the First Provincial Council of Milan, held in 1565, Part 2; then in the Second Provincial Council of 1569, Decree 1, and in the Third Provincial Council of 1573; then in the Instruction to Priests Concerning the Celebration of Mass; and finally in the Fourth Provincial Council held in 1576. These passages stipulate that the parish priest in whose parish a foreign priest takes up residence—if this occurs in the rural area—must report his arrival to the rural vicar within eight days at the latest, who in turn will inform the bishop; if, on the other hand, this occurs within the city, the parish priest himself must inform the bishop directly, who will have the priest in question submit his letter of leave, examine it, and carefully evaluate its content. In fact, if the Ordinary has granted a priest under his jurisdiction permission to be absent for a clearly defined period of time, it is prescribed that the authority to celebrate Mass be granted to him only for that period. It is also required to note the date on which the letter of leave was delivered: no more than two, four, or six months, respectively, may have elapsed from the date it was presented for reading and examination; two months, if it was written within the province; four months if it was written outside the Province but within Italy; six months if it is dated outside Italy. 14. From all these general and specific rules, it is clear how reluctant the Church is to apply the penalty established by the Sacred Canons against those who celebrate Mass without having been ordained to the priesthood; it erects, as it were, barriers so that no one may fall into such a crime that would give rise to the penalty prescribed by the Sacred Laws. 15. However, what we are about to say serves as even stronger evidence of this. A person presents himself to the ecclesiastical judge and accuses himself of having celebrated the sacrifice of the Mass or even of having heard the sacramental confession of the faithful, despite not having received priestly ordination. By presenting himself voluntarily, he enjoys a privilege by virtue of which he is assigned salutary penances and is then released, provided he is not, as they say, “in ipso suo Constituto diminutus” or has a criminal record; in such cases, in fact, he must be detained and held in prison. But if, in response to the judge’s question as to whether he knows the reason for his imprisonment, the same person immediately—without waiting for further questions or interrogation—confesses everything openly, even though there is no doubt that he has incurred the criminal penalties provided for by the Apostolic Constitutions and that, therefore, as a perpetrator of a capital offense, deserves to be freely handed over to the secular courts, he may nevertheless be granted the benefit of a reduced sentence, commuting the death penalty to perpetual servitude on the triremes; as attested by Cardinal Albizio, of blessed memory, a foremost expert in this matter: “If the person who is summoned to court and called before the judge, when asked in general terms whether he knows the reason for which he has been summoned, or whether he knows the cause of his imprisonment, or why he is there, confesses the whole truth, he is treated as if he had voluntarily confessed his guilt, and he is dealt with leniently, especially after sentencing—that is, the most severe penalty is commuted to a milder one, etc. And so it was decided by the Sacred Congregation on May 12, 1604” (Treatise On Inconstancy in the Faith, Part I, Chapter 14, No. 70). 16. Another noteworthy fact: although the Roman Pontifical strictly prescribes defrocking, even for those who have received only the first clerical tonsure—though it is then debated whether or not this should be counted among the Orders, and there is debate as to whether the ecclesiastical judge may, as we have noted, proceed alone to degrade a person in the case of Minor Orders—something he cannot do without the intervention of others when it comes to Major Orders— nevertheless, it currently happens that priests and other clerics, whether of Major or Minor Orders, are freely sentenced to the galleys or even to life imprisonment, without verbal degradation—much less actual degradation—having been carried out first; as is well known to all, and as was also pointed out by Father Catalani in his Commentary on the Roman Pontifical (Title 16, § 4, n. 6, Volume III). 17. Even now, in the prisons of the Ecclesiastical Courts, one can find some prisoners who have been incarcerated because, lacking the priestly order, they celebrated Mass, heard the sacramental confession of the faithful, and furthermore, distributed unconsecrated host particles to the faithful who requested Eucharistic Communion, claiming they had consecrated them. Among these were two who, when asked if they knew the reason for their detention, naively confessed everything; hence the question arose as to whether it was appropriate to grant them a reduction in their sentence—that is, instead of handing them over to the secular courts, where they would face the death penalty, to sentence them to service on the galleys or to life imprisonment. 18. Among those who issued the sentence—whether as advisors or as judges—there was no disagreement that both offenders had incurred the penalty requiring their delivery to the secular court and, therefore, the death penalty. Some, however, pointed out that, in light of their prompt confession—in which each had fully admitted to his crime without waiting for further questions from the judge—both deserved to be considered confessed criminals and should therefore be sentenced to service on the triremes or to life imprisonment. But a larger number of others expressed the opposite view, namely, that they were subject to the death penalty and therefore had to be handed over to the secular authorities. 19. The persistence of our illness has prevented us from participating, as we have always made it our practice to do in such cases, in the Congregations where these cases were discussed; but since we did not neglect to read and examine all the documentation afterward, we judged it more in keeping with the clemency and gentleness proper to the Pontiff to adhere to the more lenient sentence; all the more so since Cardinal Albizio, who is highly versed in this matter, has also stated in writing that it is customary to grant this reduction in punishment as a reward to those who, at the judge’s first interrogation, confessed their crime fully and openly; and there are several examples of cases that have been commuted in this manner under similar circumstances. Therefore, it is our will that the aforementioned offenders be granted the reduction in their deserved punishment, that is, that they be sentenced to perpetual service on the triremes, so that they may never be released nor obtain a commutation of this punishment, unless ordered by the Roman Pontiff then in office, who is fully aware of their crimes and all the circumstances surrounding them; however, the judges retain the authority to substitute that punishment with life imprisonment or perpetual confinement if it appears that the sentence of perpetual service on the triremes is in no way suitable for such offenders, provided that the other conditions necessary for this purpose are met. Furthermore, we establish and decree that when the sentence of service on the triremes or life imprisonment is to be formally pronounced upon the aforementioned offenders, as stated above, this notification shall be made by the judge, in the presence of those charged with managing the sacristies of the churches, who shall therefore be summoned for this purpose by the judge himself, under penalty of sanctions to be imposed upon them at his discretion; and on that occasion, they shall be given a stern warning regarding the strict observance and enforcement of all relevant regulations: namely, that they never permit unknown priests to celebrate Mass until the letter of dismissal—which they have brought from their own diocese and presented—has been examined and evaluated by the person entrusted with this duty. We recall that this has occurred on other occasions in this very city, under our predecessor of blessed memory, Pope Clement XI, when a criminal sentenced to capital punishment had to be handed over to the secular courts because he had celebrated Mass without having been ordained to the priesthood. 20. We therefore decree that the competent judges and designated courts proceed in accordance with the rules described above against those who, as of today, are found to have celebrated Mass or heard Sacramental Confession without having been admitted to the Sacred Order of the Priesthood. But since we are convinced that this crime—which is extremely grave in itself and growing more frequent by the day—must be prevented by every means and, as far as possible, eliminated and eradicated from Christian nations, we decree that the new law on this matter, established by an Apostolic Constitution (a copy of which Your Fraternity will receive attached to this Letter), shall in the future—that is, three months after the date of said Constitution—be strictly observed and enforced. In the meantime, to you, Venerable Brother, we impart the Apostolic Blessing with great affection. Given at Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on August 2, 1757, in the seventeenth year of Our Pontificate.
