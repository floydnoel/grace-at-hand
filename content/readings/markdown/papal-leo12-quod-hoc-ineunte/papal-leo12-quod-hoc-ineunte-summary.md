## Summary

Issued by Pope Leo XII on May 24, 1824, proclaiming a universal Jubilee (Holy Year) for 1825. It announces a plenary indulgence for the faithful who, after repenting, confessing, and receiving communion, visit the four major basilicas of Rome (St. Peter's, St. Paul's, St. John Lateran, and St. Mary Major) for a prescribed number of days. The document is both a formal decree of the Jubilee and a pastoral invitation, calling the faithful to pilgrimage, repentance, and spiritual renewal.

## Historical Context

The bull was written in the aftermath of the Napoleonic Wars and the political upheavals that had shaken Europe in the early nineteenth century. Leo XII explicitly notes that the Jubilee had been "grievously omitted" at the beginning of the century "because of the evil condition of the times." The Church had suffered suppression, loss of temporal power, and disruption of its institutional life under Napoleon. Leo XII, elected in 1823, sought to reassert the Church's spiritual authority and unity. The 1825 Jubilee was the first since 1775 and marked a post-revolutionary attempt to re-center Catholic life on Rome, pilgrimage, and penitential practice.

## Key Themes

- **Renewal in Christ** — The central call is to "renew all things in Christ" through purification of the Christian people, echoing a theme that would recur in later Catholic social teaching.
- **Plenary Indulgence and the Treasury of Merit** — The document explains indulgences as drawing on the "heavenly treasure" of Christ's merits, the Virgin's, and the saints', a doctrine rooted in Trent.
- **Pilgrimage as Spiritual Remedy** — The physical journey to Rome is presented as a powerful means of conversion, contrasting the pursuit of earthly riches with the "salutary riches" of grace.
- **Contrast with the Old Law** — Leo XII draws typological parallels between the Jewish Jubilee (Leviticus 25) and the Christian Jubilee: restoration of goods, liberation from servitude, and remission of debts find their fulfillment in Christ.
- **Unity and Orthodoxy** — The bull calls for the extirpation of heresies, concord among Catholic princes, and the return of the separated to the Church, outside of which "there is no salvation."
- **Role of Bishops and Princes** — Bishops are charged with teaching sound doctrine on indulgences and combating false piety; civil rulers are asked to protect pilgrims and support the sacred cause.
- **Moral Reform of Rome** — The citizens of Rome are exhorted to exemplary behavior, modesty, and devotion, lest pilgrims be scandalized.

## Significance

*Quod Hoc Ineunte* revived the tradition of the universal Jubilee after a half-century hiatus and reasserted Rome's centrality in Catholic life following the disruptions of the revolutionary era. It restates Tridentine teaching on indulgences, purgatory, and the treasury of merit at a moment when these doctrines faced new skepticism. The document also foreshadows the nineteenth-century "ultramontane" movement — the turn toward Rome as the spiritual center of a Church seeking to consolidate authority after the shocks of secularization and revolution. The 1825 Jubilee itself was a major event, drawing large numbers of pilgrims and demonstrating the enduring vitality of popular Catholicism.
