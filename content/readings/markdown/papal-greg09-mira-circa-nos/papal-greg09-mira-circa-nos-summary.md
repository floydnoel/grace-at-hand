## Summary

*Mira Circa Nos* (1228) is the papal bull by which Pope Gregory IX formally canonized St. Francis of Assisi, enrolling him in the catalogue of saints and establishing October 4 as his feast day for universal celebration.

## Historical Context

Written just two years after Francis's death in 1226, the bull reflects a Church in transition — grappling with the rise of lay preaching movements and the need to channel popular piety into orthodox channels. Gregory IX, a close friend and former cardinal-protector of the Franciscan order, had known Francis personally; the document is both a legal decree and a personal tribute. It was issued from Perugia in the second year of his pontificate.

## Key Themes

- **Typological reading of Francis's life:** Gregory presents Francis as a new Samson, Abraham, Jacob, and Jephte, weaving Old Testament figures together to frame his holiness as continuous with salvation history.
- **Evangelical poverty as the path to holiness:** The bull emphasizes Francis's literal stripping of possessions, his embrace of material poverty, and his unadorned preaching as the core of his sanctity.
- **Divine confirmation through miracles:** Gregory argues that God confirmed Francis's holiness through many brilliant miracles, which justify the Church's public act of canonization.
- **The Church's authority to canonize:** The document carefully distinguishes between the hidden state of grace known to God and the public veneration permitted by the Church, which "can only observe outer appearances" and must rely on miracles as signs.
- **Francis as a model for the universal Church:** His feast is commanded for the whole Church, making him a patron and intercessor for all the faithful.

## Significance

*Mira Circa Nos* is one of the earliest formal canonization bulls and was instrumental in establishing the Franciscan movement's legitimacy within the institutional Church. By elevating Francis — an unlettered lay penitent who preached poverty — so soon after his death, Gregory IX signaled that the mendicant ideal was not a threat but a renewal of the Gospel. The bull's vivid biblical typology also set a rhetorical template for later canonization decrees.
