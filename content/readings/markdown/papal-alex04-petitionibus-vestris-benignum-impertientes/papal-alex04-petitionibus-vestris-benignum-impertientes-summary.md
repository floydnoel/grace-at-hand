## Summary

This brief bull, issued by Pope Alexander IV on July 15, 1255, at Anagni, grants the University of Salamanca the right to possess and use a common seal (*commune sigillum*). In medieval canon law, a corporate seal was the essential mark of legal personhood — it allowed a university to authenticate charters, diplomas, and correspondence as a single juridical body. The bull is short and formulaic, closing with the customary anathema against any who would dare infringe the grant.

## Historical Context

The University of Salamanca had been founded as a *studium generale* around 1218 by King Alfonso IX of León and was later confirmed by Ferdinand III of Castile. By the mid-thirteenth century, it was emerging as one of the leading centres of learning in the Iberian Peninsula. Pope Alexander IV (Rinaldo dei Conti di Segni, formerly cardinal-protector of the Franciscans) had succeeded Innocent IV in December 1254 and was still in the first year of his pontificate when this bull was issued from Anagni. The grant of a common seal was often one of the final steps in a university's acquisition of full corporate autonomy, signalling papal recognition of Salamanca's institutional maturity.

## Key Themes

- **Corporate identity and legal personhood** — the right to a common seal was the medieval equivalent of incorporation, transforming a loose body of masters and scholars into a unified legal entity capable of owning property, suing and being sued, and representing itself in canon law.
- **Papal patronage of learning** — the bull reflects the consistent thirteenth-century papal policy of fostering universities (Paris, Bologna, Oxford, Salamanca) as instruments for the formation of clergy and literate administrators.
- **Canonical authority and anathema** — the closing sanction ("indignationem omnipotentis Dei... se noverit incursurum") is a standard clause asserting the binding force of papal privileges under threat of spiritual penalty.
- **Academic freedom within ecclesiastical oversight** — the seal conferred autonomy, but the grant came from the Apostolic See, reminding the university that its corporate existence was ultimately a concession of papal authority.

## Significance

Though modest in length and formulaic in language, *Petitionibus Vestris Benignum Impertientes* is a foundational document for the University of Salamanca. It marks the point at which the pope formally recognised the university as a self-governing corporation under canon law. Salamanca would go on to become one of the most influential universities in the early modern world — its theologians shaping debates at the Council of Trent, its jurists developing the foundations of international law (Francisco de Vitoria), and its statutes serving as a model for universities across the Spanish Empire in the Americas. This bull is thus a small but necessary brick in the legal and institutional edifice that made that legacy possible.
