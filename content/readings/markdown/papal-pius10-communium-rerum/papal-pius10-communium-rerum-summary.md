## Summary

*Communium Rerum* is an encyclical issued by Pope Pius X on April 21, 1909, marking the eighth centenary of the death of St. Anselm of Aosta (c. 1033–1109), the Benedictine monk, Archbishop of Canterbury, and Doctor of the Church. The document presents St. Anselm as a model of sanctity and learning, a fearless defender of the Church's liberty against secular encroachment, and the pioneer of scholastic theology who established the proper relationship between faith and reason. Pius X uses the occasion to draw direct parallels between the crises Anselm faced (tyrannical princes, undisciplined clergy, heretical dialecticians) and the challenges confronting the Church in his own day, particularly the rise of Modernism.

## Historical Context

The encyclical was issued in the eighth year of Pius X's pontificate (1903–1914), a period marked by intense conflict between the Church and secularising forces across Europe. Just two years earlier, Pius X had published *Pascendi Dominici Gregis* (1907), his sweeping condemnation of Modernism — a movement he described as an attempt to "reconcile the extravagances of a fickle human science with divine faith," and which he saw as an internal corruption more dangerous than open persecution. The encyclical also alludes to recent natural disasters in Italy, anti-clerical legislation in France, and what Pius X perceived as a general apostasy of rulers and peoples who had "excluded God" from society. By invoking Anselm's struggles against Norman kings who usurped Church rights, Pius X implicitly condemns modern states that sought to subordinate the Church, while commending St. Anselm's unwavering loyalty to the Apostolic See as a pattern for contemporary bishops and clergy.

## Key Themes

- **St. Anselm as exemplar**: Pius X holds up Anselm's life as a model of the harmony between humility and magnanimity, learning and piety, gentleness and unconquerable firmity in defending the Church's rights.
- **Defence of Church liberty**: Anselm's refusal to compromise with royal power (especially over investiture) is presented as the proper stance of bishops toward secular authorities who "treat the Church as a servant" rather than as "an advocate and defender."
- **Faith and reason**: The encyclical celebrates Anselm's method of *fides quaerens intellectum* (faith seeking understanding) as the correct via media between rationalist presumption (which subjects dogma to human judgment) and fideistic timidity (which abandons rational defence of the faith). Anselm is hailed as the precursor of scholasticism and of Thomas Aquinas.
- **Condemnation of Modernism**: The document repeatedly links the "heretical dialecticians" of Anselm's day to contemporary Modernists, denouncing their "craving for novelty," their contempt for authority, and their claim that the Church must adapt its doctrine to modern thought.
- **Union with the Apostolic See**: Anselm's constant recourse to the Pope during his exiles and trials is held up as the "invincible bulwark of the Church" and the pattern for all bishops, especially in times of crisis.
- **Pastoral responsibility**: Bishops are urged to speak out boldly ("cry out, give yourself no rest"), to protect the young from doctrinal poison, and to resist the temptation to seek an "ephemeral peace" through compromise with error.

## Significance

*Communium Rerum* is one of the key documents of Pius X's anti-Modernist campaign, using the authority of a revered Church Father to reinforce the teachings of *Pascendi* and *Lamentabili*. It solidified St. Anselm's reputation not only as a speculative theologian but as a patron of ecclesiastical liberty and a model of episcopal courage. The encyclical also contributed to the revival of Neo-Scholasticism that Pius X and his predecessor Leo XIII promoted, anchoring Catholic intellectual life in the tradition of Anselm and Aquinas. Though less cited today than *Pascendi* or *Vehementer Nos*, it remains an important statement of the early twentieth-century papacy's self-understanding: the Church as a perfect society standing against both external secular oppression and internal doctrinal dissolution, with the Pope as its indispensable centre of unity.
