## Summary

*Mens Nostra* ("Our Mind") is a 1929 apostolic letter by Pope Pius XI issued at the close of his priestly golden jubilee. It strongly promotes the practice of the Spiritual Exercises — structured retreats of prayer, meditation, and self-examination — as a remedy for the spiritual ills of the modern world. The pope commends the Exercises for clergy, religious, laity, and members of Catholic Action, and formally endorses the Ignatian method as the preeminent form.

## Historical Context

The document was written in the aftermath of World War I and amid the rise of secularism, materialism, and rationalism, which Pius XI diagnoses as a "grave disease" of levity and thoughtlessness that entangles people in external things and draws them away from eternal truths. It caps a Jubilee year Pius declared to mark the 50th anniversary of his priestly ordination, and follows the example of Leo XIII and Pius X, who issued similar jubilee exhortations to the clergy.

## Key Themes

- The Spiritual Exercises are presented as the sovereign remedy for the modern spirit of distraction, materialism, and moral corruption
- The Exercises have a biblical basis — Christ's 40 days in the desert, the Apostles' retreat in the upper room before Pentecost — and a continuous tradition in the Church from the desert Fathers through St. Ignatius of Loyola
- St. Ignatius of Loyola is declared the "chief and peculiar Master" of the Exercises, and his method — already approved by Paul III and later popes — is given primacy
- The Exercises are for everyone: clergy (already bound by canon law), religious, and especially the laity, including workers and young people
- Pius XI recommends "closed" (private, silent) retreats as the ideal form, and also encourages monthly or quarterly "recollections" as a condensed continuation of the Exercises
- The Exercises are closely linked to Catholic Action — the lay apostolate — as a means of forming "pacific and strenuous soldiers of Christ"

## Significance

*Mens Nostra* is the most authoritative papal document ever devoted entirely to the Spiritual Exercises. It cemented the Ignatian method as the normative framework for retreats in Catholic life and gave powerful impetus to the retreat movement that flourished in the mid-20th century. By tying the Exercises to Catholic Action and to the broader renewal of Christian society, it made the practice central not just to personal holiness but to the Church's engagement with the modern world.
