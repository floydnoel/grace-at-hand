## Summary

*Exsurge Domine* ("Arise, O Lord") is a papal bull issued by Pope Leo X on June 15, 1520, formally condemning 41 propositions drawn from the writings of Martin Luther as heretical, scandalous, or offensive to pious ears. It orders Luther's books burned and gives him sixty days to recant or face excommunication.

## Historical Context

The document was written at the height of the early Reformation, three years after Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses in 1517. Luther's ideas had spread rapidly through Germany, aided by the printing press, and had gained significant popular and political support. The pope had previously attempted to resolve the dispute through a papal legate and correspondence, but Luther had refused to recant and instead appealed to a future general council. The bull reflects the papacy's mounting alarm at the threat to its authority and to the established sacramental and penitential system of the medieval Church.

## Key Themes

- **Papal Authority** — The bull asserts the pope's supreme jurisdiction over the universal Church as successor to Peter, and condemns Luther's denial that the pope is Christ's vicar over all churches.
- **Sacramental Theology** — It defends the traditional three-part structure of penance (contrition, confession, satisfaction), the real efficacy of the sacraments, and the value of indulgences, against Luther's challenges.
- **Church vs. Individual Interpretation** — It insists on the Church's exclusive right to interpret Scripture, condemning Luther's appeal to individual judgment over conciliar and papal teaching.
- **Heresy and Unity** — It frames Luther's errors as a return of condemned heresies (Hussite, Wyclifite) and portrays his movement as a threat to the peace and unity of Christendom.
- **Penalties and Obedience** — It combines a summons to repentance with the threat of major excommunication, book burning, and ultimately delivery to secular authorities.

## Significance

*Exsurge Domine* marks the formal rupture between Luther and the Roman Catholic Church. Though Luther publicly burned the bull in December 1520 and was excommunicated the following year in *Decet Romanum Pontificem*, the document is historically significant as the definitive papal response to the early Reformation. It represents the last major assertion of the medieval papal-jurisdictional order on the eve of Western Christianity's permanent division, and its condemnations crystallize the doctrinal fault lines — authority, Scripture, grace, sacrament, and Church — that would define the subsequent century of religious conflict.
