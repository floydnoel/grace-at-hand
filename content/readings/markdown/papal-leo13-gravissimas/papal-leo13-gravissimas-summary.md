## Summary

Gravissimas is a letter of support from Pope Leo XIII to the Portuguese bishops for their defense of religious orders against hostile civil legislation. The Pope praises the bishops for their courageous joint letter to the King, condemns the government's decrees as violations of Church rights and the faithful's freedom to choose a state of life, and urges the bishops to continue defending religious congregations and Catholic interests unitedly.

## Historical Context

Written in 1901 during the final years of Leo XIII's pontificate, Gravissimas responds to Portugal's anti-clerical turn. The Portuguese government had enacted decrees suppressing religious orders and imposing harsh conditions — including restrictions on novitiates and vows — that would make it nearly impossible for communities to survive. This was part of a broader wave of secularist and anticlerical legislation across Europe (especially in France and Portugal) in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, targeting the Church's institutional presence.

## Key Themes

- **Defense of religious orders** — Leo XIII insists that religious congregations have inherent rights that civil authorities may not suppress
- **Inviolability of the religious state** — No order can exist without novitiates and vows; these are essential to religious life and cannot be bargained away
- **Rights of the faithful** — The decrees infringe on Catholics' right to freely choose a consecrated state of life
- **Limits of civil accommodation** — Civil laws may require adjustments to external governance, but never at the cost of the integrity of religious profession
- **Unity of Catholics** — The Pope urges the bishops to set aside partisan and political rivalries, and to unite the laity through Catholic publications and organizations
- **Papal support for local bishops** — The Holy See entrusts practical solutions to the bishops' on-the-ground judgment, promising canonical dispensations if needed

## Significance

Gravissimas is an important document on Church–state relations and the rights of consecrated life. It asserts that the Church — not the state — has authority over the internal governance of religious institutes, a principle reaffirmed in later documents like *Christus Dominus* and the Code of Canon Law. While written for a specific Portuguese crisis, it articulates a broader Catholic argument against the secular state's overreach that remains relevant in contemporary debates about religious freedom and institutional autonomy.
