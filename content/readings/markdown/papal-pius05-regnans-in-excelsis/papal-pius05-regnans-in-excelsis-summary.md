# Regnans in Excelsis · English editorial summary

## Summary

This papal bull, issued by Pope Pius V in 1570, excommunicates Queen Elizabeth I of England, declaring her a heretic and a usurper of the supreme headship of the Church in England. It deprives her of her royal title and absolves all her subjects from any oath of allegiance or obedience to her.

## Historical Context

Written in the wake of the English Reformation, the bull responds to Elizabeth's re-establishment of Protestantism after the brief Catholic reign of Mary I. The Act of Supremacy (1559) had made Elizabeth the Supreme Governor of the Church of England, and the Act of Uniformity enforced Protestant worship. By 1570, Catholic uprisings in the north had been crushed, and Pius V — a reforming pope deeply engaged in the Counter-Reformation — judged that only a direct papal intervention could rally English Catholics and challenge the legitimacy of Elizabeth's rule.

## Key Themes

- **Papal Supremacy** — The pope asserts his God-given authority over all kings and kingdoms, with the power to depose rulers and release subjects from allegiance.
- **Unity of the Church** — Outside the one Catholic Church governed by Peter's successor, there is no salvation; Elizabeth's schism is thus a grave spiritual crime.
- **Heresy and Usurpation** — Elizabeth is condemned as a heretic, a favourer of heretics, and an illegitimate ruler for assuming ecclesiastical authority.
- **Just Deposition** — The pope, as supreme judge, wields the "weapons of justice" to remove a tyrannical and heretical monarch for the good of the faithful.
- **Absolution from Obedience** — Catholics are freed from all oaths, fealty, and legal obligations to Elizabeth, and are commanded not to obey her.

## Significance

*Regnans in Excelsis* had far-reaching and largely counterproductive effects. It hardened Elizabeth's government against English Catholics, who were now seen as potential traitors, leading to harsher penal laws. It also placed devout Catholics in an impossible position: obey their queen or obey the pope. The bull's theory of papal power to depose civil rulers became a flashpoint in early modern political thought, and its failure marked a definitive step in the decline of papal temporal authority over monarchs. It remains one of the most consequential — and controversial — papal documents of the Reformation era.
