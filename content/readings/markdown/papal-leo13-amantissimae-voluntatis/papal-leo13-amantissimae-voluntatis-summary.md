## Summary

*Amantissimae Voluntatis* (1895) is an apostolic letter from Pope Leo XIII addressed directly to the English people, expressing his deep affection for the English race and his ardent desire for the reunion of England with the Catholic Church through prayer, particularly through the intercession of the Blessed Virgin Mary and the saints.

## Historical Context

Written in 1895, the letter marks the approaching thirteenth centenary of the mission of St. Augustine of Canterbury (597), sent by Pope St. Gregory the Great to convert the Anglo-Saxons. Leo XIII was deeply concerned with the divisions wrought by the Protestant Reformation in the 16th century, which severed England from communion with Rome. The late 19th century saw a gradual Catholic revival in England — including the restoration of the Catholic hierarchy in 1850 and a wave of converts — and Leo sought to encourage this movement by calling for united prayer.

## Key Themes

- **Unity of faith as the supreme good** — Leo prays for the return of all Christians, especially the English, to the unity of the Catholic Church, which he identifies as Christ's will.
- **The power of prayer** — The letter insists that prayer, especially when offered with humility and persistence, is the primary means of obtaining grace for conversion and reunion.
- **The special role of England in salvation history** — Leo recalls St. Gregory the Great's mission, England's ancient devotion to the Holy See, and its traditional title "Dowry of Mary."
- **Marian intercession** — The letter includes a "Prayer for England" to the Blessed Virgin, appealing to her as England's Queen and Mother.
- **The necessity of Christian living** — Leo exhorts Catholics to exemplify uprightness, justice, charity, and moral purity so that their prayers may be effective.
- **The Rosary and popular piety** — He recommends the Holy Rosary and grants indulgences for reciting a special prayer for England's conversion.
- **Social concern** — Leo notes with approval English efforts on the social question, education, temperance, and care for the poor and vulnerable.

## Significance

The letter is notable as a direct, affectionate appeal to the English nation as a whole, not merely to English Catholics. It stands as one of Leo XIII's key ecumenical gestures before the modern ecumenical movement, emphasizing prayer — rather than polemic — as the path to reunion. The "Prayer for England" composed for the letter remains a cherished devotion among English Catholics. The document also reflects the late-19th-century "Second Spring" of Catholicism in England and Leo's broader strategy of engaging modern societies through charity, sound doctrine, and patient hope.
